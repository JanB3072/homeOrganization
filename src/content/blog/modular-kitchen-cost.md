Two quotes for the same L-shaped kitchen can differ by two lakh rupees, and both can be honest. That is the sentence that should stay in your head before you compare any modular kitchen price. The reason is not that someone is lying. It is that the phrase "modular kitchen" describes a way of building, not a fixed list of things included.

This piece takes a quote apart line by line. I am writing about the Indian market, where modular kitchens are commonly sold per square foot and per running foot, and where most of the published pricing lives. The figures below come from public 2026 guides and design sites, and I treat them as market ranges rather than as a price for your home.

## What modular actually means

Start here, because the word causes half the confusion. A modular kitchen is made from factory-produced cabinet units: base units, wall units and tall units, assembled on site in a few days. That is a construction method, the same way a flat-pack shelf is a manufacturing method. It is not a promise that a sink, a chimney or a set of drawers is included.

One design article puts it plainly. Modular describes prefabricated units put together at the site, and what you get depends on the quotation. Another adds the contrast that matters in India: unlike a traditional masonry platform kitchen built on site, a modular kitchen uses standardized modules combined in different configurations.

That distinction explains the quote gap. If two sellers start from different assumptions about what the room contains, the word modular gives them no shared definition to argue over.

## The number everyone quotes

Published 2026 ranges vary, and they vary for reasons that are worth understanding rather than smoothing over.

1. One interiors guide says most modular kitchens in India cost between roughly 1.5 lakh and 4 lakh rupees, with a basic version near 55,000 and premium work above 5 lakh. It also says the kitchen often takes 20 to 30 percent of a whole home interior budget.
2. A price guide gives three material tiers: economy with HDHMR board at roughly 1,200 to 1,800 rupees per square foot, mid-range plywood with PU finish around 2,000 to 3,000, and premium acrylic or glass at 3,000 to 5,000. It puts a typical ten by eight foot L-shaped kitchen at 1.5 to 4 lakh fully done.
3. A Pune-specific guide gives budget rates of 1,100 to 1,500 per square foot, mid at 1,500 to 2,200, and premium at 2,200 to 3,500, stating that these include carcass, shutters, standard hardware, countertop and installation. It separately gives running-foot figures of 12,000 to 18,000 for budget, 18,000 to 28,000 for mid, and 28,000 to 45,000 for premium.
4. A local guide for one neighbourhood quotes a far wider span, from 1.85 lakh to 12.5 lakh, based on layout, material, hardware and appliances. It prices by the square foot of cabinet shutter area, which is a different measure again.

Read those four together and one thing is obvious. There is no single national price. There is a set of tiers, and the number you are shown depends on which measure the seller uses and which items sit inside the quote.

## Why square feet and running feet disagree

This is the most useful technical point in the whole subject, and it is where a lot of bad comparisons happen.

Square-foot pricing usually refers to the floor area of the kitchen, or in some quotes the area of the shutters. Running-foot pricing refers to the length of the base cabinet run. One guide gives an eight by ten foot L-shaped kitchen as roughly 14 to 16 running feet, and a straight galley in a one-bedroom flat as eight to ten. Same floor area, very different amount of cabinet.

The countertop makes the gap larger. It is priced on the run, at roughly two feet deep. A U-shaped kitchen has three runs, so its countertop costs much more than a straight kitchen with the same floor area. A price that looks reasonable per square foot can become expensive once the runs are added up.

This is also why a cheap per-square-foot quote and an expensive all-in quote cannot be compared directly. One guide makes exactly that point: compare material-only with material-only, and all-in with all-in. A quote at 2,500 per square foot that includes everything may be cheaper than one at 1,800 that lists only the boxes.

## The quote, line by line

Here is where the money actually goes, and what each line tends to contain.

**Carcass.** This is the box structure of the cabinets. Design sites say it is almost always included, but the board grade changes the price significantly. One article contrasts an HDHMR carcass with a BWP carcass, and notes that the difference is one of the main reasons two quotes for the same layout diverge. If the quote does not name the board, ask. The same rule applies to a small shelf: the [deep cabinet guide](/blog/how-to-organize-deep-kitchen-cabinets/) exists because an unnamed board and an unmeasured space turned into a costly mistake.

**Shutters.** These are the doors and drawer fronts. Laminate, PU, acrylic and glass sit at different price tiers, and the finish is the most visible line on the quote. It is also the line people focus on too much, because a beautiful shutter on a weak box is a short-lived kitchen.

**Hardware.** Hinges, channels and handles are usually listed as included, and this is where the word becomes slippery. One design article points out that "hardware included" can mean basic hinges that sag after a few years, or branded soft-close systems from makers such as Hettich, Blum or Häfele. Unless the brand and model are written down, you do not know which one you are getting.

**Countertop.** Usually included, with the material named, such as granite or quartz. Confirm whether the rate covers the full run and the edges, and whether a sink cutout is extra. Getting an architect or a draw person to mark the runs on a plan is worth more than arguing about the per-square-foot rate, and the [baking sheet rack guide](/blog/kitchen-baking-sheet-organizer-rack-vertical-cabinet/) is a small example of how a measured space beats an assumed one.

**Installation.** Basic installation of the modules is commonly included. What is not always included is the work around them: electrical points, lighting, chimney connection, plumbing changes, or adjustments on site.

Those five lines make up the core quote. Everything below is where the budget quietly expands.

## What is usually left out

A design article published in September 2026 lists the items buyers most often assume are included and are not. Appliances are the biggest. Chimney, hob, oven, microwave and dishwasher are almost always quoted separately, even when the showroom display shows a complete kitchen. The display is a picture of a room, not a bill of materials.

The sink and faucet are frequently separate too. So are internal accessories: pull-out baskets, bottle pull-outs, cutlery organizers and corner units are typically upgrades rather than standard. This is the line that surprises people most, because the showroom draws you in with a corner mechanism and the quote arrives with a plain shelf.

Backsplash tiles behind the counter are usually extra, as are ceiling lights, strip lights under the cabinets and transport. One guide lists on-site adjustments among the additional charges. In a real apartment, walls are rarely square and floors are rarely level, so those adjustments are not a scam. They are the cost of fitting factory units into an old building.

The practical response is simple. Make a second column on the quote and write in every excluded item, then price those separately. A kitchen that looks like 2 lakh on paper can become 3 lakh before the first meal is cooked there, and that is normal if nobody did the second column.

## Three mistakes that inflate the final number

The first is comparing a material-only quote with an all-in quote. The per-square-foot number looks lower on the first one, so it wins on the spreadsheet and loses in reality. Match the scope before you match the price.

The second is choosing the finish before the storage. Acrylic shutters are visible; a well-placed set of drawers is felt every day. Internal accessories are usually upgrades, so adding them after installation costs more than specifying them at the start. If you know you need a tall unit for dry goods and a pull-out near the stove, put them in the first drawing. The [pantry hub](/kitchen/pantry/) and the [small pantry ideas](/blog/small-pantry-cabinet-organization-hacks-for-apartment/) both show how much difference the right storage makes, even in a much smaller room.

The third is assuming the per-square-foot rate describes the whole job. It describes one tier of materials. It does not tell you how many running feet you are buying, whether the appliances are in, or which hardware is fitted. A quote without those three answers is a starting point, not a price.
