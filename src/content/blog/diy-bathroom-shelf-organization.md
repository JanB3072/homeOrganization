I did this on a Saturday in February, with the bathroom window open and a towel spread on the floor. The shelf in question is a 30-inch-wide open board above the toilet, the kind that came with the flat-pack cabinet and was never quite level. It had reached the point where putting a mug down on it required moving three other things first.

I want to describe the order I did it in, because the order turned out to be the whole lesson. My first attempt, a couple of years earlier, started at a shop. I came home with six matching woven baskets, tipped everything into them, and felt organised for about a month. By month three two of the baskets had grey speckles along the bottom seam. That failure is worth more than any product recommendation here.

## Saturday morning: everything off, then a count

Everything came off the shelf and onto the towel. I wiped the board with a damp cloth and looked at the water ring where a bottle had been sitting. Then I counted, because I had been telling myself the problem was that the shelf was too small.

Forty-seven items. Nine were past their expiry date, including a sunscreen from two summers ago and an eye cream with a six-month-after-opening symbol that I had opened in March. Six were duplicates, mostly two of the same shampoo because I had bought a replacement before finishing the first. Four had never been opened at all. Three were things that belonged in the kitchen or the first-aid box and had migrated. What I actually used in a normal week came to about nineteen items.

Nineteen things fit comfortably on a 30-inch shelf. Forty-seven do not, and no basket arrangement changes that arithmetic. I threw away the expired ones, combined the two shampoos into one bottle, put the unopened extras in a box under the sink, and returned the migrants to wherever they came from. That took twenty minutes and cost nothing.

I did not buy any storage that weekend. That was deliberate. Buying containers before you know what survives is how you end up with six baskets holding eleven things you never use, which is what my first attempt had been.

## Why the woven baskets failed

A small bathroom with the door shut will go to 80 or close to 100 percent relative humidity during a hot shower, and it can stay above 70 percent for half an hour afterwards if the extractor fan is weak. Cellulosic materials, paperboard, cotton, jute, wicker, take up moisture from the air until they reach equilibrium with it. They do not dry out quickly, because the water is inside the fibre, not beaded on the surface. Mould only needs a sustained relative humidity above roughly 60 percent plus a little organic material, and wicker is organic material with plenty of crevices holding skin cells and soap residue. The grey speckles on my baskets appeared at the bottom seam, exactly where water sat.

The same mechanism rules out cardboard boxes, fabric-lined drawer organisers, and raw MDF with unsealed edges. MDF also swells at the edge and does not shrink back to its original dimension, so a board that has been damp once stays slightly fat and fuzzy. What survives is non-porous: injection-moulded PP or ABS, acrylic, glass, glazed ceramic, and powder-coated steel or aluminium. They wipe dry in seconds. They are also easier to see into, which turns out to matter more than how they look.

There is a second, less discussed reason to be fussy about an open shelf above a toilet. Flushing with the lid up produces an aerosol plume that can carry droplets a metre or two, and some of that settles on the surfaces above and around the bowl. Studies on this usually involve hospital wards and tracer organisms, so I would not claim a specific risk from a domestic shelf. But I would not store cotton pads, a toothbrush, or an open jar of face cream on an uncovered board directly above a toilet. Those moved into a lidded container or behind the cabinet door. The open shelf now holds towels, spare toilet paper, and a plant that tolerates the steam, with the [over-the-toilet rack](/blog/over-the-toilet-storage-ideas/) taking the things that used to sit directly above the bowl.

## Where things went back

With the surviving nineteen items on the towel, I stopped thinking in categories. Grouping all hair products together or all skincare together is how a shelf looks tidy in a photograph and stays annoying in use. What determines whether a thing gets put back is how often you reach for it, so I worked from the body outwards.

For an adult standing in front of a shelf, the comfortable reach band runs roughly from waist height to shoulder height, call it 30 to 55 inches from the floor, with the easiest spot at eye level around 58 to 65 inches on a wall-mounted shelf. The [wall hanger guide](/blog/small-space-vertical-storage-solutions-wall-hangers/) splits the same reach band into zones for hooks and rails. Below about 24 inches you are bending; above 70 you are stretching or fetching a step. My board sits at about 62 inches, so almost the whole surface is in the easy band, which is why it had become the dumping ground in the first place.

The daily things, face wash, deodorant, toothbrush cup, the one shampoo in use, went in a shallow open tray at the front edge of the shelf where a wet hand can find them without looking. Weekly items went behind that tray. Backups, an unopened toothpaste, spare soap, a bulk pack of cotton pads, went up into the cabinet above, in a single labelled box so I would not have to open four things to find them. The heavy stuff, a spare towel rolled up, a bottle of cleaner, went on the lower shelf near the wall.

One thing I did right by accident: I left a gap about the width of a mug on the middle shelf. It felt like wasted space for the first fortnight. Then I bought a new cleanser and it went straight into that gap instead of onto the counter. A shelf filled edge to edge has nowhere to absorb anything new, and the overflow is what starts the slow slide back into a pile.

Depth matters more than people expect. A container deeper than about 6 inches on a shelf of this height hides whatever is at the back, and things at the back of a bathroom shelf are forgotten until they expire. I use two shallow trays side by side rather than one deep bin, the same front-to-back logic as the [under-sink pull-outs](/blog/how-to-organize-under-bathroom-sink/). Transparent sides help, though a fully transparent container full of mismatched bottles creates visual noise that reads as clutter even when it is sorted. I ended up with one clear tray for small things and an opaque one for the less attractive packaging.

## The board itself, and when to leave it alone

While everything was off, I finally checked whether the shelf was actually straight. It was not, and it had a visible bow in the middle. The board is 5/8-inch melamine-faced particleboard spanning 34 inches between two side panels. A row of full 500 ml bottles puts roughly 4 to 5 kg on that span, and particleboard creeps under sustained load, which is why the sag had become permanent rather than springing back when I emptied it. No organiser fixes a shelf that is slowly folding.

The options are to shorten the span with a centre bracket into a stud, add a vertical divider that doubles as a post, or replace the board with 3/4-inch plywood or solid timber sealed on all faces. I used a divider, mostly because it also stopped the trays sliding into each other. Turning a bowed shelf over is the tempting shortcut; it just transfers the curve to the other direction and looks worse under a row of bottles.

If you are adding a new shelf rather than reorganising an existing one, the mounting is the part to get right. Find the studs, usually 16 inches on centre in older US framing, and drive screws into them for anything carrying real weight. Hollow drywall needs toggle bolts, which spread the load behind the board and can hold 20 to 30 lb each in 1/2-inch plasterboard; the little conical plastic plugs in a cheap kit are for a towel ring, not a shelf of glass bottles. The [wood wall organizer build](/blog/diy-bathroom-wall-organizer-wood/) uses the same studs-or-toggles rule on a smaller scale. On tile you need a diamond or carbide bit, a low speed, and masking tape to stop the bit wandering, then a nylon plug through the tile into the substrate. Construction adhesive or "no-nails" products need 24 to 72 hours to cure before loading, and they are miserable to remove later.

Check for pipes and cables before drilling, particularly on a wall shared with a shower mixer or above a socket. A cheap stud finder with AC detection is worth more than any basket. And in a rental, skip the drilling entirely: a tension pole shelf in the corner or a freestanding narrow unit does the same job and leaves no holes to patch.

## A week later, and a year later

The first version of my plan included a weekly wipe-down with a labelled cleaning schedule. That lasted about eleven days. What actually stuck was hitching it to something already happening: on Saturday morning, while the shower is still warm, I put back anything that wandered onto the counter and run the same cloth over the shelf. Maybe three minutes. The trays lift out, so I rinse them in the sink about once a month and let them dry before they go back, which is the step that prevents the faint film that used to build up under the bottles.

A year on, the shelf has about twenty-two things on it. Three have changed, one tray has been replaced after I dropped it, and the wicker has not come back. The count is the useful part. When I notice the shelf looking crowded, I do not shop for a bigger container; I empty it and check the expiry dates again, because the number has crept up from nineteen to twenty-something and I know exactly why.

If you only do one thing from all of this, take everything off the shelf before you buy anything. The [shower caddy](/blog/hanging-shower-caddy-storage-hacks/) is the wet-zone version of the same rule, and the rest of the [bathroom storage guides](/bathroom/storage/) apply it to cabinets. The odds are good that you will find nine expired items and two identical shampoos, and that the shelf was the right size all along.
