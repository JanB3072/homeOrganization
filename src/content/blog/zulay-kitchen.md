The name sounds like an invented brand word until you read the About Us page. Zulay was a person. The company is named after the mother of its co-founder, and the story she is part of explains more about the products than a feature list would.

This is a piece about what Zulay Kitchen actually is and what it sells, built from the company's own pages and from what those pages leave out. I am not reviewing individual products or repeating affiliate rankings. I am describing the shop, then asking the question that matters in a small kitchen: does any of this earn a place in the drawer?

## The name belongs to a person

The About Us page opens with a sentence that settles the question. The company is named after Zulay, described there as a dedicated mother who inspired the mission of bringing practical, durable and stylish kitchen gadgets into every home. Further down, the story is signed by Aaron Cordovez, co-founder, who writes that Zulay Kitchen is named after his extraordinary mother.

He says she was raised in poverty in Venezuela and, against the odds, brought her children to live in the United States. As a single mother she worked long hours and still found time to cook healthy meals. He writes that he did not understand the scale of that effort until he had children of his own.

That origin is not decoration. It tells you what kind of company this is and what it is trying to sell. The tools are framed around family meals, busy weeks and cooking with children, not around restaurant technique. The stated belief is that cooking should be simple, fun and fast so there is more time for the people at the table. If you came looking for a serious brand with an engineering story, this is a different thing.

One detail the page does not give is a founding year. It does not name the co-founder's wife, though Aaron writes that the two of them created the business together. I mention those gaps because it is easy to fill them with guesses, and guessing is how a short brand article turns into fiction.

## What kind of company it is

The company describes itself as family-owned. It sells through a Shopify storefront, with product pages, recipe posts and a section called Kitchen Tips. The price positioning is clear from the catalogue: inexpensive, everyday tools rather than premium equipment. One of its own blog posts lists a mini milk frother at under six dollars, powered by two AA batteries and made with a stainless steel whisk. That figure may have changed since the post was written, but it gives a fair picture of the range.

A third-party review describes the same positioning, noting that most products sit at an affordable entry level while some higher-end items reach around ninety dollars. That is an outside observation rather than a company claim, and I would treat the live store as the only reliable price source.

The shipping statement is worth knowing. The site's own FAQ says it currently ships only within the United States. Overseas delivery windows appear elsewhere on the same page, which is a little confusing, but the direct answer restricts orders to the US. If you live elsewhere, check before you spend time on the catalogue.

## A product map, grouped by the job

The catalogue makes more sense when sorted by what you are trying to do, rather than by the order the site displays. These groups come from the store's navigation, homepage text and product listings.

**Coffee and drinks.** Milk frothers are the flagship category. The site sells handheld battery models and rechargeable versions, and a separate coffee appliances section covers related tools. Cold brew makers appear too, usually as a jar with a lid and a spout. This is where the brand is most recognisable, and many people first encounter Zulay through a frother.

**Juicing and citrus.** Manual juicers and lemon squeezers are a visible group. The storefront shows a citrus juicer with a handle, and the homepage names lemon squeezers among its core products. These are simple hand tools, and they suit the brand's low-price, high-use idea better than almost anything else it sells.

**Everyday prep.** General kitchen utensils form a broad category: spoons, spatulas, whisks, tongs and the teak wood utensil sets that appear on the homepage. Heavy-duty kitchen scissors are listed among the gadgets. These are the items most likely to be bought as a set, and also the most likely to duplicate what is already in a drawer.

**Baking.** Cake decorating supplies, including pastry bags and piping tips, have their own product pages. This fits the family story, since baking with children is one of the scenes the brand uses to describe itself.

**Small gadgets and home.** The wider catalogue includes items such as grill thermometers and plates, which sit under home and dining rather than pure prep. The store also publishes recipes, so the site is part shop and part content library.

A useful caution: category sizes vary. One third-party review notes that some product groups contain only four or five items. That is not a criticism, but it means the brand is a set of focused ranges rather than a full kitchen supplier. You will not find every tool there.

## What the low prices do and do not tell you

A six-dollar frother is a tempting object. It is also a useful reminder that price and frequency are different questions. A cheap tool used every morning is a good purchase. A cheap tool used twice and then pushed to the back of a drawer is the most expensive thing in the kitchen, because it cost money and space.

The brand's own language leans on durability and thoughtful design. I cannot verify those claims from the pages alone, and I would not repeat them as facts. What can be said is that the catalogue is positioned as affordable and practical, and that the company ties its products to ordinary family cooking. Whether a specific item lasts depends on the item, and reviews of individual products should be read with the usual care about who wrote them and why.

There is a second issue with inexpensive tools in a small room. They are easy to acquire and hard to remove. A set of twelve utensils costs little, but it occupies the same drawer as the six you already own. The [twenty-minute rule](/blog/20-20-decluttering-rule-explained/) is a reasonable filter here: if a new tool does not replace or clearly outperform something in the drawer, it does not come in.

## Who should shop here, and who should not

This brand makes sense for someone setting up a first kitchen on a tight budget, for a household that wants a few simple tools without a premium price, and for anyone who needs a specific small item such as a handheld frother or a lemon squeezer and does not want to hunt through a large retailer.

It makes less sense for a serious cook who already owns good tools, for someone outside the United States given the current shipping limit, and for anyone trying to buy a complete kitchen from one brand. The catalogue is a collection of useful small things, not a full system. ## The drawer test before you buy

Here is the habit I use with any small gadget, regardless of brand. Before ordering, name the exact place it will live. If you cannot name a slot, shelf or bin, you are not buying a tool. You are buying a future clearing-up session.

Then ask what it replaces. A milk frother might replace a whisk and a jar, or it might sit beside them, which is the same mistake I made with a gadget that ended up in the [deep cabinet](/blog/how-to-organize-deep-kitchen-cabinets/) instead of on the counter. A citrus juicer might replace the habit of squeezing lemons by hand, which is a real gain if you make dressings constantly. A utensil set rarely replaces anything, because you already own utensils. This is why the sets look so good in a basket and so annoying six months later. The [cabinet zone guide](/kitchen/cabinets/) is a better way to work out what the drawer actually lacks before a shopping trip.

Size matters in a way that product photographs hide. A frother with a stand needs counter space. A cold brew jar needs a shelf at the right height. A pastry bag set needs somewhere that will not crush the tips. The [pull-out shelf guide](/blog/diy-pull-out-shelves-for-kitchen-cabinets/) is useful if the only home is a deep lower cupboard, and the [clear container guide](/blog/best-clear-plastic-bins-for-fridge-storage/) makes the same argument about visibility in the fridge.

If you are buying for someone else, the drawer test becomes harder. A gift can be wonderful and still be a burden. The [decluttering guide for overwhelmed days](/blog/how-to-start-decluttering-when-overwhelmed/) starts from the idea that every object asks for attention. A small kitchen asks for more attention than most.

The About Us page gives a clear family story, the store has real product categories, and the brand has been around long enough for the story to be repeated in other places. It does not tell you whether the one item you want is worth the space.

So the practical answer to "what does it sell" is a short list: frothers and coffee tools, citrus juicers, utensils, small baking supplies and a handful of gadgets. The practical answer to "should I buy" is a question about your own kitchen. Name the slot first. Then order.
