A bin is the last thing anyone plans in a kitchen and the first thing that annoys everyone who cooks. In a small room it takes up floor, or it takes up cabinet space, and either way it competes with something more pleasant. The size question sounds simple, then turns out to depend on pipes, door swings, how often you take the bag downstairs, and whether you recycle at all.

I measured my own cupboard before I bought anything, which is not as sensible as it sounds. I did it because the first bin I owned was too tall for the space and the lid scraped the countertop every time I opened it. That was a small mistake repeated three times a day. In this piece I start with the measurement, because capacity numbers are meaningless until you know what the room can accept.

## Measure before you look at litres

Begin with the cabinet, not the product page. Open the door fully and measure the internal width, the depth from front to back, and the height. If the bin will live under the sink, measure from the floor to the underside of the sink basin, not to the countertop. One retail guide makes this exact point and it is the detail people miss most often. The trap is a basin that leaves ten centimetres less clearance than the cabinet appears to offer.

Then note the plumbing. The drain pipe and the hot and cold lines occupy the back of the cupboard, and a straight bin will hit them. Under-sink bins are shaped or offset to work around this, which is why a normal pull-out cannot simply be dropped in. A guide on under-sink sizing gives a 300 to 400mm cabinet width and a depth range of roughly 450 to 550mm as typical for a single unit. Those are useful ballpark figures, not a substitute for a tape measure in your own kitchen.

If there is no spare cabinet, measure the floor instead. A free-standing bin needs a clear path from the counter to the bin, then from the bin to the door. In a galley kitchen that path often runs through the cooking zone, which is why a bin near the prep area is worth more than a larger one tucked in a corner. The [cabinet zone map](/kitchen/cabinets/) applies the same thinking to the rest of the room.

## Work out how much you actually throw away

Capacity advice that starts with household size is usually too crude. Two people who cook every night produce far more scraps than two people who eat out most days. A single person who meal-preps on Sunday can fill a bin faster than a family that orders in.

A better question is how often you are willing to empty it. If you take the bag down every two or three days, a 10 to 20 litre bin is usually plenty. If the bin has to last a week because the outside collection is infrequent, you need more volume or a second container. This is a habit question disguised as a product question.

There is one published figure that helps. A guide on compost sizing suggests most couples produce four to eight litres of kitchen scraps a week. That is scraps alone, not packaging or general waste, but it gives a scale. If you separate food waste, your main bin can be smaller than you think, and the food-waste container becomes the one you empty most often.

Track your current bin for a week before buying. Note the day it fills and what forced it: peels, packaging, a single bulky box. Bulky items are the usual culprit in small kitchens. A cereal box does not weigh much and takes a third of the bin, which is why a slim bin feels full long before it is heavy. That is also a reason to flatten packaging before it goes in, a habit that costs nothing and delays the next bag by a day or two. Giving each category one container, as the [small pantry hacks](/blog/small-pantry-cabinet-organization-hacks-for-apartment/) do for dry goods, works just as well on the waste side of the room.

## Match the cabinet width to the bin type

The appropriate size is largely decided by the cabinet. Product listings for pull-out systems give a consistent pattern, and it is worth understanding before you shop.

1. A **300mm cabinet** is the common entry point for a slim pull-out. Listings show single bins in the 10 to 30 litre range, with some twin 10L plus 20L units built for a 300mm base in side-mounted form. This is the usual choice for a small kitchen with one narrow spare cupboard.
2. A **400mm cabinet** supports more capacity. One supplier lists 15 and 16 litre bins at this width, and another gives 400mm as a typical under-sink width. If you have a 400mm opening, you have more room than you need for a couple.
3. A **450mm cabinet** opens up separation. A 10 litre plus 20 litre twin unit is a common fit, giving 30 litres in total with two compartments. This is the size I would choose if I recycled seriously and had the cupboard.
4. A **600mm cabinet** is a luxury in a small kitchen. Listings at this width run from 27 litres up to 45 litres, sometimes as twin or triple units. If you have a 600mm cupboard to spare, your kitchen is not as small as you think.

Free-standing bins sit outside this system. A 10 to 20 litre pedal bin with a narrow footprint can work beside a counter, provided it does not block a door or the path to the sink. In a very small room, a bin with a lid that lifts rather than swings outward saves a few centimetres of clearance and a lot of irritation.

## Pull-out, under-sink or free-standing?

Each type solves a different version of the problem.

A pull-out bin lives in a cabinet and slides toward you on runners. It keeps the bin out of sight and out of the walking path, which is the main reason people choose it. The trade-off is that the mechanism uses some of the cabinet width, so a nominal 300mm unit may hold less than you expect. Soft-close runners are worth having on a bin you open twenty times a day.

An under-sink bin is a pull-out shaped to clear the plumbing. It reclaims a cupboard that is otherwise awkward to use, and it puts the bin exactly where you rinse a vegetable or wash a pan. The constraint is the pipework, and the measurement to the underside of the basin is the one that decides whether it fits. The [under-sink plumbing guide](/blog/under-sink-storage-plumbing-friendly/) deals with the same obstacle from the storage side, and the logic transfers directly.

A free-standing bin is the cheapest and easiest option, and in a rental it may be the only realistic one. Its weakness is visibility. A bin on the floor takes up space you can see, and in a small kitchen that visual cost is real. A narrow model in a colour that recedes helps a little. Putting it in the gap beside a cabinet helps more. The [pull-out shelf guide](/blog/diy-pull-out-shelves-for-kitchen-cabinets/) is worth reading if the only spare space is a deep lower cupboard, because a tray that comes toward you beats a bin you have to lean into.

## Three failures I see repeatedly

The first is buying by capacity alone. Someone reads that 30 litres is popular, orders it, then finds the cabinet is 280mm wide. The listing said 300mm in small print, and the difference matters. Measure first, shop second. This is the same error as buying a shelf from a photograph and discovering the wall is two inches shorter, a mistake I described in the [deep cabinet guide](/blog/how-to-organize-deep-kitchen-cabinets/).

The second is a twin bin that nobody separates. Two compartments sound organised until you find yourself putting a banana peel in the general waste because the food bin is full and the other one is empty. If your local collection does not separate materials, or if you live alone and do not generate much recycling, a single bin with a bag you change often is simpler. The [decluttering guide for overwhelmed days](/blog/how-to-start-decluttering-when-overwhelmed/) makes a similar argument about systems that ask for more discipline than a household has.

The third is ignoring the lid swing. A pedal bin with a lid that arcs outward needs clear space in front. In a galley with a narrow aisle, that arc can block the only path past the stove. A lift lid, a swing top that moves within the bin footprint, or a pull-out with no projecting lid avoids the problem. Check the opening mechanism as carefully as the capacity.

## Where the bin fits in the small-kitchen plan

A bin is not a storage decision in the decorative sense. It is a workflow decision. It should be close to where food is prepared, because that is where most scraps are created, and close enough to the door that taking it out is not a production. In a 580-square-foot apartment, those two points are often the same three feet of counter, which is convenient.

The bin also affects how the rest of the cupboard works. A pull-out under the sink takes space that might otherwise hold cleaning supplies, so those supplies need a new home. The [pantry hub](/kitchen/pantry/) handles the dry-goods side of that rearrangement, and the [clear bin comparison](/blog/best-clear-plastic-bins-for-fridge-storage/) uses the same idea of assigning one container to one job. If you are adding recycling, decide where the flattened cardboard goes before you buy the second compartment. A bin without an exit plan for its contents is just a tidy pile.

Liners are a quiet cost. Odd-sized bins sometimes need specific bags, and a special liner is an ongoing expense that never appears in the purchase price. Before ordering, check what the maker recommends and whether those bags are easy to find locally. A standard bin that takes ordinary kitchen bags is dull and extremely convenient. The same is true of the [label system](/blog/how-to-label-pantry-jars-custom-vinyl-stickers/) in a shared kitchen, where a two-second read stops someone putting the wrong thing in the wrong container.

## The answer, if you want one number

If you have a 300mm cupboard, choose a single pull-out around 10 to 20 litres. If you have 450mm and recycle, choose a 10L plus 20L twin. If you have no cupboard, choose a 10 to 20 litre free-standing bin with a lid that does not swing into the walking path. Then empty it every two or three days and stop worrying about whether it is big enough.

A smaller bin emptied often is more pleasant than a large bin that stays half full and smells by the weekend. That is the real trade in a small kitchen. You are not choosing how much rubbish the bin can hold. You are choosing how often you have to deal with it, and how much of the room you give up while you wait.
