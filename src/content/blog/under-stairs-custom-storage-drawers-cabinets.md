A carpenter friend stopped me before I had finished drawing the first box. I had sketched a neat row of four drawers following the stair line, and he pointed at the diagonal member carrying the treads and asked what I intended to screw them to. I had assumed it was just a board. On this staircase it was a cut stringer, with a triangular notch sawn out of it above every tread, and the remaining strip of wood at each notch was doing real work. Cutting another notch for a drawer runner would have been a genuinely bad idea.

That is the first thing to know about this project. Most of the guidance about under-stairs storage is about layout. The part that can cost you money or a staircase is the structure behind the plasterboard.

## What is behind the wall

Residential stairs in the US are usually built to something close to the IRC dimensions: maximum riser around 7.75 inches, minimum tread depth 10 inches, minimum headroom 6 feet 8 inches measured vertically from the sloped line of the tread nosings, and a minimum stair width of 36 inches. Those numbers matter here because they define the triangle you are trying to use. A typical 8-foot floor-to-floor rise at 7.5-inch risers means about 13 risers and roughly 10 feet of horizontal run.

The treads sit on stringers, the diagonal members on one or both sides. A housed stringer has grooves routed into a solid board; a cut stringer has the tread and riser profile notched out of it. The code limits how little wood may remain below those notches, commonly around 3.5 inches of uncut material, precisely because a heavily notched stringer behaves like a weaker beam. There is no spare section to cut into for a drawer.

None of this prevents storage. It changes where the load goes. The practical approach is a freestanding carcass, a box of its own that sits on the floor and is fixed to the wall framing at the back, with the drawer runners screwed into that box rather than into the stair. The staircase stays untouched, and if you ever move out, the whole unit comes away. Where the underside is already boxed in with drywall, find the studs with a detector before driving anything long, and remember that supply pipes and cables are often run along the wall under a stair. The [wall hanger guide](/blog/small-space-vertical-storage-solutions-wall-hangers/) goes into anchor types in more detail than this build needs.

The other structural question is the stair as an escape route. Anything you build must not narrow the clear width of the stair or the landing, and a door that swings out into the hallway at the bottom of a flight is a bad idea in a house where that hallway is the way out. Drawers that pull into the hallway are fine as long as you do not leave them open; a full-height hinged door at the tall end needs a moment of thought about which way it swings.

## The volume you actually get

It is worth doing the arithmetic before you get excited. Take that 8-foot rise over a 10-foot run at 36 inches of stair width. The cross-section is a right triangle with an area of 8 times 10 divided by 2, so 40 square feet. Multiply by the 3-foot width and you have 120 cubic feet of air under the sloped line, but almost all of that is above head height where the stair itself sits, not below it. The wedge you can actually reach from the floor, between the underside of the stringer and the ground, is more like a third of it. In practice, with a 1/2-inch lining, a floor plate, a bit of clearance above each drawer, and the space the drawer slides need, people end up with 22 to 28 cubic feet of usable interior across four or five compartments.

That is still a lot. A two-door wardrobe is around 25 to 35 cubic feet. The difference is that a wardrobe is a rectangle and this is a triangle, so almost none of it can be a standard box.

The measurement that catches people out is the second axis, and it is the same measure-the-object-first mistake that sends people home with a [storage ottoman](/blog/best-storage-ottomans-for-small-living-spaces/) too small for the blanket. On a straight flight the headroom changes along the run but is constant across the width. Add a quarter-turn winder or a landing and the underside slopes across the width too. Measure headroom at the front edge and the back edge of each proposed compartment, at both ends. A drawer built to the front-edge figure will scrape the soffit at the back, and because it is a drawer on full-extension runners you will not notice until it is loaded and halfway out.

## Assigning the compartments

Rather than draw one big cabinet, I found it easier to walk along the run and mark the points where the headroom crossed a useful threshold, then let those points define the compartments.

At the bottom, where there is 60 inches or more, you have a small closet. This is the only part of the wedge that can take a hanging rail, a broom, or a vacuum. It is also the part most visible from the entry, so it is worth a door. Somewhere around 36 inches of headroom, drawers become the efficient choice: a box 20 to 24 inches deep on 100 lb full-extension slides, with a shoe shelf angled about 15 degrees and a 4-inch lip at the front so trainers do not pile up against the slide. Below about 24 inches, pulling a drawer out means crouching to see into it, and a pull-out tray with low sides or a vertical divider for boots and umbrellas works better. The last 12 inches under the top treads is a flat seasonal bin zone that you reach twice a year, so do not waste good hardware on it.

One decision that sounds fussy and pays off: give every compartment one category, the way a [vertical closet system](/blog/vertical-closet-system-maximize-small-wardrobe/) gives every zone one job. One drawer for shoes, one for dog leads and bags, one for hats and gloves. Mixed drawers under a stair get dug through in the dark, and the contents end up on the hallway floor.

## Hardware details that decide whether it survives

Full-extension slides are not optional here, for the same reason they rescue [deep kitchen cabinets](/blog/how-to-organize-deep-kitchen-cabinets/): a drawer that only comes out halfway hides its own back half. A half-extension slide leaves the back third of a 24-inch drawer inside a cavity with no light, and that third becomes where things go to disappear. Side-mount ball-bearing slides rated 100 lb are the straightforward choice; undermount slides with soft-close look cleaner and cost two or three times as much, and they need a very square box to run true. Allow the manufacturer's side clearance, usually about half an inch per side, when you size the carcass. I have seen a beautifully built set of boxes that bound because the maker sized them to the opening rather than to the slide specification.

Drawer fronts are where the "custom" in custom storage lives. A flat rectangular front on each box leaves a staircase of visible steps, which reads as a stack of filing cabinets. Fronts cut to the diagonal, meeting on a continuous sloping line, read as part of the architecture. Cutting them accurately needs a template taken from the actual wall, because the underside of a 1970s staircase is rarely as straight as the drawing. Use a strip of thin plywood or a long straightedge and a pencil, scribe it, and cut a little outside the line. MDF takes paint well and stays flat on a long diagonal; solid timber moves with the seasons, and a long mitred front can open a 3 mm gap by February. Leave that gap anyway. Anything fitted tight will bind in humid weather.

Ventilation is easy to forget because the compartments are closed. A stairwell near a front door sees cold slab, wet boots, and warm air rising from the rest of the house. I left a 1-inch gap at the bottom of the tall-end door and used a lifted floor liner with an air channel behind it. A wipeable vinyl or polypropylene liner in each compartment is worth the twenty minutes it takes to cut: dust works down between the treads and settles in a fine grey film on everything, and being able to pull the liner out and shake it beats vacuuming inside a drawer.

If you want a light, and you do, use low-voltage LED strip with a door jamb switch or a small battery puck. A mains-voltage fitting in a closed, dusty cavity needs proper enclosure and an accessible junction, and the driver will eventually fail, so put it somewhere you can reach without dismantling a drawer. I fitted the strip to the underside of the soffit with an aluminium channel, which also acts as a heat sink and stops the tape sagging off in a few years.

## What it costs and where DIY stops

Prefabricated drawer boxes, four pairs of slides, plywood for a carcass and fronts, and paint put a basic three-drawer conversion in the region of $300 to $800 in materials. It is two or three weekends of work, most of it scribing and fitting. Hiring a cabinetmaker to template the diagonal, build sprayed fronts, and fit soft-close hardware on everything starts around $2,000 and goes up quickly with the number of compartments and the finish.

The split I would recommend: build the carcass and buy standard drawer boxes yourself, then pay for the fronts if the staircase is visible from the front door. If the stair is tucked in a hallway, painted plywood fronts cut square look perfectly intentional and cost a fraction of a mitred set. Custom fronts are a visible-surface expense, not a functional one.

There is also a version of this project that is not worth doing. If the stair is open-tread with no soffit, there is no cavity to enclose without building a wall, and the result usually looks clumsier than a freestanding cabinet. If the underside carries a boiler, a meter, or the main stopcock, you need access panels and clearance, and the storage has to work around them. And if you rent, a built-in carcass is a gift to the landlord; a freestanding narrow shoe cabinet that slides into the tall end does most of the job and leaves with you.

We ended up with four drawers, one pull-out with vertical dividers, and a tall coat compartment with a light. The tape measure came out again three times during the build because the soffit was not straight. The shoes are now in the second drawer, the dog leads in the third, and the hallway floor is clear for the first time since we moved in.
