The wall that started this was 32 inches wide between the door casing and the corner, in a powder room that needed to hold everything a full bathroom holds, minus the room. Since then I have owned three small vanities with built-in storage, live with a fourth, and can report that the useful information about them is exactly the information that never appears in the listing: what the drawer actually does, where the pipe goes, and what the board is made of underneath the paint. Three vanities later, here is each lesson in the order it was learned.

## Vanity one: the drawer that was not a drawer

The first one was beautiful, 24 inches wide, shaker doors either side of what the photo clearly showed as a stacked pair of drawers. In person, the "drawers" turned out to be one real drawer up top and, below it, a false front on door hinges, behind which sat a single shelf and the trap. Half the visible storage was scenic.

That vanity taught me to read the spec line for the phrase that matters, functional drawers, and underneath that, the slide type. Centre-mounted wooden runners, the cheapest kind, sag in bathroom humidity and stop the drawer two-thirds out, so the back four inches of the box becomes a museum of things you know are there. Full-extension ball-bearing slides cost the maker a few dollars more and hand you the whole box. The [under-sink zone map](/blog/how-to-organize-under-bathroom-sink/) covers what to do with the cabinet style; for drawers, the test is one motion: pull one out all the way in the shop, and watch whether it stops where the photo promised.

## Vanity two: the pipe problem

The second one had genuine full-extension drawers and died a quieter death: the top drawer, advertised at 5 inches deep, could open only about two-thirds before it met the water trap behind the facade. No U-shaped cutout, so the drawer front closed and the box behind it was a stub.

Plumbing eats a predictable bite. The P-trap and the tailpiece occupy roughly a 6 by 6 inch block at the centre back of the cabinet, and every well-designed small vanity routes its drawers around it with a cutout shaped like a shallow U. Poorly designed ones just shorten the drawer and hope nobody measures. The arithmetic that matters at purchase: the usable drawer depth is the box depth minus the cutout, so a "6-inch" drawer with a 3-inch notch holds a 3-inch layer of anything, which is a washcloth, not a hair dryer. The [makeup drawer audit](/blog/bathroom-drawer-dividers-for-makeup/) works out what daily items actually need; most of them want more than 3 inches.

Depth failed next. The 18-inch-deep cabinet looked generous in the photo and held, in practice, a soap pump, two folded towels and nothing that plugs in. A hair dryer needs about 9 inches of clear box plus cord, a curling wand with the cord wrapped wants 13, and a straightener in its heat pouch wants to lie flat somewhere it can cool. Depth, not width, is the dimension that decides whether built-in storage works, and listings always lead with width because it is the bigger number. Eighteen inches deep is a soap cabinet. Twenty-one is the standard that fits a small bathroom. Twenty-two and more, on a wall-hung box, starts to crowd a 30-inch-wide walkway, which is its own trade.

## Vanity three: the edge that drank

The third survived two years and lost to a slow leak at the basin rim that wicked into the counter's particleboard core. By the time it showed, the edge banding had lifted, the core had swollen half an inch, and the drawer below no longer closed flush. The vanity was not badly made; it was badly made for a bathroom, which is a different sentence.

Materials decide whether built-in storage is a ten-year purchase or a three-year one. A plywood carcass with sealed edges shrugs off the occasional wet cloth; particleboard with thin paper edge banding drinks at the first seam that lifts, and bathroom humidity finds every seam. Sealed MDF doors are fine, and solid-wood frames are fine, but the box is what sits over the trap where damp air lives. On the counter, an integrated ceramic or cultured-marstone top with a moulded basin has no rim seal to fail; a separate vessel basin adds 4 to 6 inches of height, which matters on a comfort-height box, and adds a silicone joint, which is a future maintenance date.

## The five moves in the showroom

Every lesson above compresses into five physical actions, and none of them requires asking a salesperson anything.

1. Pull the top drawer to full extension and look back: does it stop short of the box, and is there a U-cutout clearing the trap?
2. Open the door and touch the bottom panel and the edge banding. Sealed plywood feels smooth and continuous; paper banding has a lip you can catch with a thumbnail.
3. Sit on your heels and reach the back of the lower cabinet. If your arm cannot, neither will a vacuum hose, and that shelf will never be cleaned.
4. Measure the counter rim height and add any basin. Compare it to the elbows of the shortest person in the house.
5. Look at the hinge side of the door against the wall it will face. A door that opens toward a side wall loses half its arc, and with it the shelf behind the hinge.

The fourth vanity, the one still in service, passed all five and cost less than the second one, which is the part that keeps me recommending the checklist over the brand name.

## The measurements that decide before style does

Style is the last filter, and the room applies the first ones. Widths of 18, 24, 30 and 36 inches cover most of the market; the wall decides, minus 2 inches of breathing room each side so the top's overhang and your hips both fit. Depth of 18 to 21 inches keeps a walkway open; anything deeper needs 30 inches of clear floor in front, the same clearance logic that governs [counter surfaces](/blog/bathroom-counter-clutter-solutions/). Height runs 30 to 32 standard, 36 comfort; with a vessel basin on top, subtract accordingly or the finished rim ends up at 40 inches, which is a bar, not a vanity.

Then measure the route the vanity travels, because nobody does and deliveries get refused at the front step: the front door, the corridor, the bathroom door, the turn at the stairs. A 30-inch cabinet does not go through a 28-inch door, and no amount of on-site assembly fixes a one-piece top that cannot round the corner. The last piece of the wall, above the counter, is the [mirror and its mounting height](/blog/double-vanity-mirror-placement-guide/), which wants the same measure-first treatment the cabinet just got.

For a wall narrower than about 24 inches, the honest answer is that no vanity fits, and the [wall-hung floating style](/blog/how-to-style-a-floating-vanity/) with a slim basin, or a pedestal with a tall cabinet elsewhere, beats forcing a 16-inch cabinet that stores one roll of tape.
