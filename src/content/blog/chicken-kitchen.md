To understand Chicken Kitchen, start with one dish. The Chop-Chop is a bowl of chopped grilled chicken with 28 possible toppings, and the official menu says it can be built into thousands of combinations. Miami New Times goes further and says that before the Chipotle burrito bowl and before Pollo Tropical's Tropi-Chop, there was the Chicken Kitchen Chop-Chop. That claim is the closest thing this chain has to a origin story in a single sentence.

Everything else follows from it: the open flame grill, the Mediterranean framing, the quick-service counter, the yellow branding. This piece starts with the dish and works backwards to the company, then separates it from two other restaurants with the same name.

## The dish that made the name

The menu page describes Chop-Chop bowls as big, bold, delicious and highly customizable. You can build your own or choose from eleven existing favourites. With 28 toppings on offer, the number of combinations is large enough that the chain markets the choice itself.

The other named items on the official menu give a sense of the range. There is the Wrapito, a Mini-Chop, salads, grilled breast meals, a Cheesadilla, Healthy Kids Meals, side orders and desserts. The sides include Balsamic Tomatoes, Corn Mix and Sweet Potatoes. The menu also calls out authentic guacamole, a Chocolate Brownie and a pair of signature sauces, Mustard'n Curry and Spicy Mustard'n Curry.

That is a small menu by modern standards. It is built around one protein and a set of variations, which is exactly how a quick-service kitchen stays fast. The [cabinet zone map](/kitchen/cabinets/) uses the same idea in a domestic setting: a limited set of well-chosen parts does more than a large collection of vague ones.

Miami New Times identifies the founder as Christian de Berdouare and quotes the line about the Chop-Chop predating better-known chopped bowls. A visitor account on a travel site calls it the dish that revolutionised the bowl format long before bowls became fashionable, and mentions generous portions. These are secondary sources, and I would treat the "first" claim as local pride rather than a verified fact. What is verifiable is that the dish is central to the brand today.

## The company behind the bowl

The official site says the concept began in the fall of 1985, when de Berdouare, calling himself the Chief Chickenologist, opened a 300-square-foot store at the corner of 62nd Street and Second Avenue in New York City. It describes the moment as a reaction to a country hooked on greasy burgers and fried food. The idea was to serve freshly prepared, healthy meals from natural ingredients at prices people could afford.

The founder's own words set out the rules. Nothing canned, frozen or processed. No hormones, antibiotics or genetically modified ingredients. The chicken is grilled, broiled or steamed rather than fried, reheated or microwaved. It is cooked on an open flame in view of customers, and the side dishes are made from scratch in small batches through the day.

The menu page gives the food a deeper backstory. It says the recipes are inspired by the countries de Berdouare lived in: France, Greece and Italy. It ties them to the Mediterranean diet and to the home cooking of his mother Katerina, who prepared food for him and his brother Alain. That is a founder story, not an independent nutritional finding, and the page presents the Mediterranean diet as one of the healthiest in the world.

The site also says that after 39 years the company had opened 30 locations in Florida and Texas. A new store is advertised at 15738 SW 72nd Street in Miami, with the phone number (786) 703-9522. A location intelligence firm tracked 29 locations in August 2026, while another industry profile said 13 as of November 2025 and described branches in Coral Gables, Kendall, Brickell and Doral. Those numbers disagree, which is normal for chains that open and close sites. The official finder is the one to trust.

## Why the bowl idea worked

A Chop-Chop is not a complicated invention. It is chopped grilled chicken, a set of vegetables and sides, and a sauce, served in a bowl. Its success is a lesson in reducing decisions. The customer picks a base, a set of toppings and a sauce, and the kitchen assembles it at speed.

This is the same logic behind a good small kitchen. If the ingredients are visible and the tools are in reach, dinner is a sequence of small choices. If they are buried, every meal begins with a search. The [small pantry ideas](/blog/small-pantry-cabinet-organization-hacks-for-apartment/) and the [fridge zone guide](/kitchen/fridge/) both attack that problem, and the [clear bin guide](/blog/best-clear-plastic-bins-for-fridge-storage/) explains why seeing what you own changes what you cook.

A build-your-own bowl also shifts responsibility. The chain can claim a healthy meal because the raw materials are simple, while the customer decides how much sauce and which toppings go in. That is clever, and it is worth being honest about. The label on the sign does not choose your lunch. Your choices do.

## The other Chicken Kitchens

The name is not unique, and the third-party listings make the problem worse by mixing them together.

Chicken Kitchen in Toronto describes itself as a home-grown Canadian brand serving chicken sandwiches. Its site says the chicken is cut by hand and prepared fresh each day from local Canadian Grade A birds. That is a sandwich shop, not the Miami bowl chain.

Chicken Kitchen in London takes another direction entirely. Its own site calls it the home of London jerk chicken, with locations in Camden, Shepherd's Bush, Acton and Tottenham, all halal. It promotes a Dumpling Burger that combines jerk chicken with a fried dumpling bun. Again, no Chop-Chop.

This is the practical reason to lead with the city. If a search result mentions a bowl with 28 toppings, it is the Miami company. If it mentions a sandwich or a dumpling bun, you are somewhere else.

The answer to what Chicken Kitchen is known for, then, is the Chop-Chop. The answer to where it is, is Miami and a handful of nearby states. The other restaurants with the name have their own dishes and their own addresses, and they should be read on their own terms.
