The bin came out of the garage in April smelling like a basement. It had a lid that clicked shut, a label that said weather resistant, and six months of my wool coats inside. Three of them had mildew spots along the folds. The lid had done its job; the clothes had not been dry enough, and the seal I had paid extra for kept the damp in.

That is the part a product listing will not tell you. A garage is not a closet with a different address. It is an unconditioned room with a concrete floor, a temperature swing, and a relative humidity that can run from 30 percent in January to 90 percent in July. Choosing a bin means choosing which of those problems it actually solves.

## The garage is a different climate

Three things attack clothes in a garage.

- **Concrete.** A slab holds moisture and sits several degrees colder than the air. A bin directly on it becomes the coldest surface in the room, and condensation forms on the inside of the lid.
- **Temperature swings.** An unheated garage can go from 100°F in August to 14°F in January. Plastic expands and contracts, lids bow, and cheap resin turns brittle in the cold.
- **Humidity.** Wet cars, a washer, a freezer, a door left open in the rain. Even with the lid shut, vapor gets in through a lid that is not gasketed.

A bin in a bedroom closet has to keep dust off. A bin in a garage has to hold a seal through all three.

## Six bins, five rejected

I bought six different bins over two winters and kept exactly one. The elimination went in order, and each rung removed something.

1. **No gasket.** The cheap snap-lid tote. Dust and humidity came in through the seam. Rejected in a month.
2. **Gasket, two latches.** Better seal, but the lid bowed between the two latches when it got hot, and the gasket lost contact at the corners. Rejected.
3. **Six latches, polypropylene.** Held a seal, then cracked at a corner in February. PP gets brittle below about 40°F. Rejected.
4. **Thin walls.** Measured 1.5 mm at the side. Stacked two high and the bottom bin bowed outward, which broke the lid seal on the one above it. Rejected.
5. **Clear, no UV rating.** Fine for a month, then the plastic went cloudy and a blue coat faded on the side facing the window. Rejected.
6. **HDPE, gasketed, six latches, 3 mm walls, opaque.** This one survived eighteen months. It is the one still on the shelf.

The order matters. A gasket is worth nothing without latches to hold it, and latches are worth nothing if the walls flex under a stack.

## Read the spec sheet, not the label

Every word on the front of a bin is marketing. The useful numbers are on the bottom or in the listing details.

| Label says | What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| weather resistant | continuous gasket, EPDM or closed-cell foam | a snap lid without a seal leaks vapor |
| secure latches | 4 to 6 latch points | 2 points let the lid bow in heat |
| heavy duty | HDPE, resin code 2 | PP (code 5) gets brittle in cold |
| stackable | side wall 2.5 to 3 mm | 1.5 mm walls bow and break the seal |
| large capacity | 20 to 30 gal, stated stack load | above 3 high the bottom bin takes 100 lb |
| clear | UV stabilized, or keep it dark | light fades fabric and degrades plastic |

The resin code is the one people skip. HDPE takes impact at low temperature and does not craze. Polypropylene is clearer and cheaper and cracks at the corners in a cold garage. Both are fine in a bedroom; only one is fine out there.

## The gasket is a two-way trap

Here is the thing that ruined my coats. A gasket keeps water vapor out. It also keeps water vapor in.

Clothes that look dry can hold 8 to 15 percent of their weight in moisture, and wool holds more than cotton. Seal a slightly damp coat in a gasketed bin in October and the vapor has nowhere to go. By April it has condensed on the lid and dripped back onto the folds. The bin did not fail. I did.

The packing order fixes it:

1. Wash everything, then dry it a full 24 hours longer than seems necessary.
2. Let the bin itself air out with the lid off for a day, especially if it is new.
3. Put two to four silica gel packets in a 20 gallon bin, in a mesh pouch at the top, not buried under clothes.
4. Wrap wool and silk in muslin or acid-free tissue before they go in. Plastic against protein fiber for years is a bad trade, which is why a [breathable garment bag](/blog/breathable-garment-bags-for-wedding-dress-storage/) beats a sealed one for a gown.
5. Fill the bin loosely. Compressing a down coat to make it fit crushes the loft, same problem as the [vacuum bags for bulky bedding](/blog/space-saving-storage-bags-for-blankets-bulky-clothes/).
6. Label the outside with the contents and the date, not just "winter".

## What goes in, and what stays out

Wool, cashmere, and holiday knits belong in a bin only after a wash and a full dry. Body oil is what attracts moth larvae, and the [cashmere care routine](/blog/how-to-wash-and-store-cashmere-sweaters/) and the [holiday sweater folding rules](/blog/storing-holiday-sweaters-without-stretching/) both start there. Add a [cedar block](/blog/cedar-blocks-for-closet-moth-protection/) if you want a deterrent, but remember it repels adults and does not kill larvae.

Keep out of a garage bin: leather, anything with a wet stain, down with any damp left in it, and anything you will need in the next eight weeks. A sealed bin in a cold garage is also a bad home for ski boot liners; the [ski gear plan](/blog/how-to-store-ski-gear-in-a-closet/) keeps those in a closet with airflow.

For the pieces that should stay near the door, the [under-bed boxes](/blog/under-bed-winter-clothes-storage-boxes/) are the better tool. A garage bin is for the six-month category, not the weekly one.

## Where the bin sits

Placement does as much as the lid.

- Get it off the concrete. A pallet, a wire shelf, or even two boards giving 2 to 4 inches of air gap stops the cold-surface condensation.
- Keep it away from the water heater, the washer, and the dryer vent. Those are the humidity sources in most garages.
- Keep it out of direct sun. A garage window will fade fabric through a clear lid and make the plastic brittle.
- Stack no more than three high, heaviest at the bottom, and check the stated stack load.
- Leave an inch between the bin and the wall so air can move.

## The twice-a-year check

A sealed bin is not a set-and-forget system.

- **Spring**, when the winter clothes go in: open last year's bin, smell it, check the folds, replace the silica packets, and recharge or replace the cedar.
- **Fall**, when they come out: air the clothes for a day before they go into the closet, and wipe the inside of the bin with a dry cloth.

That is the [winter clothes swap](/blog/how-to-store-winter-clothes-in-summer/) and the [heavy coat plan](/blog/where-to-store-heavy-winter-coats-off-season/) on a calendar, which the [seasonal rotation checklist](/blog/seasonal-closet-rotation-checklist-printable/) makes hard to forget. A written inventory card taped to the lid is the small habit that stops you opening four bins to find one scarf, and it is the same labeling logic the [whole-home system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) uses everywhere else.

## What I would buy now

One opaque HDPE bin, 27 gallon, continuous gasket, six latches, 3 mm walls, on a shelf four inches off the concrete, with silica in a pouch and a date on the lid. That is the entire answer, and it cost about forty dollars.

Five bins taught me that the seal is the easy part. The hard part is bone-dry clothes, air under the bin, and a date you will actually check. Buy for the climate, not for the stack of labels on the front.
