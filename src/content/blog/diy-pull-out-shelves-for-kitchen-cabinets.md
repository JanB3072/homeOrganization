The pot that broke me lived in the back-left corner of the base cabinet, under the shelf, behind the colander, in the part of the kitchen cabinet that no flashlight angle ever fully reached. Retrieving it was a two-handed excavation with the cabinet door biting my shoulder, and by the time it came out I had usually forgotten what I opened the cabinet for. That corner held roughly a third of the cabinet's volume and got roughly none of its use, which is the standard arithmetic of a deep cabinet: everything in the back is storage in name only.

The pull-out shelves that fixed it were a Saturday, three drawer boxes, four pairs of slides, and about $38 in hardware per cabinet, no cabinet removal, doors still on, countertop untouched. The install itself is genuinely simple, screw slides level, tap boxes on, adjust. What separates the working pull-out from the drawer that binds, rattles, or never clears the door is not skill. It is five measurements, taken before the hardware store run, each one guarding against a specific way this project fails. That sequence is the whole article, because the install steps are ten minutes of screwdriving and the measurements are the build.

## Measurement one: opening width, at the hinge

The first number is the cabinet's inside width, and the trap is where you take it. The door hinge sits inside the opening and steals real estate, about an inch on a full-inset door, closer to half on a surface-mount, and the drawer box must clear it at full extension or the box chews the hinge every cycle. This is the same geometry that stole two inches of width in the [narrow pantry cabinet](/blog/small-pantry-cabinet-organization-hacks-for-apartment/), cabinets lie about their interiors and the hinge is always the liar. Measure at the hinge side, at mid-height, because boxes are rarely square, and write down the smaller of the two widths the cabinet offers.

## Measurement two: depth, which is really the slide length

Interior depth back wall to door frame sets the slide length, and slide length sets the box depth. Full-extension ball-bearing slides come in even lengths, 16, 18, 20, 22 inches, and the box depth matches the slide, sitting one to two inches short of the cabinet's total depth so the drawer front can clear the closed door. The expensive mistake runs the other direction: buying slides longer than the depth allows means the drawer cannot close, and the return trip to the hardware store is cheaper than the return trip through a mail-order slide that turns out to be two inches too ambitious.

## Measurements three and four: the vertical stack

The third number is the gap between shelves, or between the bottom shelf and the countertop, because that gap is the drawer's height budget and it divides into a pot drawer and shallow ones. Ten inches stacks a stockpot with its lid on, five inches handles the everyday stuff, and the stack in my cabinet ran three drawers: pot box low, two shallow boxes above, filling a 22-inch gap with about an inch of breathing room at the top. The fourth number is the one nobody mentions: the slide bracket itself needs about an inch of mounting height at its rail line, and if the drawer's top edge sits flush against the shelf above, the slide has nowhere to screw. A half inch shaved off the box height at planning time saves a redrilled mount line later.

## Measurement five: the door swing

The last measurement is the door's actual opening angle, because a pull-out deeper than the door can swing is a drawer that opens to two-thirds and stops, its back contents still in cabinet-shadow, which rebuilds the original problem inside the new hardware. Hinges open 95 to 110 degrees in a normal kitchen. Hold the door open, note where it stops, and check the box clears that angle with an inch to spare. If the door is lazy, a $6 hinge adjustment buys the degrees, and it is the cheapest fix in this whole project.

## The arithmetic, then the Saturday

With five numbers on tape, the box math falls out: box width equals opening width minus an inch per slide side minus an eighth-inch tolerance, because a quarter inch under and the drawer binds, an eighth over and it rattles down the road. The build is pine sides, plywood bottoms, glue and screws, diagonals checked square before the glue set, because an out-of-square box binds in its slides forever and no tuning rescues a parallelogram. Slides screw to level lines inside the cabinet, boxes tap on, ten empty runs each, adjusted forward or back in half-inch steps until the box clears the door at full extension and closes flush.

The slides are where the money matters. Ball-bearing full-extension glides at about $12 a pair are the difference between this upgrade working and not working, because a three-quarter-extension slide hides the back third of the box, and the back third is where the pots were dying in the first place. The epoxy budget glides are the classic false economy, sagging under a pot load within a year. Full extension is the entire point: the drawer comes out past the door with everything facing the room, one motion out, one motion back, the vending machine standard this kitchen's cabinets now share with the pantry.
