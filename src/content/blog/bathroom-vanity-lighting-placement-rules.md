Lighting guides for vanities all converge on the same conclusions: sconces beside the mirror at around eye height, 3000 K, CRI 90 and up, a dimmer if you can. All true, all repeated, and almost none of them show the working. The conclusions come from photometry, the arithmetic of how light actually travels, and once that arithmetic is on the table, the rules stop being taste and become consequences. They also start bending usefully for rooms that do not match the assumed layout, which is where most real bathrooms live.

## Two laws and a face

Light spreads from a source like an inflating sphere, so the amount landing on a given area falls with the square of the distance: double the distance, a quarter of the illuminance. And a surface only receives the component of light that strikes it squarely, which drops with the cosine of the angle between the beam and the surface normal. Two lines of physics, but they explain more vanity lighting arguments than colour temperature ever will.

Now apply them to the target, which is not the vanity at all. It is a face: a mostly vertical, mostly concave object standing about 20 to 24 inches from the mirror. Vertical surfaces are terrible customers for ceiling lights. A downlight 6 feet up strikes a standing face at a grazing angle, so most of its output skims past, and what does land comes from above, filling the brow and throwing the eye sockets and the underside of the jaw into shadow. This is why every bathroom with a single central ceiling light is also every bathroom where makeup happens badly.

Side light, from sconces at face height, does the opposite. Arranged at roughly 45 degrees to the face, its beams arrive close to square-on, which multiplies the effective illuminance from the same lumens, and because each side fills the shadows the other would cast, the face reads evenly in its own mirror. The classic advice, sconces at 60 to 66 inches flanking the mirror, is simply where those two conditions, eye-line height and near-square incidence, intersect for a standing adult. The 66-inch ceiling of the range exists because the top of most vanity mirrors sits near 75 to 80 inches, and a light source above the glass starts behaving like a smaller version of the ceiling light again.

Run one concrete number to show the difference. An 800-lumen LED bulb radiating evenly gives a luminous intensity near 64 candela. At 50 centimetres, a normal sconce-to-face distance, that is 64 over 0.25, roughly 250 lux square-on, call it 180 after the cosine at 45 degrees. Two of them, one per side, stack to around 400 lux, which lands exactly in the 400-to-500-lux band where shaving and makeup are comfortable. The same 800 lumens in a ceiling fixture 1.5 metres away and grazing the face deliver perhaps 60 or 70 effective lux. Same hardware store shelf, sixfold difference in the only number that matters, which is the light on the face.

## The three layers, with their numbers

Because the face's job changes across the day, one light level serves all of them badly, and the fix is layers, each with its own target.

| Layer | Target at the face | Source | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task | 400-500 lux | Side sconces or edge-lit mirror | Shaving, makeup, contact lenses |
| Ambient | 150-200 lux | Ceiling or wall washers | Moving around, cleaning, general use |
| Night | 20-50 lux, warm or amber | Low strip, nightlight, dimmed sconces | Not waking fully at 3 a.m. |

The task number is the one the arithmetic above produces. Ambient sits lower than instinct suggests; a bathroom bright enough to feel cheery at 9 a.m. is glare territory at 6 a.m., and 150 to 200 lux keeps the room navigable without weaponising the mirror. The night layer is the one most renovations skip and the one people miss most: at 3 a.m., the choice is not between darkness and 2,000 lumens, it is a 20-lux warm strip near the floor or the wall base that gets a body in and out without the full wake-up that bright overhead light guarantees. On a dimmer, the task layer can double as the night layer if the dimmer actually reaches low enough, which leads to the electrical half of this subject.

## Where the light hardware actually goes

With the physics set, the mounting questions become short. Sconce centres at 60 to 66 inches, flanking the mirror with the glass edge a few inches away so the source is visible to the face but not glaring down the sightline. If the room takes one light instead of two, a single sconce on the side of the dominant user, wall side usually, beats any overhead option. Vertical LED strips or edge-lit mirrors integrate the same geometry into the glass, and a well-made one measures its output in the task band; the selection criteria for those, CRI and colour, are worked through in the [vanity table guide](/blog/best-makeup-vanity-table-with-lights-storage/), so here it is enough to say that the geometry of an edge-lit mirror is the geometry of two sconces flattened into the glass, and the same 400-lux target applies.

A light bar above the mirror is the layout most houses arrive with, because the builder put one electrical box up there and everything followed. It fails the incidence test but not hopelessly: bars that angle their output downward and forward, rather than spraying the wall, put meaningfully more light on the face, and the shadow penalty shrinks as the bar moves closer to the glass and lower toward the top of it. Before re-plastering walls to move boxes, a plug-in pair of uplights or side lamps on the counter edges, or a mirror with integrated side light, recovers most of the benefit for the cost of an outlet, which the [counter guide](/blog/bathroom-counter-clutter-solutions/) already covers from the receptacle side.

What placement cannot fix is what the [mirror height guide](/blog/double-vanity-mirror-placement-guide/) calls the light's second job: the light is also in the mirror. Any source within the reflected field sits in your eyes while you look at your own face, a veil of glare over the whole image. Bare bulbs in the reflected sightline are the worst case; shaded sources, or strips mounted along the vertical edges of the glass where the reflection sits beside your face rather than in it, keep the image clean. This is also why frosted shades win in a bathroom even though clear glass photographs better.

## What the shade costs, and the beam angle

The arithmetic above assumed a bare, even source, and no bathroom sconce is one. A shade subtracts. An opal glass or frosted diffuser typically costs 20 to 30 percent of the raw lumens, a fabric shade closer to 40, and a mirrored or heavily figured glass can eat half. The 400-lux target survives this if you budget it in: two 800-lumen bulbs behind opal shades deliver roughly 280 effective lumens each, which still lands the face in the low 300s of lux at 45 degrees, enough for routine but shy for makeup, which is the arithmetic behind the common advice to oversize vanity bulbs one step beyond what feels right in the showroom. Showrooms also cheat by distance: a bulb judged at arm's length under shop lighting performs differently at 50 centimetres beside a mirror at 6 a.m., and the number on the box is the only part of that comparison that travels.

Beam angle matters as much as the shade. A bare LED chip behaves like a point source and dumps much of its light into a narrow cone; a sconce designed for vanity work, with an internal reflector or a diffusing lens, spreads that cone across a wider angle, which is what turns one bright spot into an even field across the face. Two setups with identical lumen boxes can differ by a third in effective facial illuminance purely on beam spread, and the cheap test in a shop is to hold your palm where your cheek would be: an even warm patch across the whole palm beats a hot centre with dim edges, whatever the packaging claims.

One more photometric interaction worth knowing: colour temperature and illuminance lean on each other. At the 400-to-500-lux task level, 3000 K reads warm and correct, and 2700 K starts to feel dim and yellow even at the same measured lux. At night-layer levels, 20 to 50 lux, the preference flips, and the same 2700 K or warmer reads restful while 3000 K starts to feel clinical. This is the practical version of why hotel bathrooms feel different at midnight than at 7 a.m. even when nothing was changed: the lighting designer built two scenes, and a two-circuit or well-dimmed setup lets a home do the same.

## A worked example: one 72-inch double vanity

The same method, run on a real layout, shows where the standard advice bends. Take the 72-inch double vanity from the mirror guide: basins centred 30 inches apart, a mirror over each, one electrical box behind the mirror line and none on the side walls, which is the constraint most homes actually present.

The textbook answer, four sconces flanking both mirrors, needs two new boxes per side, which on a tiled wall is a re-tiling conversation. The honest compromise that the arithmetic supports: two sconces at the outer edges of the pair, at 60 to 66 inches and about 26 to 30 inches from each basin's centre, plus one shared light between the mirrors, which the box behind the glass line can feed without new circuits. Each user then has a near-square side source on the wall side, the centre light fills the inner side's shadow at a slightly steeper but workable angle, and the facial illuminance lands in the mid-300s of lux, short of textbook but comfortably usable. What does not work in this layout, and the calculation is what shows it rather than taste, is keeping the original single bar above the mirrors and hoping: grazing incidence plus distance puts both users under 100 effective lux no matter how bright the bar.

The remaining gap closes with the cheapest move in the whole subject, which is letting each user's side source do double duty and adding an edge-lit mirror later if the makeup standard is strict. Lighting admits incremental fixes in a way that tiling never does, which is a decent argument for solving the geometry first and the fixture budget second.

The layout also deserves one check that costs a phone: after the fixtures go in, photograph the face in the mirror under task light alone, from where you actually stand. Shadows read in a photo more honestly than they do live, because the eye adapts and forgives in a way the sensor does not. Deep sockets or a dark jaw in the picture mean the sources drifted too high or too far back, and nudging the bulbs an inch down the wall, or switching a flat-glass shade for one that throws forward, moves more light onto the face than any bulb swap will.

## The electrical half

Placement rules get all the attention, and half the real-world failures are electrical. A dimmer and an LED have to be sold as a pair; mismatched, the combination flickers at low output, hums, or refuses to dim below about 30 percent, which quietly kills the night layer. Load matters in the other direction too, because old dimmers expect tens of watts and a pair of 9-watt LEDs may sit below the minimum, which is the flicker nobody can diagnose by looking at the bulb.

Switch placement is a layout decision that outlives every fixture. The switch belongs inside the door, reachable without stepping into the splash zone, and where a room serves two users, splitting the mirror task circuit from the ambient circuit is the cheapest upgrade that survives every argument about brightness. The fan deserves its own switch or a timer, because a fan wired to the light goes off the moment the light does, and the humidity control that protects the cabinets, the mirrors and the stone runs on a 20-minute tail, as the [floating vanity guide](/blog/how-to-style-a-floating-vanity/) argues from the cabinetry side.

Ratings close the loop: damp-rated fittings anywhere the general bathroom humidity lives, wet-rated only where spray lands directly. And the whole room's circuits sit behind GFCI protection as a matter of both code and the arithmetic of wet hands and metal taps, which the counter post covers from the outlet side.

One last electrical fact that placement guides skip: the 400-lux plan has a half-life. LEDs fade before they die, drifting down toward 70 percent of their initial output over the years the box calls their lifetime, and in a bathroom the diffusers collect a film of dust and soap mist that another 10 to 20 percent quietly absorbs. The mirror task that measured 450 lux on install day can be at 300 within three or four years with nothing broken, which explains the slow feeling that the bathroom "seems darker than it used to be". A quarterly wipe of the shades, and a note of the install date on the inside of the cabinet, turn that drift from a mystery into a schedule.

## Renters, and the no-wiring versions

Nothing above requires an electrician in a rental. Plug-in sconces hung on the wall beside the mirror, battery strip lights with adhesive mounts, and rechargeable bar lights inside a mirror cabinet reproduce the side-light geometry with an outlet and a hook. The honest limits: battery strips dim fast near end of charge, adhesive mounts in a steamy room share the fate of every suction cup, and a plug hanging beside a basin wants a drip loop so water runs off the cord, not into the socket. Imperfect, reversible, and geometrically correct beats a perfect install in a house you do not own.

The light is also the last consumer of the [counter's display logic](/blog/vanity-top-makeup-organizer-ideas/): whatever sits in the back row lives under the heat of the nearest source, which is one more argument for sconces on the wall instead of lamps among the bottles.

The rest of the [vanity guides](/bathroom/vanity/) handle the furniture, the mirror above it and the stone on it. On the wall beside the glass, the whole subject compresses to four sentences: aim at 400 to 500 lux square on the face, get there from the sides at eye height, layer a lower ambient and a low warm night level behind it, and make sure the wiring, dimmer and ratings agree with the plan before the fixtures are chosen.
