The sweater smelled like a cedar chest and still had three holes under the arm. It had lived a full year between two cedar blocks, and the blocks had done exactly what cedar does: they made the closet smell like a forest and did nothing about the larvae already in the wool.

That is the gap between the marketing and the biology. Cedar is a decent deterrent and a poor treatment, and the difference matters because most moth damage happens in a stage you never see. If you understand which stage eats, you can use cedar where it works and stop expecting it to do a job it was never able to do.

## The moth only damages in one stage

The clothes moth has four stages, and only one of them eats fabric.

1. **Egg**, four to ten days. Laid in dark crevices, often on the fabric itself.
2. **Larva**, one to three months. This is the only stage that eats, and it prefers protein fibers: wool, cashmere, silk, fur, and feathers.
3. **Pupa**, eight to forty days depending on temperature. It does not eat.
4. **Adult**, one to two weeks. It does not eat either. Its whole job is to mate and lay eggs.

So the holes you find in November were made months earlier, by a larva the size of a grain of rice, in the dark, usually under the arm or at the collar where body oil collects.

## What cedar actually does

Cedar contains aromatic oils, mainly thujaplicin and cedrol, that repel adult moths and make the closet a less attractive place to lay eggs. That is a real effect. It is also a narrow one.

- It does not kill eggs.
- It does not reliably kill larvae already inside a sweater.
- It fades. The oil is volatile, and the surface dries out over months.
- It works better in dry air. A humid closet cuts the effect.

A cedar block is a fence, not an exterminator. It keeps new moths out and does little for the ones already in.

## The stage-by-stage ledger

| Stage | Duration | Eats fabric? | Cedar effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg | 4 to 10 days | no | discourages the adult from laying nearby |
| Larva | 1 to 3 months | yes, all the damage | repels some, does not kill |
| Pupa | 8 to 40 days | no | none |
| Adult | 1 to 2 weeks | no | repels, does not kill |

Read that table as a job description. Cedar covers the adult stage, partially. Freezing and washing cover the egg and larval stages. You need both, and cedar is the weaker half.

## How many blocks, and where

The label guidance most brands print is one block per 100 cubic feet. A closet 5 feet wide, 2.5 feet deep, and 8 feet tall is exactly that, so a standard reach-in closet takes one to two blocks, and a walk-in takes four to six.

Where they go matters more than the count.

- One near the rod, every 10 to 12 inches, so the vapor sits where the shoulders of the clothes are.
- One on each shelf, at the back, behind the folded stack rather than on top of it.
- One per drawer, in a cloth pouch.
- One in each storage box of off-season wool, which is where the damage usually happens.

Keep the block out of direct contact with light fabric. The vapor is what works; the oil can leave a mark on a white blouse or an untreated silk scarf. A small cotton pouch solves it.

## Refresh, do not replace

Cedar blocks are sold as if they expire. They do not. They dry out.

Run fine sandpaper over the surface every three to six months and the scent comes back, because you have exposed fresh wood and fresh oil. A few drops of cedar oil on the same block does the same job. The block only needs replacing when it has cracked or lost its shape.

This is the part that gets skipped, and it is why so many people conclude cedar does not work. A block that has been sitting on a shelf for two years is a piece of wood with no oil left in it.

## If you already have holes

Cedar will not fix this, and neither will a stronger-smelling block. The order is clean, freeze, vacuum, then protect.

1. Bag every wool, cashmere, silk, and fur item.
2. Freeze at minus 18°C for 48 hours. That kills eggs and larvae, which cedar cannot.
3. Wash or dry clean everything, because body oil is what attracts the larva in the first place.
4. Vacuum the closet, including the rod, the shelf edges, the corners, and the floor seam. Eggs sit in lint, not only in fabric.
5. Put the cedar in after the cleanout, as a barrier against the next generation.

If you find live larvae or repeated holes after that, add pheromone traps to monitor and consider a professional treatment. Cedar is not the tool for an active infestation.

## The smell is not the proof

A strong cedar smell feels like protection, which is why the block that failed on my sweater still seemed to be working. The scent is a proxy for oil, not for efficacy, and it fades long before the risk does.

Cedar is the last step, not the first. ## Where cedar fits in the rotation

Cedar is a seasonal tool, and the seasonal reset is when it earns its place. When the wool comes out in October, sand the blocks, check the folds, and put one in every bin. When it goes away in April, do the reverse and freeze anything that looked suspicious.

## What I do now

Four blocks in a reach-in closet, sanded every spring, one in each wool bin. The sweater with the holes went to the tailor for a patch, and the rest of the closet got a vacuum and a freeze before anything went back.

Cedar did not save that sweater. It was never going to. What it does is make the closet a slightly worse place for a moth to start, and that is worth four blocks and ten minutes of sanding a year. Expect a fence, not a fumigation, and the holes stop surprising you.
