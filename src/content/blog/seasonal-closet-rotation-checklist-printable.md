The first rotation checklist I made had forty-one lines. I filled it in once, in October, then never looked at it again, because a list that long is a second job and the closet was already finished by line twelve.

The version that survived three years has twelve lines and one column that no printable I have seen includes. That column is what this page is about, because the tasks were never the hard part.

## A checklist answers three questions, not forty

Most rotation sheets are task lists. Take out the summer things, put in the winter things, vacuum the bags, add cedar, wipe the shelf. That is one afternoon of work and it is genuinely the easy half.

The hard half is the three questions that decide whether next October is faster or slower than this one. Which garments actually got worn. Where each thing went. And what you were unsure about, recorded at the moment you were unsure.

| Column | What it holds | What it buys you |
|---|---|---|
| Item | Garment, roughly identified | The only written record of what you own |
| Last worn | A month, not a date | Turns "I might wear it" into evidence |
| Where it went | Bin number, shelf, or offsite | Spring stops being a treasure hunt |
| Hold | Undecided items plus one date | Keeps the decision from blocking the job |

Four columns. Everything else on the sheet is optional, and most of it should be cut.

## The Hold column is the whole system

Every rotation stalls at the same place: a garment you are not ready to decide about. It is expensive, or it fit two summers ago, or it was a gift. Standing there holding it, you either keep it out of guilt or spend ten minutes arguing with yourself, and both options cost the afternoon.

A hold column gives that garment somewhere to go. You write it down, write the date sixty days out, and put it in a labelled bin. The job continues.

Labelling matters more than it sounds. The bin needs the date on the outside, not just on the sheet, because by the time it matters the sheet is in a drawer and the bin is in front of you. A strip of masking tape with the month written on it does the work, and peeling the tape off when the bin is emptied is the reminder that the sixty days passed. I have missed that reminder twice, found the tape still on the bin in March, and each time the honest move was to keep the contents, because a deadline nobody tracked is not a deadline.

Sixty days later, one of two things has happened. You went and got it, which means it earned its place, or you did not, which is the answer the argument was never going to produce. Working through the whole closet this way, roughly a fifth of what I owned went into hold bins in year one, and about four fifths of that fifth left.

The date is what makes it work. Without one, a hold bin is just the place clothes go to be forgotten with a clear conscience.

## Trigger on weather, not on the calendar

October 1 is the date most guides suggest, and it is wrong most years, in both directions. The year I switched early, the last warm fortnight arrived with the coats already binned. The year I switched late, I wore the same thin jacket for three weeks and never quite caught up.

Three consecutive nights below 10°C is the trigger out. Three consecutive days above 15°C is the trigger back in spring. Both are visible in a ten day forecast, and both are more reliable than a date chosen in a magazine.

The practical version is to work the evening before the third cold night, so the coats are on the rail the morning they are needed. Doing it after the cold arrives means a week of wearing a summer jacket in bad weather, and that week is where most people decide rotation is a chore rather than a fix. The same logic runs in reverse in spring: a warm fortnight in March is not the signal, a warm fortnight in April usually is.

The related mistake is treating this as two events. Transitional weather means two wardrobe states that do not fit either season, so the honest version is four small passes: early autumn, late autumn, early spring, late spring. Each pass moves a dozen garments rather than the whole rail, and each one takes under an hour.

Splitting the big switch into a take-out day and a put-in day is worth the extra scheduling. The take-out half is fast, because you are only removing. The put-in half is where decision fatigue sets in, and it goes noticeably better on a fresh morning.

## The sheet has to survive the closet, not the printer

A printable that only looks right on screen fails in the room where it is used.

A4 is too large. It does not clip to a rail without folding, and a folded sheet is a sheet you stop reading. A5 at 148 by 210 mm clips to the rod with a bulldog clip, sits in a coat pocket, and still has room for four columns and twelve rows.

Paper weight matters more than people expect. 80 gsm curls at the corners within a week in a humid closet, and a curled sheet slides out of the clip. 120 gsm or heavier stays flat through a winter.

The clip is part of the system. A sheet left on the bed gets moved twice, put on the dresser, and abandoned. A sheet hanging in front of you at eye level gets filled in because it is already there.

And leave the bottom third blank. Every printable I have used needed rows I did not anticipate, and a sheet with no empty rows becomes a sheet with cramped margins and unreadable notes.

## The tick mark illusion

Here is the failure that took me two years to notice. A checklist full of ticks feels like proof of progress, but a tick only records that you did a thing. It says nothing about whether the thing worked.

I had a sheet where every line was ticked for three consecutive seasons, and the closet was no better each time, because nothing on it recorded outcomes. The line that changed this was moving "last worn" to the front of the sheet and filling it before touching a hanger.

That column is uncomfortable in a useful way. A dress with last worn set to "last June" and a hold date thirty days out is a decision that has already been made, and the sheet just says it out loud. When it was my memory making that call, the dress stayed.

If the sheet is going to do one job, make it that one. Ticks are for tasks. Months are for clothes.

## Three things I stopped printing

The useful half of designing a sheet is subtraction, and three features I added in year one turned out to be noise.

Colour swatches. A row of little printed squares so you could mark whether a garment was black, navy or grey sounded sensible until I used it. The colour is on the garment. Writing it down costs a lookup and buys nothing, because nobody forgets that a coat is navy.

Dry cleaning dates. The idea was to track which pieces had been cleaned and when. In practice the receipt lives in the bag with the garment, and copying the date onto a sheet that lives in the closet creates two records that disagree within a month.

A separate spring list. For a while I kept a winter-out sheet and a winter-in sheet, on the theory that they were different jobs. They are the same job read in two directions, and two sheets mean the spring one never gets filled in because the autumn one is the one hanging on the rail. One sheet, both directions, same twelve rows.

What replaced all three was space. The blank bottom third of the sheet holds the things I did not anticipate, which every season turns out to be something.

## What I print now

Twelve rows, four columns, one date at the top. Half a page of tasks at the bottom, most of which I ignore.

The rotation itself is unchanged from what the [winter storage method](/blog/how-to-store-winter-clothes-in-summer/) describes: clean, dry, cedar for wool, no sealed plastic on anything that might still be damp. The containers are the [fabric bins that breathe](/blog/collapsible-storage-bins-with-lids-fabric-stackable/) for knitwear and the [flat under-bed boxes](/blog/under-bed-winter-clothes-storage-boxes/) for blankets, and the list records which bin number each went into.

Coming back in spring, the rail gets rebuilt in the [colour order](/blog/hanging-clothes-organization-by-color-guide/) that survives a rushed morning, which is faster to restore than to invent. The [capsule count](/blog/capsule-wardrobe-closet-layout-design/) caps how much returns at all: if a season's worth of clothes does not fit the rail, the surplus is a storage question, not a folding one.

The one line I added last year and would not remove is a count. Forty-two garments went out, thirty-one came back. The gap is the [decluttering pass](/blog/how-to-declutter-before-moving-checklist/) that happens on its own if you let the sheet ask the question, and it is why the [larger system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) starts with a list rather than a shelf.
