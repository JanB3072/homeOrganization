I bought the strip for bobby pins and spent the first evening testing everything else in the bathroom drawer. Of eleven small metal tools, six stuck, two clung doubtfully, and three dropped straight onto the counter. The failures included a pair of tweezers that looked exactly like the pair that held. That inconsistency is the whole subject, and it has a fairly ordinary metallurgical explanation.

## The stainless problem

Stainless is not one material. The grades used for grooming tools split into two families with different crystal structures. The 304 and 316 grades, sometimes stamped 18/8 or 18/10 for their chromium and nickel content, are austenitic: face-centred cubic, and essentially non-magnetic in the annealed state. The 430 and 420 grades are ferritic or martensitic, body-centred, and magnetic. Knife blades and cheap tweezers tend to be 420 because it can be hardened. Good corrosion-resistant tweezers tend to be 316 because it survives autoclaving and skin acids.

Then it gets messier. Cold working changes things. When you draw a wire, stamp a blank, or bend a pair of tweezers into shape at room temperature, some of the austenite transforms into strain-induced martensite, which is magnetic. So a tool made from 304 can pick up a noticeable pull along its bent tip while staying almost inert along the flat handle. That is why a bobby pin, which is hard-drawn spring steel, grips beautifully, and a polished stainless nail file may not grip at all. Aluminium, brass, titanium, and plastic never will.

I now test with the actual magnet before I plan any layout. A fridge magnet is too weak to be a fair test; use the strip itself. Anything that needs a second chance to stay up goes back in the drawer. There is no point designing a tidy row of cells around tools that will be on the floor by Thursday.

## What the pull-force number means

Listings for magnetic strips quote a pull force, usually in pounds or kilograms, measured by pressing the magnet flat against a thick, clean, mild-steel plate and pulling perpendicular to the surface. Real use is nothing like that. On a vertical strip the force you need is resistance to sliding, which is roughly a third to a fifth of the perpendicular pull, sometimes less if the tool has a smooth plated finish. On top of that, magnetic force falls off steeply with distance. A millimetre of paint, a decorative vinyl wrap, or a rubber protective film between magnet and tool is enough to cut the available hold by a large fraction. This is why a strip that will snap a spanner to it will let a lightweight pair of eyebrow scissors creep slowly downward over an afternoon.

The practical consequence: hang light tools, and give them a small mechanical assist. A strip with a shallow lip along the bottom edge, or a strip mounted with a slight backward lean, catches anything that starts to slide. Some of the purpose-made bathroom strips have a row of small notches for exactly this reason. A perfectly flat, perfectly vertical expanse of magnet is the hardest version of the problem.

Neodymium versus ferrite is the other choice. Sintered NdFeB magnets have a very high energy product for their size, which is what makes a slim 12-inch strip possible, but the alloy is mostly iron and oxidises readily. Manufacturers plate them with nickel-copper-nickel or encase them in epoxy. In a bathroom, chloride from tap water and from cleaning sprays finds pinholes in that plating; the rust that forms underneath occupies more volume than the metal it replaced, and the coating blisters off. Ferrite, the dark ceramic magnet from a fridge sticker, is much weaker for the same area but is already an oxide and does not care about humidity. Inside a closed cabinet, away from the shower plume, a plated neodymium strip is fine. On an open wall three feet from a showerhead, I would use a stainless-covered steel plate with ferrite magnets behind it, or skip magnetism altogether.

## Mounting it, after getting the first one wrong

My first attempt was a peel-and-stick strip on the inside of a mirrored cabinet door. It lasted about three weeks. Adhesive foam tape holds well on a clean, dry, non-porous surface at room temperature; a cabinet that fills with steam twice a day is none of those things, and the weight of a dozen steel tools applies a constant peel load rather than a shear load. It is the same humidity problem that rules out wicker and cardboard on [open bathroom shelves](/blog/diy-bathroom-shelf-organization/). It came off during the night and I found the pins in the sink.

The second version used two screws into the door stile. Eight minutes, including finding a screwdriver. A few things I checked that time:

The strip goes about four inches below the top edge of the door, which puts it at eye level when the door is open and leaves the shelves alone. I held it in place with masking tape and closed the door slowly before drilling. On shallow cabinets, or on doors with a thick mirrored panel, the shelf fronts come surprisingly close to the back of the door, and anything hanging proud of the strip gets clipped every time you shut it. A 12-inch strip is usually enough; a 24-inch one invites you to fill it with tools you do not use.

Screw length matters on a 3/4-inch door. Standard cabinet doors are 15 to 19 mm thick, and the useful thread engagement in that is short. Use the shortest screws that will bite, or put a backing washer on the outside face, the same rule as mounting a [wood wall organizer](/blog/diy-bathroom-wall-organizer-wood/) into a stud. If the door is hollow-core or thin, mount a small steel sheet to the door with through-bolts and let the magnets stick to that. Trying to solve it with stronger magnets on the other side of a 19 mm panel will not work: at that distance the field is essentially gone.

I also moved the strip away from the hinge side by a couple of inches. Doors accelerate and stop abruptly, and a tool hanging near the outer edge gets a bigger jolt than one near the hinge. Everything stayed put after that.

One safety note that is easy to forget: keep strong magnets away from pacemakers and implantable defibrillators, generally cited as a six-inch clearance, and away from magnetic stripe cards and anything with a small Hall sensor. A strip inside a cabinet is unlikely to bother anyone, but a strip mounted at hip height on an outside wall in a narrow hallway is a different matter.

## What to put on it, and what to leave out

The things that work are small, flat, steel, and used often enough that you want them in reach: bobby pins, nail clippers, cuticle pushers, a metal nail file, small scissors with steel blades, the metal cap of a grooming tool. Tweezers go on if they pass the test; if they do not, a shallow acrylic pocket next to the strip is a better home than a drawer, and the [divided acrylic bins](/blog/clear-acrylic-bathroom-bins-with-dividers/) are sized for exactly those awkward little tools. Bobby pins are ideal. They are spring steel, they are light, and on a strip you can see every one of them, which means you stop finding them in the bathroom carpet with a bare foot.

Do not put anything wet on the strip. A damp razor or a just-rinsed clipper will leave a rust mark on a nickel-plated magnet within days, and the mark is impossible to polish out. Do not put tubes, pencils, or anything in a plastic case there. And do not hang anything you would mind dropping on a tile floor.

After six months I had about fourteen items on the strip and an empty space at one end. The empty space turned out to be useful. When I buy a new tool, it gets tested and either goes into that gap or back in the drawer. A strip filled edge to edge has no way to absorb anything new, and the overflow is what ends up loose on the counter.

## When it is the wrong fix

If your collection is mostly plastic, the magnet solves nothing. If the cabinet door is glass, or a mirrored panel you would rather not drill, a stick-on shallow tray or a divided drawer insert does the job with no compromise; the [vanity drawer guide](/blog/bathroom-vanity-drawer-organization-tips/) works through how to size those inserts. If you share the bathroom with small children, remember that a neodymium magnet and a handful of small steel objects are a swallowing hazard, and a screw-mounted strip at a reachable height is not a good idea. And if the real problem is that the drawer contains forty items rather than twelve, no organiser on earth will help until you throw some of them away.

For a small pile of steel bits in a steamy cabinet, though, it is a solid ten-dollar fix, and one of the simpler projects in the [bathroom storage section](/bathroom/storage/). Test the tools first, screw the strip to the door, keep the light things high, and leave a gap. The drawer stops being the place where sharp small objects hide.
