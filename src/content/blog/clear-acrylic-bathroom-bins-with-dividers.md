The first one I ordered was 14 inches deep. The drawer was 13 and a quarter. I know that now because I measured it after the box arrived and I could not close the drawer. It sat on the counter for a week while I decided whether to return it, holding nothing, and I noticed something else during that week: the corner seams had already gone slightly milky where the two panels were glued.

That purchase is the reason I now measure in a particular order and read the material line on listings instead of the photo.

## Measure the thing being hidden, then the drawer

Most people start with the gap in the furniture. That is backwards. The bin exists to hold a specific pile of objects, so start with the pile.

I emptied the drawer onto a towel and grouped what was there: three lipsticks, two mascaras, a pencil, a foundation bottle, a compact, two small palettes, a tube of concealer, twelve bobby pins, and a nail clipper. Then I measured the awkward ones. A lipstick is about 20 mm across. A mascara tube is roughly 25 mm. Foundation bottles run 35 to 45 mm, and a round compact can be 75 mm. Add 8 to 10 mm of clearance to each and you get your cell widths, with a little room for a finger to get in. Cells of 2 to 3 inches cover the narrow things; a 4-inch cell takes a compact or a couple of palettes. The [vanity drawer guide](/blog/bathroom-vanity-drawer-organization-tips/) works through the same clearance arithmetic for a whole drawer rather than one bin. Fixed grids built entirely from 2-inch cells look efficient in product photography and cannot hold a single foundation bottle upright.

Then measure the drawer, and measure the inside, not the outside. Vanity drawers commonly run 12 to 16 inches front to back, but the usable depth is often less than the nominal because of the drawer front, the runner clearance at the back, and, in a pedestal sink, the plumbing. Under-sink cabinets have a trap and supply lines eating into the top rear corner. A bin that is 1 inch too deep either stops the drawer closing or sits proud of the runner and gets scraped every time you pull it out.

Height is the third number and the one people forget. I have a drawer with 4 inches of clear space above it before the countertop. A 5-inch bin fits inside the drawer and cannot be lifted out again once it is there, which matters because you need to take it out to clean it. Lay your tallest bottle on a ruler, then check the clearance above the drawer with the drawer open.

## The material line is where the real difference hides

"Clear plastic organiser" covers three materials that behave very differently in a bathroom.

Cast PMMA, sold as acrylic or by trade names like Plexiglas, is the good one. It transmits about 92 percent of visible light, has a refractive index near 1.49, and is stable enough under UV that it is used for outdoor signage and aircraft glazing. It is not immune to sunlight, but on a bathroom windowsill it stays clear for years. Density is around 1.18 g/cm³, which is why a solid acrylic tray feels surprisingly heavy for its size.

Polystyrene, often labelled PS or just "clear plastic", is the cheap one. Its refractive index is about 1.59, so it actually looks a bit brighter and glassier than acrylic when new. The aromatic rings in its polymer backbone absorb ultraviolet light, and the resulting photo-oxidation both yellows the material and cuts the polymer chains, which makes it brittle. It is the same failure you see in [clear shoe boxes](/blog/clear-plastic-shoe-boxes-stackable-design/) left on a sunny shelf. A PS bin on a sunny sill can go straw-coloured and crack at a corner inside a year. It is also softer, so it scratches more readily.

PETG sits between them. It is tougher than acrylic and resists impact well, takes solvent cement less gracefully, and is more prone to fine scuffs. It is a reasonable choice for a bin that gets knocked around, less good if you care about optical clarity after a year.

The manufacturing method matters as much as the resin. A one-piece injection-moulded bin has no joints at all, though the need for a draft angle means the walls are subtly tapered and wall thickness has to be controlled to avoid sink marks. A fabricated bin is cut from flat sheet and the corners are joined with solvent cement, typically a chlorinated solvent that swells the two surfaces so the polymer chains interdiffuse. Done well, the joint is optically clear and nearly as strong as the sheet. Done badly, or done well but then stressed by a heavy load, the joint holds residual solvent and internal stress and develops crazing: a lacework of microscopic cracks that starts at the corner and spreads. Those are the milky corners I saw on the bin sitting on my counter.

## Solvents are the quiet way to ruin one

This is the part that is genuinely absent from most buying guides. PMMA is susceptible to stress cracking around certain chemicals. Isopropyl alcohol, acetone, ethyl acetate, and many essential oils will do it, especially at a stressed joint. A disinfecting wipe across the corner, a cotton pad soaked in nail-polish remover left in a cell, a perfume bottle with a leaking sprayer, an ammonia-based glass cleaner: any of these can produce a frosted patch that no amount of polishing removes, because the damage is inside the material rather than on the surface.

Cleaning that is safe is boring. Mild dish soap, lukewarm water, a microfibre cloth. Paper towels are wood pulp with a measurable mineral filler content and they leave a swirl of fine scratches that scatter light; after a dozen wipe-downs the bin looks permanently fogged. If something has dried on, soak it for a few minutes rather than scrubbing. And keep acetone-containing products in the cabinet, not in the acrylic.

## The leak-proof claim, taken literally

Listings use "leak-proof" loosely for open divided trays. It is worth doing the arithmetic. A 30 ml bottle of serum tipped onto a flat tray with 3 mm of depth will cover on the order of 100 square centimetres before surface tension and the walls stop it, which is most of a medium bin. Dividers help in one specific way: they confine the spill to one cell instead of the whole drawer, and they stop a rolling bottle from lying on its side and draining. They do not seal anything unless they are welded to the base, and most removable dividers are simply slotted in with a clearance gap at the bottom.

Real containment needs a gasketed lid: a silicone or TPE seal in a channel, plus latches to compress it. That exists, and it is useful for travel or for a cabinet shared with cleaning products. In a drawer you open every morning, a latched box becomes an annoyance within a fortnight. My compromise after the return was two containers: a small lidded PP box for anything liquid and leaky, and the open divided acrylic tray for pencils, tubes, compacts, and tools. Anything small and steel goes on the [magnetic strip](/blog/magnetic-organizer-for-bathroom-cabinet/) instead, which is a cheaper cell than any divider. The tray is what I see when I open the drawer; the box lives behind it.

There is a related claim about "waterproof" that is sometimes true but beside the point. Acrylic itself does not absorb water and will not swell. The glue line, the printed label, and any metal hinge on a lidded version are the parts that fail in a steamy cabinet.

## What the layout costs and how long it lasts

A cast-acrylic bin with removable dividers runs roughly $25 to $45 in the sizes that fit a vanity drawer. A PS one is $10 to $15. I resented the difference until I replaced the cheap one twice. If you expect the organiser to last three years, the cast version is cheaper per year and it never gets cloudy enough that you stop being able to see what is in the cells, which is the entire point of buying clear.

Removable dividers are worth the small premium. Collections change: a grid sized for lipsticks becomes useless the month you switch to a foundation with a wider bottle. Slotted dividers let you reconfigure in a minute. If you buy a fixed grid, buy it for the objects you own now and accept that it is a temporary solution.

Two smaller details I would check on the listing. The base should have small feet or a textured bottom, otherwise condensation under the bin makes it stick to the drawer and slide around as one unit. The top edges should be polished or rounded; a raw saw-cut edge on cheap sheet is sharp enough to nick a finger and catches on a microfibre cloth.

## When a clear bin is the wrong answer

If the drawer gets direct sun for hours, no clear plastic is a great long-term choice, and an opaque PP or bamboo insert will look better in year three. If what you are storing is mostly liquids, a lidded box beats a divided tray. If the drawer is shallower than about 2 inches, a flat non-divided liner does more than any bin with walls, and the [shelf reset](/blog/diy-bathroom-shelf-organization/) is a better use of the afternoon than shopping. And if the collection is genuinely too large for the drawer, a bin reorganises the overflow without reducing it; I had to admit that about half of what I owned was expired or duplicated before the organiser made any difference.

The bin I ended up with is 12 by 8 inches, 3 inches tall, one-piece moulded, with four removable dividers and two empty cells. It has been in the drawer for about a year and a half. The corners are still clear, and the rest of the [bathroom storage guides](/bathroom/storage/) cover the shelf and cabinet side of the same room. I wipe it with a microfibre cloth when I clean the sink, and I have stopped buying a second eyeliner because I thought I had run out. That last effect was worth more than the organising. Anything that failed the drawer's frequency test went one level further out, to the counter, and the [counter surface budget](/blog/bathroom-counter-clutter-solutions/) is how I decided which of those earned the spot.
