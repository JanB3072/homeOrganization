Nana's Kitchen sounds like one place. It is not. Search results show several independent food sites using the name, most of them built around a grandmother and her recipes. They have different authors, different cuisines and different reasons for existing.

That matters when you are looking for a recipe. A Cream Cheese Cookie from a Toronto baking blog and an Iraqi biryani from a YouTube channel are both "Nana's Kitchen" results, and they have nothing else in common. This piece maps the sites I could identify, with the source strength made clear, and then looks at what a home cook can take from them.

## The one I could verify from its own site

My Nana's Kitchen is the clearest case. Its About page is signed by Sarah Rosen, who describes herself as a young marketing professional living in Toronto, Canada. The blog is dedicated to her grandmother, Shilly Rosen, who died of cancer a couple of years before the page was written. The tagline is about the love of baking and everything else grandmothers teach you.

The blog is heavily weighted towards sweets. The About page shows 69 recipes in its categories: 25 desserts, 35 snacks, 13 mains, 10 sides and 6 appetizers. Tags include Baking, Bars, Brownies, Cake, Caramel, Chocolate, Coconut, Cream cheese, Dairy-free, Gluten-free, Lemon, Oatmeal, Vegan and Vegetarian. Named recipes visible on the site or in its links include Nana's Date Squares, Cream Cheese Cookies and Zebra Fudge Cookies.

This is a memorial project as much as a recipe archive. Rosen writes that her grandmother was a best friend and role model, that she taught her baking, and that the blog records the interests, tips and recipes she picked up from her. That context explains the tone. These are not neutral recipes. They are a way of keeping someone present.

## The family archive

Inside Nana's Kitchen is a different sort of site. The author writes that she had always loved cooking and that three grandchildren entered her life one at a time, which is when she began calling herself Nana. Search results from the site describe it as a family recipe collection.

The dishes named in indexed pages give a clear picture. Stuffed Artichokes is described as one of her grandmother's specialties, with a recipe built around five artichokes, an onion, a whole head of garlic, celery and butter. Spinach and Rice Balls is introduced as a traditional Sicilian rice ball and a version the author found in Sicily, where spinach was the most popular filling. Blog listings also include Shepard's Pie, Thanksgiving Sliders, a Thanksgiving Wrap and Twice Baked Potatoes.

I could not open the site directly because it returned a Cloudflare block page. Those dish names come from search results showing indexed pages, not from a fresh read of the site. Treat them as a map of what is there, not as a verified current menu.

The site does have a Recipe Box organised by category, including Appetizers, Beef, Brunch, Chicken, Desserts, Eggplant, Holidays, Lamb, Pasta, Pizza, Salads, Sauces and Gravies, Seafood, Sides, Soups and Veal and Pork. That structure is old-fashioned in a useful way. It is a family filing system rather than a content strategy.

## The other Nanas

Several other sites use the same name, and they pull the search results in different directions.

A Jamaican Nana's Kitchen is described in a community article as a plant-based vegan blog and recipe developer founded by Marianna. The same article quotes her talking about dehydration and the liver after carnival season, which tells you the site mixes recipes with general wellness advice.

Nana's Kitchen by Shima Ibrahim appears in a recipe aggregator with Iraqi dishes. The indexed entries include Iraqi-style biryani, described as a spiced rice dish with vegetables, shredded chicken and raisins, and Chefta, also called Kubba Halab, a stuffed rice dumpling with a spiced meat filling. That is a specific and useful body of work, and it has nothing to do with Toronto baking.

Nana's Little Kitchen is another distinct site, focused on converting Southern recipes to the Trim Healthy Mama plan. Its recipe page includes dishes such as roasted garlic green beans and tomatoes, spinach casserole and several homemade ranch dressing mixes, with low-carb and low-fat labels.

There is also a North American catering brand called Nana's Kitchen offering ready-to-finish meals for restaurants and cafés. That is food service, not a recipe blog, and it belongs in a different category entirely.

## What these sites share

The common thread is not a cuisine. It is a way of writing about food. The recipe is attached to a person, and the person is usually a grandmother or a grandmother figure. The instructions often carry a story: a dish she made without measuring spoons, a filling that reminded the author of Sicily, a cookie from a grandmother who has died.

That style has a weakness. Family recipes are written from memory, and memory is generous with quantities and vague with timings. The Stuffed Artichokes entry gives a list of ingredients, but the prose around it admits that the exact version was never written down. A cook following it needs to use judgement.

## Why the name keeps multiplying

Nana is a family word, not a brand. It is short, warm and available, so cooks around the world have used it for a blog. Search engines then gather all of them under one phrase, and the reader assumes there is one site to find.

The practical fix is to search with the full name and a subject. My Nana's Kitchen plus Cream Cheese Cookies takes you to the Toronto blog. Nana's Kitchen plus Iraqi biryani takes you to Shima Ibrahim. Nana's Kitchen plus Stuffed Artichokes leads to the family archive. That is faster than trying to work out which site is the real one, because there is no single real one.
