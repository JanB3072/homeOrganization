The part of a pair of kitchen shears that matters most is the part nobody photographs. Not the gleam of the blades, not the shape of the handles, but the pivot: the small joint where the two halves cross. Everything that makes shears pleasant or miserable to live with is decided there.

I say that because the question people ask is about jobs. Best for poultry, best for herbs. Those are fair questions, but they are second questions. The first question is whether the tool can be cleaned after the poultry job, because a hinge full of raw chicken is a different problem from a dull edge.

So this piece runs from the inside out. Pivot first, then steel, then edge, then handles. Only after that do I get to the chicken and the chives, which is where most buying guides start.

## The short answer, if you want one pair

Buy a take-apart pair of stainless steel all-purpose kitchen shears with a mild serration on the edge. That covers herbs, packaging, twine, parchment and most poultry work short of heavy bone. It is the answer I would give someone who wants one tool and does not want to think about it again.

If you regularly break down whole chickens or a Thanksgiving turkey, add poultry shears. They are a different shape of tool, heavier and built for joints. If you use soft herbs by the handful several times a week, multi-blade herb scissors are a reasonable third purchase. Neither of those changes the first recommendation. It just narrows what the first pair has to do.

## Start at the pivot

Nearly every buying guide I read while researching this makes the same point, in slightly different words. Come-apart shears, ones where the two halves separate at the pivot, are the feature to look for first. The reason is hygiene and it is unglamorous: the joint and the flat faces between the blades collect fat, protein and little fragments of whatever you cut. A pair that separates can be washed there. A sealed pair cannot.

One guide puts it plainly. A closed joint packed with chicken fat will come out of a dishwasher cycle still packed with chicken fat, only hotter. That line stuck with me because it overturns the usual shortcut. People read "dishwasher safe" and assume the machine solved the problem. The phrase is a claim about materials. It says the steel and the handle survive detergent and heat. It says nothing about whether spray reaches the inside of a screw or a rivet.

The practical test takes five seconds in a shop. Open the shears, then try to separate them. If the halves slide apart and back together with a confident click, good. If there is wobble, or if the pivot feels loose before you have cut anything with it, put them down. A loose joint only gets looser, and shears with a loose joint crush herbs instead of cutting them.

I have owned one fixed-pivot pair. It was a gift, it looked handsome, and it was fine for paper and the occasional spring onion. The first time I used it on a chicken thigh I understood what the guides were going on about. There was no way to clean the joint properly, and I never quite trusted it again. That is the whole argument for take-apart in one evening.

## Then the steel

Stainless is the sensible default. It resists rust, which matters more than edge retention in a small kitchen where a wet tool sometimes spends an hour in the sink. Buying guides repeatedly warn that cheap plated steel dulls and corrodes, particularly after acidic ingredients. Tomatoes, citrus and vinaigrettes are hard on a plating that is only a few atoms thick.

There is a trade-off at the expensive end. Some guides point to high-carbon or SK5 steel and note that harder steel holds an edge longer. They also warn that it can be more brittle, so it should not be used to open cans or cut frozen food. A harder blade is not automatically a better blade. It is a better blade for a narrower set of jobs, and a riskier one in the hands of someone who uses shears for everything. The same trade-off turns up in the [pantry container guide](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers-for-flour-and-sugar/), where the most advanced material is not always the one that survives daily use.

Blade thickness comes up less often but is worth checking. One guide suggests roughly two to three millimetres as a useful range for durability. Thicker blades feel more authoritative on a chicken and clumsier on chives. That is not a defect. It is the physical reason the two jobs pull in different directions, and it is why the one-pair answer is always a compromise.

## The edge: smooth or serrated

Micro-serration is the feature that makes a real difference on poultry skin. Slippery skin slides away from a plain edge; a fine serration grips it and cuts instead of squashing. Several guides list micro-serrated edges on their recommended pairs for exactly this reason.

A plain edge is easier to wipe clean and kinder to soft herbs. A serrated blade moving through basil leaves can tear them, which is why herb scissors use multiple fine straight blades rather than a saw pattern. If you are choosing one pair for both jobs, a mild serration is the compromise. It grips skin without shredding a chive, provided it is sharp.

Sharpness matters more than the pattern. A blunt serrated blade is worse than a sharp plain one, and neither is a pleasure. If the shears come with a sharpening note from the maker, read it. Some hardened steels are awkward to sharpen at home, which is another quiet cost of buying too much steel for your actual needs.

## Handles, springs and locks

This is where the two specialist tools diverge sharply. Poultry shears are usually heavier and often spring-loaded. The spring reopens the blades after each cut, so your hand spends its effort on closing rather than on prising the handles apart between cuts. For a whole bird, that reduces fatigue enough to notice. For a dozen chive stems, it is unnecessary machinery.

A bone notch sits near the pivot on many poultry models. It is a small curved cutout for gripping a narrow bone so the blades can get around a joint. A lock keeps the blades shut in a drawer, which is a genuine safety feature on a heavier tool.

All-purpose shears skip most of this. Their handles are lighter, the action is simpler, and the tool is easier to manoeuvre for fine work. That is why guides such as Wüsthof's recommend all-purpose shears for herbs, packaging and parchment, and poultry shears for whole birds, fat and bone. The distinction is not marketing. You can feel it the first time you try to trim parsley with a spring-loaded poultry pair.

## Now the job: a chicken on the board

Spatchcocking is the job that converts people. You turn a chicken breast down, find the backbone and cut along each side of it with shears. A knife can do this, but it needs a different grip and a stable board, and the bone never quite behaves. Shears follow the bone rather than trying to force through it.

A take-apart all-purpose pair will manage a medium chicken. The blades will be working hard, and you may need to reposition more than once. Poultry shears make the same job shorter, because the curve and the weight are designed to get under the rib cage and around the joint. If you do this monthly, the all-purpose pair is fine. If you do it weekly, the specialist earns its drawer space.

Skin, fat and small cartilage are where the serration helps. A plain edge tends to slide off wet skin, and the additional hand pressure needed to compensate is exactly what causes the blade to twist at the pivot. A serrated edge bites on the first pass.

Afterwards, the cleaning is the real test. Separate the halves, wash the faces and the joint, dry them properly. A damp hinge is how a good pair starts to rust, whatever the steel grade on the label.

## Now the job: a bunch of herbs

Herbs ask for the opposite. You want a fine point, a clean edge and enough control to snip directly over a pot without sending stems across the worktop. A heavy poultry blade demands more force than a chive needs, and the spring makes delicate work feel oddly resistant.

Multi-blade herb scissors are the specialist here. Several parallel blades cut at once, so a bunch of chives becomes an even sprinkle in a single pass. They are genuinely good at that one thing. They are also awkward to clean, because the spaces between the blades collect plant matter and need a brush. If you use them twice a year, they are a novelty. If you finish a bunch of parsley every week, they save real time.

For everything else, the tip of a standard pair is hard to beat. Snipping herbs into a bowl is faster than washing a board, and it leaves the knife clean for the next job. This is one of those small habits that changes how often you cook with fresh herbs at all. A pair of shears within reach makes the extra handful more likely.

That availability is worth more than a small difference in blade quality. Shears in a drawer under a stack of tea towels are shears you do not use. I keep mine in a shallow bin near the prep area, alongside the peelers, where I can grab them without moving anything else. ## One pair or two?

The honest answer depends on frequency, not on budget. Most kitchens need one good take-apart all-purpose pair. It handles the chicken once a month, the herbs every other day, and the packaging that no knife wants to touch. Buying three specialist tools at the start usually means three tools that are never quite where you need them.

Add poultry shears when breaking down birds becomes routine. Add herb scissors when a multi-blade tool would genuinely be used weekly, not because the photograph is satisfying. A small kitchen has a fixed amount of drawer real estate, and every tool that earns a place has to displace something else. The [twenty-minute rule](/blog/20-20-decluttering-rule-explained/) is a fair way to decide what goes.

There is also an argument for two all-purpose pairs in a household that cooks together. One lives near the prep zone and one near the stove. It sounds excessive until someone is holding a hot pan and asking the other person to pass the shears from three cupboards away. For that household, a second cheap take-apart pair is a better purchase than a premium single one.

## The cleaning that decides whether it lasts

Raw poultry is the reason all of this matters. Separate the shears, wash the blade faces and the pivot, and dry them before they go back together. A quick rinse under a tap is not cleaning. It is a brief visit from water.

If the shears do not come apart, hand washing around the joint is the only reliable method, and it is never as good. This is the point at which the phrase "dishwasher safe" becomes a slight trap. It is true and incomplete, like saying a car is washable while ignoring the mud in the wheel arches. The [fridge cleaning walkthrough](/blog/aesthetic-fridge-organization-tips/) is built on the same idea: a surface that looks clean and a surface that is clean are not always the same thing.

Drying matters as much as washing. A hinge left wet overnight can develop a faint orange stain at the screw, and once that starts, the joint never moves quite as smoothly again. A thumbnail-sized patch of rust is not a reason to throw the tool away, but it is a reason to change the habit.

Oil is optional. A drop of food-safe mineral oil on the pivot once or twice a year keeps a good pair moving freely. Cooking oil works in a pinch but can go sticky over time, which attracts more residue than it prevents. If you would not put it on a salad, think twice about putting it in the joint.

## Where the tool lives

A small kitchen rewards tools that are visible. Shears belong in a bin or a narrow slot within reach of the main prep surface, not in a cutlery drawer where they hide under skewers and takeaway forks. A shallow tray keeps them upright and makes the pivot visible, which also reminds you to clean it.

The [pull-out shelf guide](/blog/diy-pull-out-shelves-for-kitchen-cabinets/) is useful if your prep tools currently live in a deep lower cabinet. A shallow tray that slides out beats kneeling and rummaging every time you need the shears. The [pan organizer guide](/blog/best-pan-organizer-rack-for-kitchen-cabinet/) makes a similar argument for awkward flat items, and the [baking sheet rack](/blog/kitchen-baking-sheet-organizer-rack-vertical-cabinet/) handles the things that refuse to stack.

If you keep a herb scissors as well, store it in the same bin but with the blade guard on. Multi-blade tools are surprisingly good at catching on tea towels. Labelling the bin is unnecessary in a two-person home and very helpful in a shared one. ## What I would skip

Skip the giant set. A block of eight scissors with colour-coded handles looks like a solution and is usually seven tools you already own in slightly different forms. The [kitchen hub](/kitchen/) groups the rest of the room by zone, and it is easier to apply that thinking to tools than to buy a bundle and hope.

Skip anything whose advertising leads with titanium. Coatings wear, and a worn coating on a blade is harder to care for than a plain stainless surface. If a guide cannot tell you what the steel is and whether the pivot separates, it is selling you the finish.

Skip the assumption that expensive means fewer decisions. A premium pair still has to be washed, dried and stored where you can reach it. The [whole-home organization system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) makes the same point at room scale: a better object in a bad location stays a bad object.

And skip the idea that there is one perfect pair for poultry and herbs. There is a good compromise, and there are two good specialists. Choose based on what you actually cut this month, not on what you might cook next year.
