A mirror is the only thing in the bathroom you install once and consult twice a day, in a rush, from the same spot. That is why placement errors feel worse than they look on paper: mount it two inches high and every shave and every eyeliner session happens in the top third of the glass, for a decade. The two classic mistakes are both height errors, one mirror hung so low the glass shows the chest and the tap, one hung so high it frames the ceiling. Both are fixable on paper in five minutes, before the drill exists.

The usual guides give you a number, often 5 to 10 inches above the counter, and stop. The number is a symptom. What follows is the geometry that produces it, then the decisions that are genuinely special to a double vanity, then light and hardware. My own bathroom went through this in October, two mirrors over a 72-inch vanity, and one of the numbers below comes from having hung the first mirror half an inch proud of its twin and living with it for a weekend.

## What a mirror actually has to show

Side view, one basin, not to scale:

```
              E  eye, ~62 in standing
              |\
   mirror     | \
   +------+   |  \   ray out, slightly downward
   |      |   |   \  hits the glass and
   |   M  |<--+    > reflects back down
   |      |   |   /  at the same angle,
   +------+   |  /   reaching chin and jaw
              | /
   -----------+----------- countertop, 36 in
```

The law doing all the work here is that light leaves at the angle it arrives. Stand at arm's length from a mirror and look at your own chin: the ray travels down from your eye, bounces, and the reflected ray climbs the same angle. The consequence is the part almost nobody believes the first time they hear it. To see your whole face, hairline to chin, the mirror only needs to cover half of that distance, positioned so its top edge sits halfway between your eyes and your hairline and its bottom edge halfway between your eyes and your chin.

Run the numbers for a real face. Hairline to chin runs 8 to 9 inches for most adults, so a pane roughly 5 inches tall, correctly placed, shows the entire face. Add head shape, because a haircut is a mirror's second job, and glasses wearers look upward to position frames, and the practical pane grows to 14 to 18 inches. Height of placement matters more than height of glass.

The other variable is the eye. Standing eye heights scatter from about 56 inches for shorter adults to 66 and above for tall ones, with the population centres around 62 for women and 66 for men. A shared vanity has no average user in the morning; it has two specific ones. Set the mirror to the shorter user's eyes, and the taller partner looks down an inch rather than crouching, which is the cheaper concession. In my house that put the centres at 60 inches to the mirror's midline, bottom edge 7 inches off a 36-inch countertop, which lands exactly where the generic 5-to-10-inch advice points, except now the reason is visible: the bottom edge tracks the counter for splash and style reasons, the centre tracks the eye, and the eye wins whenever the two disagree.

For a seated user the geometry changes completely, eye height around 44 to 48 inches, and no amount of compromise serves both. That is a two-mirror decision: one at standing height, one lower, rather than one pane serving nobody.

## Two mirrors, and the wall between them

A double vanity adds one geometric problem a single mirror never has: each user appears, in fragments, in the other's glass. Frame two mirrors closer than about 4 inches apart and the edge of your own reflection sits a few degrees of eye movement from a slice of your partner. Beyond the mild horror of it at 6 a.m., it also doubles the visual noise, because two reflective panes near each other multiply everything they see, including the overhead light and the shower door.

Spacing therefore follows plumbing, not wall. Centre each mirror over its basin, which on most double vanities means centres 30 to 36 inches apart. With typical 24-inch mirrors that leaves 6 to 12 inches of wall between frames, right in the comfortable band, and it also leaves the sconce positions free, since the wall between the mirrors is exactly where one shared light or two recessed boxes tend to go.

The alternative, one wide mirror spanning both basins, is a styling choice with real advantages and one hidden cost. Laid out honestly:

| | Two separate mirrors | One wide mirror |
|---|---|---|
| Alignment tolerance | Each hangs on its own centre; a 1/4-inch mismatch between them is invisible | Every level error shows as a wavy horizon across the whole wall |
| Cross-reflection | Possible if frames sit under 4 inches apart | None, by definition |
| Room feel | Breaks the wall, reads narrower | Doubles the light and the width of a small room |
| Per-user fit | Height and angle settable per person | One compromise height for all |
| Cost and install | Two smaller panes, two hanging operations | One heavy pane, usually 60 to 80 pounds |

I chose two, and the deciding argument was the table's second row turned around: with two, my half-inch height error between the frames, which I discovered with a laser level an hour after the silicone cured, simply does not read. On a single pane it would have been the first thing every guest noticed.

## Weight, wall and hardware

A 30 by 36 inch quarter-inch mirror weighs around 30 pounds, and the arithmetic scales fast, about 1.2 pounds per square foot per millimetre of thickness, so a 60-inch single pane in 6 mm glass approaches 70 pounds. Unlike a wall basket, whose load leans outward, a mirror hung flat against the wall puts nearly all of that into pure downward shear, which is the friendliest direction for a fixing to point. Drywall forgives shear far more than tension. The [wall desk arithmetic](/blog/vertical-wall-desk-space-saving-home-office/) is the hard version of this; a mirror is the easy case, but easy does not mean studless, and 30 pounds on two plastic plugs in humid drywall is exactly the combination that lets go after a few seasons.

Hardware choice decides how much precision the install demands. Two fixed D-rings on screws are the cheapest and the most brutal: both hooks must sit level to within about a millimetre of each other or the pane leans, and leaning a rigid pane of glass on two point hooks also loads the glass in a way it will remember. A wall rail or French cleat under the top edge, or four padded clips, turns the job into one level line instead of two perfect points, which is why frameless mirrors in hotels hang that way. My two mirrors went up on D-rings because the frames dictated it, and getting the pair level consumed more of the afternoon than the drilling did.

Two safety notes that belong in any mirror guide. First, a mirror in a bathroom should have a safety film on the back, the same laminated backing that tempered glass under ANSI Z97.1 carries, so that a crack stays in the pane instead of falling into a tub in shards. Most decent mirrors have it; the cheapest unbranded ones do not, and it is worth asking before buying. Second, every hole in tile follows the routine from the [wire basket wall guide](/blog/wire-basket-wall-storage-for-toiletries/), masking tape, carbide bit, no hammer through the glaze, silicone into the hole, because a mirror hole is bigger and closer to eye level than a basket screw, and a chipped crescent in a shower wall tile is bad, while the same chip at face height in front of a mirror is all you will ever see.

## Light before glass

No mirror corrects bad light, it only shows it. The single overhead fixture that builders love throws light down the front of the face, and the geometry guarantees shadows: brow ridges shadow the eyes, the chin shadows the jaw, and shaving or applying eyeliner happens inside those shadows. The fix is direction, not brightness. Light belongs beside the glass at eye height, roughly 60 to 66 inches on the wall or on the mirror's surface itself, arriving from the sides the way a window does. Two sconces, one flanking each basin, or an LED mirror with light strips down both edges, reproduce that geometry; a bar light above the mirror is the halfway house, better than a ceiling can, worse than either.

Colour is the other half, and the [vanity lighting guide](/blog/best-makeup-vanity-table-with-lights-storage/) already makes the quantitative case for CRI above 90 and a sane temperature, so the short version here is placement-specific: cool north-daylight bulbs in a warm-toned bathroom make everyone look the way they will look nowhere else, and the makeup applied under that light goes on wrong for the rest of the day. Around 3000 K, CRI 90+, and consistency between the two mirrors is the target. Consistency matters more than it sounds, because at a double vanity one person is often shaving under one mirror while the other checks colour under the other, and a 500 K mismatch between the two panes turns that into an argument about which room's mirror is lying.

One wet-room note: any fixture inside the shower's splash zone wants a damp-rated or wet-rated marking, and a mirror edge inside that zone wants to be glass with a sealed edge, because of what the humidity does to mirror backs, which is the next section.

## What humidity does to the mirror itself

A mirror is glass with a silver layer behind it, then a copper coat, then paint. At an unsealed edge, water vapour works its way in and attacks the silver, and the result is the familiar black stipple creeping inward from the corners, called edge rot or desilvering. It is not dirt and it is not reversible. Cheap mirrors in steamy rooms start showing it within a few years, always from the edges and corners first, which is why the placement advice includes keeping the mirror out of the direct shower spray, and why a bead of neutral-cure silicone around an exposed glass edge is cheap insurance. A mirror that sits close above a full-width basin also takes a splash line along its bottom edge; 5 to 8 inches of counter clearance keeps the glass out of the wettest band.

Fog is the same water in a friendlier form, and there are three honest ways to fight it. A heated demister pad behind the glass, 40 to 80 watts, warms the pane above the dew point and keeps a clear window for as long as it is on; it is the only method that works during the shower rather than after. Anti-fog sprays work by changing the surface tension so droplets spread into a film, and they need reapplying every week or two in a daily-shower bathroom. The fan matters more than either: a bathroom fan sized to the room, run for 20 minutes after the shower, drops the humidity that fogs the glass and the humidity that eats the silver. The ventilation math in the [floating vanity styling guide](/blog/how-to-style-a-floating-vanity/) applies to mirrors exactly as it does to cabinetry.

## Mounting without the wobble

The install sequence that keeps a pair of mirrors reading as a pair: mark the centre line over each basin with the vanity's plumbing as the reference, not the wall, because walls run out of true more often than plumbers do; mark both mirror centres at the chosen eye height; snap or draw one horizontal line across both positions and hang to the line, not to each mirror's frame, since frame mouldings vary by a millimetre or two between two identical-looking boxes; hold each pane to the line, level it, mark the holes, drill, and hang. A second pair of hands is not optional above 30 pounds, both for the lift and because someone needs to read the level while the other adjusts.

The tolerance worth knowing: a single mirror hung 1/4 inch off level is noticeable from across the room, against a door frame or a tiled wall that supplies the reference. Two mirrors hung 1/4 inch apart in height are almost invisible, because nothing between them gives the eye a shared edge. That asymmetry is the quiet argument for the two-pane layout in anything other than a perfectly built wall, and it is why I stopped chasing the last eighth of an inch on my second mirror and let the silicone cure.

## Renters and small rooms

A mirror is also one of the few bathroom upgrades a rental can absorb without drilling, though the honest options narrow. A leaning floor mirror beside a vanity solves the height problem entirely and adds a full-length view the wall never provided, at the cost of floor area that a [small bathroom may not have](/blog/bathroom-counter-clutter-solutions/). Adhesive mirror hangers exist and hold small panes, but the same humidity that walked my suction basket off the tile in eleven days works on every adhesive bond, and a 20-pound pane is not something to learn that lesson with. For a child's bathroom, the same geometry runs in miniature: eye height around 45 to 50 inches, mirror centre matching it, which usually means a second, smaller mirror hung below the adult one rather than moving the main glass down and back up.

The [double vanity drawers](/blog/bathroom-vanity-drawer-organization-tips/) and the rest of the [vanity guides](/bathroom/vanity/) cover what happens on the counter and below it. On the wall, the summary survives compression better than most: mirror centre at the shorter user's eyes, basins set the spacing, light beside the glass rather than above it, hardware into something structural, silicone in every hole, and one horizontal line shared by both panes. For why the light belongs beside the glass and not above it, the [vanity lighting rules](/blog/bathroom-vanity-lighting-placement-rules/) do the arithmetic that this guide's side-light advice only asserts.
