Every October, someone opens a box of winter clothes and finds a smell, a stain, or a hole that wasn't there in April. The gear didn't fail over winter. It failed over the four months it sat in a hot attic in a sealed plastic bag. Learning how to store winter clothes in summer is mostly about understanding what actually damages fabric in storage, because the damage is done by five specific enemies, and each has a specific countermeasure.

This is the full system: the enemies first, then the ten-minute ritual that handles all of them at once.

## The five enemies (and what actually works against each)

1. Moisture and mold. Humidity above about 60 percent, trapped against fabric, grows mildew in weeks, and mildew smell is nearly permanent. The countermeasure is dry clothes into breathable containers, stored off concrete floors (concrete wicks moisture), with a desiccant packet in each bin in humid climates.

2. Clothes moths. The counterintuitive one: larvae eat the stuff on the fabric, body oils, sweat, food traces, more than the wool itself. A clean sweater is a poor meal; a worn-three-times sweater is a buffet. This is why "wash everything before storage" is the entire moth strategy, and cedar is the garnish. Cedar blocks smell nice and repel weakly; their oil fades within a few months, and research consistently ranks them below sealed storage in effectiveness. Use them as a scent layer inside a sealed or zipped system, never as the system.

3. Dust. Harmless but grim. Dust settling into knits for a season means a wash cycle and some fiber wear at unpacking. Any lidded bin or zipped bag solves it; open shelving in a guest-room closet does not.

4. Heat. Attics and garages hit 90°F-plus in summer, and sustained heat embrittles elastic, weakens fibers, and sets any invisible stains permanently. The countermeasure is location: interior closet, under-bed, anything that stays near living temperatures.

5. Creasing and compression. Six months of a sharp fold embosses a permanent line into wool; six months in a vacuum bag flattens down insulation so badly it may never fully re-loft. Knits fold fine (fold along existing seams, refold halfway through summer if you're near them); coats hang.

## The container decision, by fabric

The single most common storage mistake is one container for everything. Fabric type decides the box:

| Fabric | Container | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Wool, cashmere, angora | Breathable cotton zip bags | Must breathe; sealed plastic traps humidity and grows mildew |
| Down jackets & parkas | Hang in breathable garment bags | Vacuum or compression kills the loft that insulates |
| Synthetics (fleece, poly base layers) | Sealed plastic bins or vacuum bags | Immune to the moisture problem; vacuum is fine |
| Hats, gloves, scarves | One labeled bin per category | Small items vanish; one bin each beats six loose |
| Boots | Boot shapers or stuffed with paper, in open bins | Shaft creases set permanently if collapsed |

One nuance on plastic: a lidded bin with the lid unsnapped on one corner is a legitimate middle ground in dry climates, mostly dust-proof, slightly breathing. A fully gasket-sealed tub of wool in a humid summer is the mildew recipe.

## The ten-minute June ritual

The whole process fits in one session per category, and it's a genuine HowTo. Here are the steps:

1. Wash everything, even what smells clean. Machine or dry-clean per labels; the point is removing the oils larvae eat. This is the step people skip and the step that decides whether moths arrive.

2. Repair before packing. The loose button and the small tear become archaeological finds in October. Ten minutes of needle work now, or you'll forget where the tear was.

3. Sort by fabric and by frequency. Wool with wool, synthetics with synthetics, and a separate "definitely next winter" pile from the "maybe" pile. The maybe pile is where the [one-year rule from moving decluttering](/blog/how-to-declutter-before-moving-checklist/) applies, and June is its natural audition date.

4. Pack by the container table above. Fold knits along seams with tissue paper at the fold for delicate cashmere; hang coats on wide shaped hangers in breathable bags; boots get stuffed and binned.

5. Choose the location last. Interior closet floor, under the bed, or the upper shelf of a bedroom closet. The [second rod and upper zones of a small closet](/blog/vertical-closet-system-maximize-small-wardrobe/) are exactly where seasonal gear lives double-duty. Cool, dark, dry, off concrete.

6. Add the passive protectors. Desiccant packets in humid climates, cedar or lavender as the scent layer, and one labeled inventory list taped inside the bin lid, because October-you does not remember what June-you packed.

7. Set the October check. A calendar note to open each container: ten minutes to air everything, check for moth activity or must, sand or replace cedar, and refold anything that's been folded four months.

## The unpack side (half the system)

Storage quality shows up at unpacking, so build the ritual to end well. Air everything for a few hours before it re-enters the wardrobe, re-wash anything that smells faintly of storage (sun and fresh air fix most of it), and run the lint brush over wool before its first wear. If anything shows moth damage, act on the whole container, not the one sweater, because larvae travel, and the neighbors in that bag are suspects too.

## What a moth problem actually looks like

The October check is only useful if you know what you're looking for, and moth damage is shy. It hides where fabric folds against fabric:

- Irregular holes at collars, cuffs, and underarms. Larval feeding happens where the garment creases, so the first holes are rarely in the middle of the back.
- Sand-like grit in fold lines: larval droppings, usually tan or brown.
- Webbing or tiny casings, rice-grain-sized tubes that the larvae drag around like sleeping bags.
- Bald patches on knits rather than through-holes; surface grazing comes first.

The response rule: if one sweater shows damage, the entire container gets treated, not just the suspect. Freeze the affected batch for 72 hours (kills larvae at all stages), wash everything else in the bin, and inspect the neighboring containers. Larvae travel slowly but they do travel.

## Where to store: the location ranking

The container decision gets all the attention, but location does more damage or less. Best to worst, from real summers:

1. Interior bedroom closet (floor or top shelf): living temperatures, dark, dry, the default winner.
2. Under the bed in low flat bins: same climate, invisible, and out of the way; the only trade is dust, which lidded bins handle.
3. Guest room or hall closet: fine if it stays cool and doesn't share walls with the water heater.
4. Basement: only with dehumidified air and shelving, because concrete floors wick moisture straight into boxes.
5. Garage and attic: last resort, honestly closer to "don't." A summer up there costs elastic, adhesives in down baffles, and any starch or sizing in the fabric.

One exception worth paying for: if the only available space is a garage, a small climate-controlled storage unit beats it, because the unit's rent is less than one replaced down parka.

## Leather, fur, and the specialty cases

The fabric table covers cloth; the specialty materials have their own rules and their own failure modes:

- Leather jackets: condition before storage, then hang on a wide hanger in a breathable bag, never plastic, which dries the hide and fails long-term without conditioning. Leather wants to breathe and be slightly humid-proofed, which is the opposite of wool's needs.
- Fur and structured wool coats: professional storage is genuinely worth considering for real fur; home storage means cool, wide hangers, breathable cotton, and absolutely no compression.
- Boots with leather soles: clean, condition, stuff with acid-free paper, and store upright, because collapsed shafts crease permanently within weeks.
- Technical gear (ski pants, shells): zip everything closed so zippers don't set in a bent position, and keep them out of vacuum bags, because membranes and seam tape dislike heat and compression.

## Three storage mistakes that cost gear

The sealed attic tub. Wool in a gasket-sealed bin in a 95°F attic gets humidity, heat, and darkness, three enemies at once. Location first, container second.

Vacuum-bagging the down. It saves space now and destroys insulation permanently. Down hangs or breathes, always.

Treating cedar as armor. Cedar smells like protection and performs like a perfume. Clean clothes plus sealed or zipped bags are the protection; cedar is the air freshener on top.

## The whole point

How to store winter clothes in summer reduces to three decisions. Clean (wash everything, because larvae eat stains), breathe (cotton bags for naturals, plastic only for synthetics, down hangs), and location (cool, dark, dry, off concrete). Add cedar for scent, a desiccant for humidity, and a ten-minute October check, and the gear that goes into storage in June comes out in October exactly as it went in, which is the entire job. The system costs almost nothing; the coats it protects cost plenty.
