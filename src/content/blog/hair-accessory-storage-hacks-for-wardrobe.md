At the worst point there were forty-one hair ties in the house and I wore three of them. They lived in a deep drawer, mixed with claw clips and a tangle of ribbon, and finding a match in the morning took longer than tying the ponytail. The count was not the problem. The drawer was one container for four kinds of object that break in four different ways.

What follows is the version that has held for two years, and it starts with admitting that a scrunchie and a claw clip have nothing in common except the shelf they sit on.

## Four shapes, and why one bin cannot serve them

Hair accessories divide by shape, and the shape decides what destroys them.

| Shape | Examples | Failure mode | Storage answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ring | Scrunchie, elastic, hair tie | Elastic fatigue from tension | Loose in a shallow tray |
| Bar | Bobby pin, long clip, ribbon tie | Bending, and losing its partner | Flat, joined to its pair |
| Plate | Bow, flat barrette, headband | Shape memory, flattened curve | Supported or hanging |
| Toothed | Claw clip, fine comb | Snagging everything soft nearby | Own lidded compartment |

Once the four piles existed on the bed, the storage question answered itself. The rings wanted a bowl, the bars wanted a flat surface, the plates wanted to hang, and the toothed pile wanted to be alone. Every "hair accessory hack" I had tried before this failed because it treated all four as one category.

The rest of this page is the reasoning behind each answer, because the reasons are where the money is saved.

## Elastic dies from stretch, not from use

A hair tie is a loop of elastic, and elastic has a property called recovery, which is how far it returns after being stretched.

Recovery degrades under sustained tension much faster than under repeated short use. Stretch a scrunchie over a bottle neck, a hook or the corner of a mirror for six months and it comes back noticeably looser, having done no work at all. Leave the same scrunchie in a pile and it will still hold a ponytail years later.

That single fact rules out most of the storage products sold for this: the pretty hook rails, the bottle-top dispensers, the carabiner clips. They all work by keeping the elastic stretched, which is the one thing to avoid.

Two exceptions are worth knowing. Loose elastics in a shallow tray do not tangle if the tray is at least twice the diameter of the largest scrunchie, so a 15 cm bowl holds about a dozen without a single knot. And a scrunchie that has gone slightly slack can often be recovered by soaking in water at 60 to 70°C for ten minutes, which relaxes the fibre; this works on natural rubber blends and does little for fully synthetic ones, but it costs nothing to try before throwing a good fabric scrunchie away.

The aesthetic problem is separate. Dark elastics in a dark container are invisible, and a container of invisible things gets topped up rather than used. A clear jar with the elastics piled loosely, or a tray with a pale interior, is the difference between knowing what you own and buying a fourth multipack.

## Pairs have to be joined, not sorted

Bobby pins, small snap clips and matching barrettes share a failure that has nothing to do with storage material: they separate.

Two loose pins in a tray become one pin in the tray and one pin in a coat pocket, and eventually neither. The fix is mechanical, not organisational. A thin magnetic strip on the inside of a drawer or the back of a cabinet door holds pins in rows of pairs, and pulling one pair off is a single gesture. Snap clips can be clipped onto each other, one hooking the other, so the pair moves as a unit.

The same logic applies to earrings, which is why the [velvet insert layout](/blog/velvet-jewelry-drawer-inserts-custom-fit/) puts a pair in one cell rather than two cells side by side. The physical constraint is the same: anything used in twos needs to be stored as a two.

The one thing to avoid is the small open dish. It looks right on a shelf and it becomes a place where pins go to be lost individually, because nothing holds them together and nothing stops them sliding out the back when the dish is picked up.

## The depth ceiling is about eight centimetres

Retrieval is a hand operation, and a hand can only reach so far into a container before the fingertips stop being useful.

The practical ceiling is about two finger joints, which is 7 to 8 cm. Below that, you can see and touch the bottom at the same time. Above it, the container becomes a dig, and a dig in the morning is what makes people give up and buy a new pack.

| Depth | What it does | What breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Under 5 cm | Full visibility, easy pinch grip | Small items slide over the edge |
| 5 to 8 cm | The usable band | Nothing, if the tray is not overfilled |
| 8 to 12 cm | Fingers reach but cannot see | Bottom layer becomes storage, not rotation |
| Over 12 cm | Retrieval by feel | Everything at the bottom stops existing |

That is why jars work for elastics and bins work for headbands, and why the same jar is wrong for bobby pins. The elastics are large enough to be found by touch at any depth; pins are not.

The other half of the depth question is shelf height. A wardrobe shelf gap of 30 cm with a 25 cm bin in it wastes the top, and stacking a second bin on top pushes the bottom one past the reach ceiling. The working arrangement is one shallow tray per shelf gap, with visible air above it.

## Anything with teeth has to be isolated

A claw clip has five to eight teeth with a slight inward curve, and those teeth are hooks. In a bin shared with fabric scrunchies, the clip will find a scrunchie within a day and pull a thread.

The same applies to fine tooth combs, which is why they should not live in the same drawer as tights or knitwear. The damage is usually not to the clip but to the soft thing next to it, and it appears as a snag in a place with no obvious cause.

A lidded compartment solves it, and a lid matters more than the material. An open-topped box in a shared drawer will still catch the neighbouring item when the drawer is pushed shut.

The [accessory tray layout](/blog/closet-accessory-tray-configuration/) makes the same argument for other small hardware, and the [hat storage piece](/blog/hat-storage-ideas-for-small-closet/) applies it to brims and pins: the object that can catch something should never share a container with the object that can be caught.

## Plates have shape memory, and it is permanent

Bows, flat barrettes and headbands are the category where storage determines whether the item still works.

A headband has an arc of 12 to 14 cm diameter that is set at manufacture, and it holds that arc by being rigid along one axis only. Laid flat under other items, the arc flattens; a flattened headband no longer grips, and the fix is not available because bending it back overcorrects.

A bow is worse. Ribbon has a memory of the shape it was tied into, and a bow pressed under a bin for months comes out with its loops flattened and its tails creased. No steaming restores the crisp fold, because the crease is in the weave.

The storage answers are two. For headbands, a curved support, which can be as simple as a wide hook, a rolled towel inside a box, or a purpose made arc. For bows and ribbon ties, hanging, which is why the [ribbon hanger](/blog/best-scarf-organizer-hanger-for-closet/) in the photograph works: it suspends each bow by its knot so nothing presses on the loops.

The catch is that the hanger only solves the plate category. Hanging a scrunchie on it stretches elastic, and hanging a claw clip on it snags the fabric. One hanger, one category, no exceptions. The same restraint appears in the [belt storage comparison](/blog/how-to-store-belts-in-closet/), where the rack that holds a dozen belts is the wrong home for a coiled leather one.

## The second set that lives in a bag

There is always a second collection, and it is not in the wardrobe. Four elastics, two pins and one clip migrate into whatever bag is in use, and if that set has no home it becomes the reason the wardrobe set looks short.

A bag needs a container as much as a shelf does, and the constraint inverts. Depth is not the issue, because a bag is shallow; pressure is. A hard box in a handbag presses against the lining and the contents, and a hair clip in a hard box under a laptop will crack. The right container is a thin drawstring pouch, which weighs nothing, conforms to whatever space is left, and can be found by feel in the dark.

The count wants to stay small, at about seven pieces. A bag set that grows past ten stops being a travel kit and becomes a second drawer, at which point the same elastic fatigue and the same invisible dark scrunchies apply, with none of the visibility that fixed the wardrobe.

Transferring the pouch between bags is a ten second habit and the only maintenance required. The rule is the same as on the shelf, one container for one category and a fixed place it returns to, just at the scale of a handbag.

## What actually sits on the shelf now

Three clear bins, one jar, one hanger, one lidded box. That is the whole system, and it costs less than the deep drawer insert I bought first.

The bins are 7 cm deep, which is inside the reach ceiling, and they hold headbands upright against the back wall so the arcs are supported rather than squashed. The jar takes elastics loose, with no tension anywhere in the system. The hanger carries bows and nothing else. The lidded box holds the claw clips.

The counts settled at about ten pieces after the first clear out, from a starting point of forty-one. Nothing was thrown away for being worn out; most of it had simply never been visible. Four weeks in, the test was whether the morning retrieval still took one gesture. It did, and it has kept doing so, which is the only measure that matters for a container this small. Nothing here is a trick. It is four categories, four depths, and one rule about tension.
