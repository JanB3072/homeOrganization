The first time I did this by the book, the drawer would not close. Every pair was folded into a neat little packet and stood on its end, exactly as described, and the packets slowly leaned into each other until the whole row was a wedge that lifted the drawer front by a centimetre.

The method was not wrong. It was designed for fabrics that stand up on their own, and the things in this drawer are the opposite of that. What follows is the version that has held for two years, and the three places where the original needs adjusting.

## Vertical folding assumes a fabric that stands

The whole point of the vertical file is that you see every item at once instead of lifting a stack to find the one at the bottom. That works beautifully on cotton T-shirts, denim and towels, because a folded edge of those fabrics has enough body to stay upright under its own weight.

Underwear is thin, stretchy and low in body. A folded packet of it has no structural edge, so it collapses sideways within a day and takes its neighbours with it. The fix is not to abandon the idea but to change the geometry: roll instead of fold, and stand the roll on its circular end, which does have structure because the layers press against each other.

| Fabric | Fold and stand? | Why | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cotton jersey | Yes | Has body, holds an edge | Fold, stand on edge |
| Modal, microfibre | No | Slippery, collapses | Roll, stand on end |
| Lace | No | Catches, no structure | Roll loosely, never compress |
| Bonded knit | No | No seam to hold a fold | Roll, stand on end |
| Cotton socks | Yes | Rib holds shape | Roll, stand on end |

Same principle, different shape. The visual result is what the method is actually after.

## Underwear: roll it, and measure the roll

A rolled pair comes out at 6 to 8 centimetres across and 9 to 12 long, depending on size and fabric. That number matters more than any folding technique.

A standard dresser drawer is 12 to 15 centimetres deep. A roll standing on its end needs 9 to 12 of those centimetres, which leaves 3 to 6 above it doing nothing. Laying the rolls flat uses more of the depth and fits more per drawer, at the cost of having to lift the top layer.

My compromise is a shallow top drawer with rolls standing, and the deeper second drawer with rolls laid in a single layer, side by side, so nothing is under anything. Two drawers, both fully visible, neither one a stack.

The roll itself should be loose. Compressing a roll to make it fit a cell flattens the elastic along the fold, and it never quite comes back. If a roll will not fit, the cell is the wrong size, not the roll.

## Bras: the one thing you never fold

Bras are the opposite problem. They cannot be folded, cannot be rolled, and cannot carry weight, so the vertical method does not apply at all.

What works is nesting. One cup inside another, two or three deep, laid so the cups keep their shape and the bands are not crushed under the stack. More than three deep and the bottom cups start to flatten at the apex, which is permanent.

The storage position matters as much as the method. A bra stack should sit in its own cell, upright, resting on the bands rather than on the cups. Stored flat with weight on top, the wires bend and the cups crease, and a bent wire is the end of that bra.

A dedicated cell of about 16 by 18 centimetres holds a stack of three in most sizes. Anything smaller and you will be forcing cups into a space that deforms them.

## Socks: the part the book gets exactly right

The advice not to roll socks by their cuffs is correct, and it is the one instruction in the method I would defend without qualification. Turning the cuff inside out and wrapping it around the bundle stretches the elastic every single time, and elastic fatigue is what ends a sock's life while the sole still has years in it.

Rolling from the toe up, then standing the roll on end, keeps the pair together without stretching anything. It also makes the drawer look like the photograph, which is not nothing.

The other sock rule worth keeping: pairs live together as pairs. A drawer of orphaned socks is a drawer where you own fifty socks and can wear none of them.

## The grid has to match the contents, not the drawer

This is where most dividers fail. A uniform grid is a manufacturing convenience, and it forces the largest item to set the scale for everything else.

| Cell size | Holds | How many per 40 cm drawer |
|---|---|---|
| 8 by 8 cm | One rolled underwear | 4 across, 5 rows |
| 8 by 10 cm | One rolled sock pair | 4 across, 5 rows |
| 16 by 18 cm | Three bras, nested | 2 across, 2 rows |
| 10 by 10 cm | Spare, or a new purchase | Flexible |

The arithmetic to run before buying anything is the column count. Measure the drawer interior width, subtract the divider walls, divide by the cell width, round down. A 40 centimetre drawer with 8 centimetre cells and 3 millimetre walls gives four columns and a small remainder, and four columns of five rows is twenty rolled pairs, which is more than most people own.

Buying the grid first and making the underwear fit is how drawers end up with cells that hold two pairs each and a lid that will not shut.

## The damp problem nobody mentions

Drawer bottoms are the coolest and dampest part of a closet, and that matters more for underwear than for anything else on the rail.

Two things follow. Use a breathable divider, because a sealed plastic tray holds the air against the fabric and a drawer closed for eight hours a day is a small humid box. And do not put a sachet in and forget it, because a sachet stops working after about six weeks and then just occupies a cell.

I leave the drawer open for an hour after putting laundry away, which sounds fussy and takes no effort, because the fabric goes in warm from the dryer and the moisture has to go somewhere.

## Two weeks, and what the number does

Once everything is visible, the count becomes obvious in a way it never was in a stack. Mine landed at twenty-one pairs, which is two weeks plus laundry slack, and the surplus that came out of the drawer in year one was about a third of what was in there.

That is the same arithmetic the [colour order](/blog/hanging-clothes-organization-by-color-guide/) does for the rail and the [capsule count](/blog/capsule-wardrobe-closet-layout-design/) does for the wardrobe: visibility produces a number, and the number is easier to argue with than a feeling.

Nothing about it needs a special drawer. A [mesh divider insert](/blog/wardrobe-clothes-organizer-with-mesh-dividers/) cut to size does the same job as a fitted tray, and the [belts and small accessories](/blog/how-to-store-belts-in-closet/) that used to share this drawer moved to their own shallow one. The [jewelry box](/blog/closet-jewelry-organizer-box-with-drawers/) lesson applies here too: it is the internal depth that fails, not the finish.

The rest of the closet is unchanged. Sunglasses stay in their [lined drawer](/blog/how-to-organize-sunglasses-in-a-drawer/), scarves hang on the [loop hanger](/blog/best-scarf-organizer-hanger-for-closet/) by the door, bags stay upright on the shelf in [acrylic cells](/blog/handbag-storage-solutions-for-shelf/), and the [seasonal pass](/blog/seasonal-closet-rotation-checklist-printable/) does not touch this drawer at all, which is the sign of a drawer that is finally working. When it does need a clear out, the same [decluttering rules](/blog/how-to-declutter-before-moving-checklist/) apply: the item that has not been worn since the last wash cycle answers its own question.

Two years on, the drawer closes without a lift, and I have not once dug through it. The [wider system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) has that same goal at every scale: not an arrangement that looks right, but one that stops requiring decisions.
