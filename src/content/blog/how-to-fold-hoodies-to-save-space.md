For two years I owned a shelf that lied. Seven hoodies, folded in thirds like t-shirts, stacked like t-shirts, and by the third week of every winter the stack looked like something leaning away from a strong wind. The bottom hoodie flattened into a felt panini, the top one wore its hood like a raised flag, and pulling the middle one required rebuilding the whole tower on the floor. I folded them more often than I wore some of them, which is a sentence that should never be true about laundry.

The fix was not a folding board or a new dresser. It was learning that hoodies are not thick t-shirts, they are t-shirts wearing equipment, and every folding problem I had traces to three specific features: the hood that puts a dome on one end of every fold, the kangaroo pocket that traps a pillow of air, and the fleece loft that compresses flat under the weight of the stack. Once you fold for those three, the two classic methods, the square fold and the military roll, finally do what tutorials promise. Here are both, step by step, with the hoodie-specific moves that the generic versions skip, and the verdict on where each method belongs in my closet.

## Why hoodies fight every fold you already know

Thirty seconds on the mechanism, because it explains every step that follows. The hood is the stack killer: it is extra fabric on one end, and if it lands folded however it lands, every brick in your stack has a bump. Bumps do not compress, they create shear, and shear is why the tower leans. Fold the hood flat first and the bump disappears.

The pocket is the hidden air mattress. A kangaroo pocket sewn across the front traps a layer of air exactly where the middle of your fold goes, and folded air does not flatten, it springs. Pressing the pocket flat before folding removes maybe a centimeter of thickness per hoodie, which across a stack of five is the difference between a drawer that closes and one that does not.

The loft is the time bomb. Fleece and brushed fleece hold their thickness by being mostly air, which is what makes them warm, and a stack's weight squeezes that air out of the bottom layers permanently. A hoodie that spent a winter at the bottom of a six-stack comes out flat-fronted, with pressure creases across the chest, and no amount of fluffing fully revives it. This is why the stack ceiling below is five, and why the heaviest hoodie never goes on the bottom.

One small detail finishes the set: drawstrings. Loose strings snag the next fold, dangle off the shelf, and catch on the drawer box. They get tamed right after the hood, every time.

## The square fold: for shelves

This is the stackable version, and the goal is a flat, uniform brick, about 25 centimeters across, thin enough to stack honestly. Six steps, in order, because the order is the method.

1. Lay the hoodie face down on a flat surface, zipper zipped or buttons done, and smooth the back with your palm so the fabric starts with no air in it.
2. Fold the hood first. Smooth it flat, then fold it back over the body, so the hood lies inside the rectangle the body is about to form. This is the move every generic folding tutorial skips, and it is the one that makes stacks stop leaning.
3. Tame the strings and the pocket. Lay both drawstrings flat along the body, press the air out of the kangaroo pocket, and smooth its panel down.
4. Fold one side in, about a third of the body width, folding the sleeve across so it lies inside the rectangle. Repeat on the other side. The hoodie is now a narrow, dense column.
5. Fold up from the hem in thirds, smoothing each fold with your palm as you go. The hem folds last so it locks the sides in.
6. Check the result flat: no dome, no dangling sleeve, strings captured. It should sit like a brick, and five of them should look like a wall, not a hill.

Two variants worth knowing. For a shelf you reach daily, stop after step five and stack. For a deep shelf or a basket where you browse vertically, take the brick from step five and fold it once more so it stands on edge, file-folder style, edges out. The file-fold variant trades stack height for visibility, and it is the same principle that runs my folded jeans, which I covered fully in the [jeans on closet shelves guide](/blog/how-to-organize-jeans-on-closet-shelves/).

Stack rules are short: five high maximum, four better, heaviest on the bottom, weekly hoodies in the top three. Two stacks of four beat one stack of eight, every time, because the bottom layer of an eight-stack is a hoodie being pressed into felt for a season.

## The military roll: for drawers

The roll earns its place in drawers, where it converts rows of hidden layers into a lineup you can read at a glance, exactly like the file system in a well-run drawer. Seven steps.

1. Lay the hoodie face down and do the hood first, exactly as in the square fold: smooth flat, fold back over the body. A roll with a dome inside it unwinds.
2. Strings and pocket, same as before: flat, airless, captured.
3. Fold the sleeves straight across the back, one then the other, so the shoulders make a T lying flat.
4. Fold both sides inward to roughly the width of your hand span. You are sizing the cylinder before you roll it, and this width is what keeps chunky hoodies from becoming cigars too fat to stand.
5. Roll tightly from the bottom hem upward, toward the collar, keeping even tension. Rolling from the hem means the finished cylinder's open edge faces up when it stands, and it stays shut.
6. Lock it: fold the loose hood over the outside of the roll and tuck. This is the ranger-roll trick borrowed from packing, and it turns the cylinder from a thing that unrolls in your drawer into a self-securing bundle.
7. Stand the roll on its flat end in the drawer, rolled side up, in rows.

The verdict on rolling is honest: it loves midweight and forgives heavy. A midweight pullover rolls into a firm, tidy cylinder that stands for months. My chunkiest fleece rolled into a fat, soft cigar that leaned on its neighbors and unwound when pulled, so it got demoted to the square fold within a week. The rule of thumb I landed on: if the roll will not stand when you let go, it does not get rolled.

What rolls buy in numbers: my dresser drawer is about 30 centimeters deep, which holds two rows of hoodie rolls plus a row of thinner sweatshirts in front, every single one visible from above. The same drawer, folded in layers the old way, held eight hoodies of which I could see three.

## Which method, where: the verdict list

The decision is made by the furniture, not the fabric. Drawers, which is to say anything deeper than it is tall, take rolls or file-folded bricks, because depth is exactly where stacks hide their contents. Shelves take square bricks in short stacks, because a shelf's height is the resource and a row of cylinders wastes the corners the way a turntable wastes a narrow cabinet, a lesson my pantry already taught me the expensive way.

Hanging gets a narrow verdict: lightweight zip-up hoodies only, on wide or padded hangers, because they keep their shape and it saves the fold entirely. Heavy fleece and thick knit hoods distort on hangers, the shoulder seams take the weight, and the loft flattens where the hanger presses, which is the same physics that makes the [heavy-coat hangers](/blog/best-space-saving-hangers-for-heavy-coats/) right for wool coats and wrong for this drawer. If the hoodie has a hood stiff enough to hold a shape on its own, it is telling you it wants to be folded.

## The drawer that makes folds survive

Folds are a system with the furniture, and the system fails at one specific point: retrieval. A fold that gets pulled out carelessly does not get put back correctly, and a drawer that requires two hands to extract one hoodie will be a pile by Friday. Three hardware facts keep it together.

Drawer dividers keep the rows honest, and the cheap spring-loaded kind work fine; the point is not beauty, it is that a roll has a slot to stand in and cannot migrate. Row depth is one hoodie deep, front to back, because the second row behind the first is a pantry cabinet's dark back shelf wearing cotton. And the pull motion is from the top of the roll with one hand, which works only because the lock-tuck from step six is doing its job. My divider rows also hold the [scarf organizer](/blog/best-scarf-organizer-hanger-for-closet/) overflow in a third column, and the same one-touch principle runs everything else in this closet, down to the boot zone addresses I set up in the [leather boots storage guide](/blog/how-to-store-leather-boots-in-closet/).

## The math, and the laundry loop

Counting my own seven: on shelves stacked the old way, seven hoodies occupied about 60 centimeters of shelf run and leaned. Today, three live on the shelf as square bricks, four live in the drawer as rolls alongside three sweatshirts, and the footprint is one drawer plus about 25 centimeters of shelf. Nothing was bought except a set of dividers at about $9. The saving is not dramatic in centimeters; it is dramatic in that I have seen every hoodie I own from above, every day, for a year, which is the actual product being sold in every folding tutorial.

The loop that keeps it is the laundry day rebuild, and it is the whole maintenance plan. Folded stacks get rebuilt top-down, rolls get re-rolled only if one unwound, and anything that fails its own fold twice in a month is telling you it belongs in a different home, which is the [20/20 rule](/blog/20-20-decluttering-rule-explained/) whispering from inside the laundry pile. Off-season hoodies leave the drawer entirely in summer, following the [winter clothes summer storage system](/blog/how-to-store-winter-clothes-in-summer/), with the overflow in [under-bed boxes](/blog/under-bed-winter-clothes-storage-boxes/) and the truly compressible items in the [vacuum bags](/blog/vacuum-storage-bags-for-comforters-review/) that folded fabric, unlike leather, tolerates fine.

## If you do only one thing

Fold the hood first. That single move, before any body fold, in either method, fixes the leaning tower, the dome, and most of the shear that makes hoodie stacks fail, and it costs zero dollars and zero tools. From there, square bricks on shelves, rolls in drawers, five high, one row deep.
