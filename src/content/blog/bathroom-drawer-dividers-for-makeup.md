For two weeks in October I kept a cheap stopwatch on the bathroom counter. I wanted a number for something I had been complaining about for a year: a drawer that looked tidy, with matching acrylic dividers, still cost me several minutes every morning. On the first Tuesday it took 47 seconds to find a brow pencil. Wednesday, 38. Thursday I gave up and used a different one.

The drawer was organised. That was the problem. It was organised by category, and a morning face is not a category exercise.

## What was actually in there

I emptied it onto a towel on the first Sunday and wrote down every item, how often I had reached for it in the previous seven days, and whether the little open-jar symbol on the packaging had expired.

| Category | Items | Used in the week | Past its date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lip products | 9 | 2 | 3 |
| Eye pencils and mascara | 7 | 3 | 1 (mascara, opened in March) |
| Base products | 5 | 2 | 1 |
| Brushes and tools | 6 | 3 | 0 |
| Small hardware | 4 | 1 | 0 |
| Total | 31 | 11 | 5 |

Thirty-one things, eleven uses, five that should have been thrown out months earlier. I also found two identical eyebrow pencils, which explained the 47 seconds: I had been choosing between a near-empty one and an unopened one every single morning without realising it.

The [vanity drawer walkthrough](/blog/bathroom-vanity-drawer-organization-tips/) I had followed when I first set this up already makes the point that a drawer should follow a person's sequence rather than a household's categories. I had read it, rearranged everything into that order, and still lost 40-odd seconds some mornings. Sequencing 31 objects is not the same as removing the 20 I never touched. Those are two separate problems, and I had only solved one of them.

## Why a full drawer is slow to search

Retail displays are arranged by category because a shopper is browsing. A person at 7 a.m. is not browsing. The sequence is almost always the same: base, brows, eyes, lips, then something to set it. Each step wants two or three specific objects, in the same place every day. What I wanted to understand, after the sequence change failed to fix the timing, was why.

There is a decent body of work on visual search behind this. When the target differs from its neighbours by one obvious feature, say a red tube among black ones, finding it is fast and almost independent of how many distractors there are. When the target shares features with everything around it, which is exactly the case with nine neutral lip products in one long row, the brain checks candidates one at a time and search time climbs roughly linearly with the number of items. Splitting those nine into three cells of three does not reduce the collection, but it converts a slow serial search into three quick ones.

Fitts's law is the other half. Time to reach a target grows with distance and shrinks with target size. A thin pencil lying at the back of a 20-inch drawer is the worst possible combination: far away and narrow. Moving the six daily items into a wide front strip, at the near edge of the drawer where a hand lands naturally, removed more time than any other change I made. I stopped reaching past things.

So the layout I needed was not a better sequence. It was fewer candidates, placed closer. Two lanes: a front strip about 4 inches deep with the six things used every morning, left to right in the order I use them, and a back lane of removable trays holding everything else, grouped loosely by type. Anything I use less than once a fortnight went into a closed box under the sink. The sequence advice from the vanity guide still holds inside that front strip; it just needed a much smaller collection to work on.

## Measuring the box, and what fits inside it

Vanity drawers in older UK and US furniture tend to come in a small set of widths: 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 inches, with depths from 14 to 21 inches and clear heights between 2 and 5 inches. Mine is 24 by 16 with 3.5 inches of usable height. Those are outside numbers. Side panels are commonly 1/2 or 5/8 inch thick, the drawer bottom often sits in a groove a quarter inch above the floor, and an undermount runner can steal another half inch of width near the back.

Measure the clear interior at three points along the depth. Drawer boxes are rarely perfectly square, especially in a flat-pack unit that has been through a few humid summers, and a divider system sized to the widest point will jam at the narrow one. Leave roughly an eighth of an inch of tolerance all round. Divider walls should finish a quarter to half an inch below the drawer side: tall ones scrape knuckles, catch on the cabinet frame above, and stop the drawer closing if anything is standing upright.

Height decides what can stand. Lipsticks are 75 to 85 mm tall, mascara tubes around 130 mm, a 30 ml foundation bottle 110 to 130 mm. In a 3.5-inch (89 mm) drawer, all of those have to lie down. That is fine, but it means the cell size is driven by length rather than diameter, and a grid built for upright lipsticks will be wrong. The [clear acrylic bin guide](/blog/clear-acrylic-bathroom-bins-with-dividers/) goes into the cell-width arithmetic, and its warning about polystyrene yellowing under a bathroom window applies just as much to a divider set bought for a third of the price.

## What cosmetics do to the dividers

A makeup drawer is a dirtier environment than a stationery drawer. Foundation and concealer contain silicone oils and film formers that leave a slightly greasy film on anything they touch. Loose powder and talc work into corners and into the grain of unfinished wood. Mascara and eyeliner shed pigment. None of it is dramatic, but over a few months it turns a pale divider set a dull grey at the points your fingers touch.

Material choice follows from that. Injection-moulded PP or ABS trays lift out and go in the sink. Cast acrylic looks better and stays clear, though isopropyl alcohol, acetone and ethyl acetate can craze it, so a bottle of nail-polish remover with a loose cap has no business in the drawer. Even the vapour is enough to fog a polished surface. Unsealed bamboo and bare MDF take up oil and swell at the edges in a room that hits 80 percent relative humidity after a shower; sealed or painted timber is fine. I would skip fabric-lined and felt dividers altogether. They feel nice for a fortnight and then hold foundation powder in a way no amount of wiping fixes, which is the same objection I have to wicker baskets on [open bathroom shelves](/blog/diy-bathroom-shelf-organization/).

Antimicrobial or silver-ion coatings come up in listings for makeup organisers. I am sceptical of the value here. The residue in a makeup drawer comes from the products, not from a sterile surface, and a coating does nothing about a mascara that should have been discarded three months ago. A removable tray you can actually wash beats a permanent treated one.

## Four kinds of divider, and how each one fails

Fixed moulded grids are the cheapest and the tidiest-looking. They also assume your collection will never change. A cell sized for a lipstick will not accept a 40 mm foundation bottle, and because the walls are solvent-bonded at the corners, that corner is where a heavy item eventually cracks the joint.

Spring-loaded adjustable bars solve that. They press against the drawer sides and can be moved in seconds. The failure mode is creep: timber moves with humidity, the spring loses a little tension, and after a few months a bar has drifted a quarter inch and the neatly arranged row has collapsed sideways. On a slick painted-metal drawer bottom they slide from day one unless there is a thin non-slip liner underneath. A liner is worth having anyway, because it stops the trays rattling every time the drawer closes.

Separate small boxes that tile together are the most forgiving. You can rearrange them in a minute, take one out to clean, and replace a single cracked one. The cost is space: every box has two walls, and in a 24-inch drawer four boxes in a row can give up close to an inch of usable width to plastic. The gaps between them also collect powder, which is why I lift them all out together rather than wiping around them.

Felt, fabric and foam inserts are quiet and stop things rolling. In a humid bathroom with oily products they are the one option I would rule out entirely.

Small steel tools are the exception to all of this. Tweezers, a lash curler, nail clippers and scissors never sit well in a shallow cell, and they are the things most likely to snag a liner. I moved mine to the [magnetic strip inside the cabinet door](/blog/magnetic-organizer-for-bathroom-cabinet/), which freed a whole cell and stopped me fishing a curler out from under a pile of brushes.

## The layout, and the second fortnight

The front lane is 4 inches deep and holds six things in the order I use them: primer, brow pencil, mascara, one lip product, a small brush, and a compact. Behind it sit three lift-out trays: eye products, lip products, and a mixed tray of backups. One cell in the mixed tray is deliberately empty. The five expired items went in the bin, the duplicate brow pencil went into a travel bag, and the unopened spares went under the sink.

I timed another two weeks. The average dropped from 41 seconds to 19, with the slowest morning at 26 seconds when I was looking for something I had moved to the back lane on purpose. That is not a rigorous experiment, and I am not going to claim a percentage improvement for a sample of one person in one bathroom. But the change was big enough that I stopped noticing the drawer, which is the only outcome I actually wanted. The empty cell filled up in the third week with a new concealer, exactly as intended.

Monthly maintenance is now one job rather than a schedule. On the first Saturday I lift out the trays, wash them in warm water with a little dish detergent, dry them completely, and check the period-after-opening symbols while everything is on the counter. Mascara is a three-month item, most liquid foundations six to twelve. A divider set exposes that information because you can see the whole collection at once, and that visibility is worth more than the tidiness.

## When dividers are the wrong purchase

If the clear height is under 2 inches, there is not enough depth for a useful cell and a flat non-slip liner does more. If the collection is a dozen items, a single shallow tray beats a grid. If your problem is that makeup looks wrong once you leave the house, the issue is lighting, not storage: a source under CRI 90 shifts foundation shades enough to fool you, and the [vanity lighting breakdown](/blog/best-makeup-vanity-table-with-lights-storage/) covers that side of the morning properly.

Renters sharing a bathroom should also think twice. A set of loose trays travels with you; a cut-to-fit grid glued into somebody else's drawer does not. And if the drawer itself is warped or the runner is sticking, fix that first. No divider corrects a box that does not close squarely.
