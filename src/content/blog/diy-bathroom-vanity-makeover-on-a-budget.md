Budget makeover guides usually open with paint colours, which skips the only decision that matters. A vanity makeover is a bet that the box under the finish is worth keeping, and in a bathroom that bet fails more often than the guides admit, because the wet room attacks cabinets where paint cannot follow. So the project starts with a diagnosis, not a colour chart.

## The tree that decides the project

```
 Is the cabinet box sound?
 |
 +-- swollen base, sagging shelf,
 |   hinge screws spinning, musty
 |   smell from the toe-kick?
 |        |
 |        +-- YES -> replace, don't paint
 |                  (paint seals rot in, not out)
 |
 +-- box sound, finish dated?
          |
          +-- style is the problem
          |      -> PAINT + HARDWARE (this guide)
          |
          +-- layout is the problem
                 (drawer too shallow, door
                 fights the wall)
                 -> reconfigure or replace
                 -> but add the cheap fixes
                    first: see tier list
```

The branches deserve their evidence. Swollen base means the particleboard core has drunk water at the floor line, usually through the toe-kick, and the swell does not dry back; paint seals the damage behind a film that keeps failing at the same seam. A sagging shelf means the span or the material is past its rating, and no finish changes load paths. Hinge screws that spin in stripped holes mean the board around them has crumbled, which is fixable with glued dowels and patience, but three of them plus swelling plus sag adds up to a box that will not hold new hardware either. Everything else, orange oak, dated hardware, a cloudy laminate top, a colour you never chose, is surface, and surface is exactly what a budget makeover fixes.

The layout branch is quieter. If the drawer stops two-thirds out and the top shelf is unreachable without kneeling, that was covered in the [small vanity selection guide](/blog/small-bathroom-vanity-with-built-in-storage/), and paint will not fix it; but the cheap tier of the [hidden storage ladder](/blog/hidden-storage-solutions-for-bathroom-vanity/) often will, and a makeover is the natural moment to do both, with the doors already off.

## The paint system, in working order

Assuming the tree said paint, the job is a chain, and the chain's links matter more than any single product. Written in the order the work happens:

1. Strip and wash. Doors off, hinges off, hardware in a labeled bag, tops and drawers out. Wash everything that will be painted with a TSP substitute or strong sugar soap: hand cream, hair product and soap splash are invisible oils, and paint over oil fails in flakes. Rinse, dry, an hour.
2. Scuff, do not strip. 180 to 220 grit over every painted surface, just enough to kill the gloss and give the primer a tooth to hold. Stripping to bare wood is unnecessary on sound paint and creates dust nobody needs; the scuff is the difference between primer that grips and primer that peels in sheets.
3. Bonding primer, everywhere. This is the product doing the real work. Bonding primers carry resins designed to key into glossy old finishes, and on oak they also fill the grain; a second thin pass on raw or oak-grain areas pays off in the topcoat. Two hours in, and the cabinet is no longer orange, just ugly-grey, which is the correct intermediate state.
4. Two thin topcoats. A waterborne cabinet enamel, satin or semi-gloss for washability, applied thin. The amateur instinct is one heavy coat, and heavy coats run, sag on vertical rails and stay soft for weeks. Two thin coats with a light de-nib sand between them, 320 grit, beats one thick coat by every measure that survives contact with a bathroom.
5. The cure, which is the step everyone skips. Surface dry is hours; full cure, where the film reaches its final hardness, is 7 to 30 days depending on the product. During the first week the finish marks under anything wet or heavy. Towels on hooks, felt pads under bottles, a note on the mirror for the household. Paint that gets scratched in week one was not a bad paint; it was treated as cured before it was.

The room imposes its own conditions: humidity under about 70 percent while painting, dust settled, and a fan that moves air without blowing it across wet film. Winter bathrooms with a hot shower an hour before painting fail at the condensation step, invisibly, and the paint tells you weeks later.

Application tools deserve one honest paragraph, because this is where first-timers lose a clean job. A 4-inch foam roller lays the flattest finish on flat door faces but carries bubbles if you press, so the touch is weightless, and each stroke ends by lightly re-rolling from wet into dry. A good synthetic angled brush does rails, edges and anything with profile, and its strokes go one way, with the grain where there is grain. Sprayers give the best result and the worst project-management: masking a bathroom to spray indoors is a full day of tape and sheeting, and the payoff only beats a careful roller on framed doors with deep profile. On a first makeover, roller plus brush is the recommendation the results support; keep the sprayer for the second one, by which point the wash-scuff-prime rhythm is automatic.

Light hides everything until you look from the side. The raking check, a phone flashlight held low and aimed across the surface while the coat is still wet, shows runs, sags and the missed stripe under every rail while they still cost thirty seconds to fix instead of a week. Do it per coat, not at the end, and re-coat windows stay open.

## Hardware, the highest return per dollar

New pulls and hinges change a cabinet faster than any paint colour, and they are governed by exactly one measurement: the centre-to-centre distance between existing screw holes. The standards are 3 inches, which is 76 mm, then 96 mm and 128 mm, plus the single-hole knob. Measure the old hardware, buy the same spacing, and the swap is fifteen minutes with a screwdriver. Any other spacing means filling old holes with two-part wood filler, re-drilling, and owning the result, which is an hour of work and a visible patch if the filler is rushed.

While the doors are off, the hinges themselves deserve the upgrade pass: soft-close hinges in the same mounting pattern as the old ones are a twenty-dollar change that the household feels every single morning. Matching the pattern means reading the old cup hinge, because European-style hinges come in full and half wrap plates for different frame thicknesses, and the cup diameter, 35 mm on nearly everything modern, tells you the drill bit if holes must move. The handle-height rule of thumb, pulls near the top rail of doors and centred on drawer fronts, matters less than consistency, because mixed positions on a pair of doors is the detail the eye catches forever.

Filled holes, where new hardware spacing differs from old, are a small craft of their own. Two-part wood filler or a hardwood dowel set in glue, flush-sanded, primed with the same bonding primer as the rest, then painted: done this way, a filled hole disappears entirely. Done in a rush with wall spackle, it shows as a dull ring for the life of the finish, because spackle never hardens enough to take the same sheen as enamel over wood.

## The countertop question

Flat laminate tops take films well, and a vinyl contact film is the only truly reversible change here, which makes it the rental default. Its failure mode is precise: water, entering at seams and cut edges. So the technique is edge-first: cut the main sheet a hair proud, wrap the front edge as one continuous piece rather than butting seams at the corner, and run a bead of clear silicone where the film meets the sink cut-out and the backsplash. Heat is the other limit, since films deform well below the temperatures hair tools hold for minutes after use, so the trivet habit from the hair-tool section is not optional here.

What films and paint should not touch is real stone. A calcite marble top is not a dated finish, it is a material with its own care logic, and painting or filming it buries the surface that the [marble care guide](/blog/how-to-clean-marble-vanity-top/) maintains for decades. On stone, the honest budget moves are a re-seal, a polish, or nothing.

Between film and stone sit the painted-countertop kits, epoxy-based, and they deserve the warning that their glossy showroom results depend on a flawless substrate and a cure week in a dust-free room, two conditions a working bathroom resists. On a cracked or chipped laminate, a replacement top, stock cultured marble cut to size, runs two to three hundred and outlives any coating.

## The faucet, the frame and the budget

If the budget stretches past paint and hardware, the faucet is the next dollar with the best return, the one part everyone touches daily. The job needs a basin wrench, new flexible supply lines, an hour and a dry towel down the pedestal; the [floating vanity guide](/blog/how-to-style-a-floating-vanity/) covers the shutoff-and-tailpiece side from the cabinet's perspective. Two checks before buying: the hole configuration, single-hole versus the widespread three-hole pattern with handles at 4 or 8 inches, and the shutoff valves, which either turn or tell you the plumber is coming first. Mirrors take a cheaper path: a mirror frame kit, four moulding strips with clips or adhesive, turns a frameless slab into a framed mirror for thirty to sixty dollars, no glass work.

## The five failures, gathered from the field

Almost every botched budget makeover traces to one of five moves, and listing them costs less than reliving them.

Skipping the wash is the most common, and the symptom arrives in month three as a flake at exactly the spot where hand cream used to land. Over-thick coats are second, sold by the same instinct that wants the orange gone in one pass, and their symptom is sags on the vertical rails that only show from the side, in the light, forever. Treating surface-dry as cured is third, and its symptom is the felt-pad dent constellation from everything that sat on the top during week one. Unsealed film edges are fourth, and theirs is a lifting corner by season's end, always at the sink cut-out, always where the water sat. And the fifth is buying hardware on looks alone, arriving home to hole spacing that does not match, then filling and re-drilling in a hurry, which is how a forty-dollar upgrade acquires its visible ring of regret.

Every one of the five is a sentence of prevention that costs nothing at the moment it matters: wash, thin coats, the cure calendar, silicone at the edges, measure before the cart fills.

One worked budget, the middle path on a sound 30-inch oak vanity: bonding primer and cabinet enamel, sixty dollars; pulls and hinges, forty; mirror frame, forty-five; film and silicone for the top, twenty-five; consumables, sandpaper, TSP substitute, brushes, thirty. Two hundred dollars, two afternoons, and the vanity that results is indistinguishable from a mid-range new box at six to eight times the money, with the old one's drawers, which fit the room, still fitting the room.

## Living with the finish, after the cure

A cured enamel finish is washable but not invincible, and the maintenance is deliberately boring. Damp microfibre, pH-neutral soap, the same routine the marble guide prescribes for stone; no abrasive creams, no melamine sponges used dry, no magic of any kind, because a scuffed satin sheen cannot be spot-repaired, only re-sanded and re-coated whole. Water finds the bottom edge first, so the toe-kick wipe that the hidden-storage projects recommend doubles as finish insurance where the film is thinnest and the mop water lives. And when a chip does happen, and on a working bathroom it will, the fix is cheap if it is early: a drop of the leftover topcoat, feathered with a fingertip, before water works into the raw edge and makes the chip grow.

The new-vs-madeover comparison ends at the same place the tree started: a sound box is worth two afternoons, a rotten one is not, and the skills, washing, scuffing, priming, driving hardware straight, transfer directly to the [wood organizer builds](/blog/diy-bathroom-wall-organizer-wood/) that usually come next on the same wall. The rest of the [vanity guides](/bathroom/vanity/) cover what happens on the counter, in the drawers and under the doors once the finish is cured.
