Three winters, three different apartments, and the coats moved four times. Each move was an attempt to answer the same question, which is not "where is there space" but "which of these spaces will not quietly destroy a 400 dollar coat over seven months".

The answer turned out to be less about square footage than about two numbers nobody measures before loading a trunk: peak summer temperature, and how far the humidity swings in a day.

## The recommendations that fail first

Attic, garage, basement, storage unit. Those four cover most of the advice online, and three of them are disqualified by physics before cost or convenience enters the conversation.

| Space | Summer peak | Humidity behaviour | What fails first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attic | 55 to 65°C | Very dry, then damp in autumn | Down loft, waxed finishes, cuff elastic |
| Garage | 35 to 45°C | 60 to 90 percent, swings daily | Zips, buckles, lining adhesive |
| Basement | 18 to 22°C | 65 to 75 percent, wicks at walls | Mould on leather, musty wool |
| Interior closet | 20 to 26°C | 40 to 55 percent | Nothing, if it stays dry |
| Storage unit | 45 to 55°C unconditioned | Varies with the door opening | Everything, and access |

The interior closet wins on every row, which is the boring result, and the useful part is knowing why the others lose rather than just that they do.

## What heat does to a coat, material by material

Down is not damaged by cold. It is damaged by heat, because the natural oils that keep the clusters separate migrate out of the fibres above roughly 40°C. A summer in an attic drives that process for months, and the coat comes down flat and stays flat.

Wool is more tolerant of heat and less tolerant of compression. A wool coat folded into a trunk for seven months takes creases along the fold that are not temporary deformation but fibre damage, and no amount of steaming brings the nap back.

Waxed cotton is the one people lose without noticing. The wax softens above about 35°C, and a coat packed against itself in a warm attic bonds to its own fabric. It comes out with shiny patches where the wax migrated and dull patches where it left.

Synthetic fill is the tough one. A polyester parka will survive an attic summer and come back slightly less puffy, which is why the cheap coat is the one that teaches the wrong lesson.

## The garage fails on condensation, not humidity

A garage's humidity number looks survivable on paper, somewhere between 60 and 90 percent depending on the season. The real problem is the daily swing, because air that warms during the day holds more moisture and then gives it up at night onto anything cooler than the air.

Metal is cooler than the air at night. That is where the water goes, which means zips, snaps, buckles and the metal eyelets on a hood. A coat can sit in a garage that never feels damp and still come out with a zip that will not run, because the rust formed on the inside of the teeth where nobody looked.

The same mechanism explains why a shed is worse than a garage, and why a car trunk is the single worst place a coat can spend a summer.

## Storage units, priced honestly

This is the recommendation that sounds responsible and usually is not.

An unconditioned unit in summer behaves like a metal box in the sun, which is to say 45 to 55°C with no air movement. A climate controlled unit solves the temperature and costs 60 to 200 dollars a month depending on the city, which is 700 to 2400 dollars a year.

A good down parka costs 300 to 600. So the arithmetic at the top end is that one year of climate controlled storage buys three replacement coats, and the unit is protecting a coat that will still age while it sits there.

The second cost is access. A coat in a unit forty minutes away stops existing in any practical sense, and the winter it is needed is the winter a second coat gets bought. That is the failure mode I have watched happen twice, and neither time was about money.

If a unit is genuinely the only option, the climate controlled one is better than a garage, which the [full winter storage method](/blog/how-to-store-winter-clothes-in-summer/) also concludes. But it should be the last resort after volume has been reduced, not the first answer to a full closet.

## Climate changes the order, not the verdict

Advice written for one region travels badly, so it is worth separating the two failure modes.

Heat is a universal problem. Any unheated space under a roof will run 20 to 30 degrees above outdoor air in sun, whether the climate is dry or humid, so the attic verdict holds from Arizona to Scotland. The number changes and the mechanism does not.

Humidity is regional. In a dry cold climate with summer relative humidity under 40 percent, a garage is survivable for a wool coat as long as the metal is protected, because there is not enough water in the air to condense on a zip. In a humid climate the same garage is a mould incubator, and the basement is worse.

That asymmetry is useful for prioritising. If only one thing can be fixed, fix heat, because it applies everywhere and it damages the fill irreversibly. Humidity damage is often recoverable with airing and a brush, and heat damage is not.

The one case where a garage genuinely wins is a coat that is already ten years old, already lost its loft, and is used for shovelling. Protecting it is not worth a shelf in the apartment, which is a decision about the coat rather than about the storage.

## Reduce the volume instead of exporting it

The move that solved this for me was not a location. It was accepting that four heavy coats do not need four coat-sized spaces.

A down parka compresses to about a third of its packed volume. A wool overcoat does not compress at all but rolls into a long narrow shape that fits under a bed. A waxed jacket folds twice and goes flat. Four coats that occupied 90 centimetres of rail became two flat boxes and one shelf bag, all inside the apartment, all at living temperature.

The [flat under-bed boxes](/blog/under-bed-winter-clothes-storage-boxes/) take the wool and the waxed jacket. The down goes into a breathable bag on the top shelf, pressed by hand rather than by vacuum. The [fabric bins that breathe](/blog/collapsible-storage-bins-with-lids-fabric-stackable/) hold gloves, hats and the scarf that always goes missing.

That is less romantic than a trunk in an attic, and it is the version that has not cost me a coat yet.

## The compression line

Compression is safe up to a point, and the point is different for each fill.

Down tolerates about three months of firm compression without measurable loss. Past six months, loft recovery drops to roughly 90 percent of original, and the loss compounds if the same coat is compressed the same way every year. That is still acceptable for a parka worn twenty times a season and not acceptable for the one worn daily.

Vacuum bags are the wrong tool for down, not because compression is harmful but because sealing is. The bag holds whatever moisture was in the coat against the feathers for months, and a coat that went in slightly damp comes out smelling of a wet dog. Pressing the air out by hand and leaving the bag breathable gets most of the space with none of that.

Wool should not be compressed at all. Leather should not be sealed at all, because it needs 45 to 55 percent humidity to stay supple and moulds in a sealed bag. The specialty cases are covered in more detail in the [rotation checklist](/blog/seasonal-closet-rotation-checklist-printable/), which is where the bin numbers get recorded.
