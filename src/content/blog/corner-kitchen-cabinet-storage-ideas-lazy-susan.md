The stockpot was in there. I knew it was in there because I had put it in there in March, and I found it again in October with dust on the lid and a dried lentil stuck to the handle. The corner cabinet in my 1930s Chicago kitchen measures 36 inches along each run, and for two years I used the back half of it as a place where objects went to retire. I am a renter, so a cabinet remodel was never on the table. A turntable was. What I did not expect was that buying one would turn into three weekends of measuring, one return, and a sheet of masking tape stuck inside the door.

I have written before about [giving the corners of a room a job before buying hardware for them](/blog/how-to-utilize-corner-space-for-storage-ideas/). A cabinet corner is a different problem. It already has a job, it has walls on two sides, and the door only opens on one of them. The square footage is there. What is missing is a way to get a hand to it.

## The part of the corner my arm could not reach

I emptied the cabinet on a Saturday and got into it with a tape measure and a flashlight. The nominal size is 36 by 36 inches, which is the number printed on the listing and the number I had been quoting to anyone who would listen. The clear square inside the two side panels is 33 inches. Three quarter inch panels, a back, and a filler strip beside the adjoining drawer bank eat the rest. The door opening is 15.5 inches wide and sits entirely on the left run. On the right run there is no door at all, just a fixed panel and the side of the dishwasher.

That asymmetry is the whole problem. Anything parked in the far right section of the cabinet can only be reached by leaning across the open left door, past a hinge arm, and under a countertop. I measured how far I could reach while standing normally, with one hand on the counter for balance. Comfortable stopped at 21 inches from the counter front. At 22 inches I was already turning my shoulder and bending my knee. Beyond 24 inches I had to kneel on the floor, which is what I had been doing every time I wanted the stockpot, and which is why I stopped wanting it.

Here is the drawing I made on the back of a grocery receipt. It is not to scale, but every number on it came off the tape.

```text
TOP VIEW, door open. Measurements in inches.

  back wall
  +--------------------------------------------+
  |   DEAD TRIANGLE: no door above this part   |
  |   beyond the comfortable reach line        |
  |                  ______                    |
  |                /        \                  |
  |               |    O     |   O = pivot     |
  |                \________/    15.75 to      |
  |                  disc         right wall  |
  |                                            |
  +----------- 22 in reach line --------------+  counter front
  |__ hinge __|  15.5 in door opening
     (1.5 in of hinge arm inside the opening)
```

The reach line matters more than any product specification I read. Once I had that strip of tape on the cabinet floor, I could see that roughly the back third of the space was only ever going to hold things I use twice a year. That is not a failure of the cabinet. It is geometry plus a door on the wrong side. A lazy susan does not create new space, it moves existing space past the opening so the door can see it. The moment I framed it that way, the shopping got simpler and a lot less expensive.

## A circle drawn inside a square

Every listing for a corner turntable shows a cheerful photograph of a disc packed with jars. None of them mention what a circle does to a square. If a disc has diameter D and just fits inside a square of side D, the area you can use is pi times (D/2) squared, while the square is D squared. The ratio is pi divided by four, about 0.7854. Even in a perfect world, a round shelf inside a square cabinet throws away 21.5 percent of the floor before you put a single jar on it.

The perfect world does not exist, because a disc needs clearance to turn. If the clearance on each side is c, the usable radius becomes D/2 minus c, and the usable fraction is pi times (D/2 - c) squared, divided by the square of the interior width. For my 33 inch interior with three quarters of an inch of clearance, a 32 inch disc gives:

```text
interior width W = 33 in
clearance     c = 0.75 in
disc          D = 32 in, radius 16 in

usable fraction = pi * (16 - 0.75)^2 / 33^2
                = 3.1416 * 232.5625 / 1089
                = 0.671  -> about 67%

with the 28 in disc I kept:
                = 3.1416 * (14 - 0.75)^2 / 1089
                = 0.507  -> about 51%
```

So the biggest disc I could have fitted would have used two thirds of the cabinet floor, and the disc I ended up keeping uses about half. That sounds like a loss until you remember what the other half was doing before: nothing, except hiding a stockpot for seven months. Half of a space I can actually reach beats all of a space I avoid.

The same arithmetic is why I no longer trust a plan drawn from the outside of a cabinet. The [pantry spacing blueprint I keep coming back to](/blog/walk-in-pantry-layout-ideas-and-spacing-blueprint/) starts from clear interior dimensions for exactly this reason. Cabinet nomenclature describes the hole in the wall, not the box you get to fill.

There is a second, more useful version of the formula, and it is the one that would have saved me the return trip. The largest disc that will turn without scraping is twice the shortest distance from the pivot to a side wall, minus twice the clearance:

```text
D_max = 2 * min(distance from pivot to left wall,
                distance from pivot to right wall) - 2c
```

My pivot was supposed to be centered at 16.5 inches from each wall. The predrilled holes in the base plate were offset by three quarters of an inch toward the right, and I did not notice until the disc was already in. Actual distances came out at 17.25 inches to the left wall and 15.75 inches to the right. With c at 0.75 inches, D_max was 30 inches. The 32 inch disc I had carried home was two inches too large on the tight side, which is exactly how far it ground into the right panel when I spun it. I have a pale grey arc on that panel now, at about knee height, where the rim rubbed.

## Four addresses, one cabinet

Once the disc was the right size, I stopped thinking about it as a shelf and started treating it as four small rooms. Each one gets a specific kind of object, and the rule for each is written on a strip of masking tape along the outer edge of that sector. I borrowed the habit from the way I [split a small apartment pantry into jobs rather than zones](/blog/small-pantry-cabinet-organization-hacks-for-apartment/), and it survived contact with real cooking better than any bin system I tried.

Address A is the front arc of the upper tier, the part that appears first when the door opens and the disc begins to turn. This is the only address that gets used daily. It holds the olive oil, the salt, the pepper grinder, the small jar of bay leaves, and the lid I use for the 8 inch skillet. Five things. I counted how often I opened the cabinet across six weeks and got 41 openings, 34 of which were for one of those five. That concentration is why the front arc is worth protecting. When a visitor puts the vinegar back anywhere else, it drifts to address B within a day and I am spinning the disc again.

Address B is the back arc of the upper tier, beyond the 22 inch line but still visible from above when the disc turns. Square and tall things live here: the cereal boxes I buy in pairs, the bag of rice, the tin of baking soda. The tallest bottle in this group is 9.25 inches, which is why I set clear tier height at 11.5 inches. Two and a quarter inches of space above the cap is what lets my fingers get around it. I learned that from stacking containers that fit on paper and did not fit in practice; the same mistake shows up in [how I now choose airtight jars by their real height](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers-for-flour-and-sugar/).

Address C is the inner ring of the lower tier, close to the pivot. This is heavy country. Two cast iron pans, the enamel pot, the mortar, and a bag of potatoes sit within about 6 inches of the center post. Nothing here is pretty and nothing here needs to be seen from the doorway. It just has to be retrievable without lifting another object first.

Address D is the outer ring of the lower tier and the dead triangle behind it. The outer ring holds light, awkward things: nesting bowls, the collapsible steamer, a roll of parchment, the spare lids. The dead triangle, which has no door above it at all, holds the roasting pan, the turkey platter, and a spare stockpot lid, each in a labeled cotton bag. I used the same [short vinyl labels I made for pantry jars](/blog/how-to-label-pantry-jars-custom-vinyl-stickers/) on those bags, with the month written under the name, because a bag that says roasting pan in March tells me nothing about whether I have used it since. Six months later, two of the three still say March.

## What the hinge steals at 90 degrees

I nearly skipped this measurement, and it is the reason the first disc came back out. A corner door does not open into empty air. The hinge arm swings into the opening, and on a 1930s cabinet with surface mounted hinges the arm projects about 1.5 inches into the clear width at 90 degrees. At 135 degrees, with the door folded back against the neighbouring run, that drops to roughly three quarters of an inch. My dishwasher sits 4 inches from the corner on the right, and the door will not travel past about 112 degrees before it taps the handle. That single number decides which shape of turntable can actually be loaded.

| Shape | Door swing to clear the rim | Usable radius in my 33 in box | What it gives up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full round, 28 in | about 135 degrees | 14 in | Front rim fouls the hinge below 110 degrees; needs the dishwasher gap |
| Kidney or D shape, 28 in | about 110 degrees | 13 in, with a cutout | Square containers sit badly in the notch; loses roughly a sixth of the arc |
| Half moon swing-out trays | 90 degrees | about 12 in per tray | Needs 18 in of clear floor in front of the cabinet to pull fully out |
| Fixed diagonal shelves | 90 degrees | 20 in of depth | Deepest shelf stays beyond the reach line, so the back half is storage, not use |

I went with the full round disc and a 3 mm rubber mat under the base plate. It clears at about 112 degrees provided I keep the outer 2 inches of the rim free of anything taller than a spice jar, which is another way of saying address A has a height limit as well as an address. The kidney shape was the safer fit on paper and lost to the fact that I store rice in square boxes.

If the door had stopped at 90 degrees, I would have gone a different way. The [pull-out shelves I built for the straight run of cabinets](/blog/diy-pull-out-shelves-for-kitchen-cabinets/) taught me that a tray you can bring all the way out solves a reach problem that no rotating shelf can solve, as long as you have floor space in front of the cabinet to receive it. In a 580 square foot kitchen, that floor space is the aisle I stand in, so a full swing-out unit was never going to be polite. The broader lesson about deep cabinets is the one I keep in the [deep cabinet guide](/blog/how-to-organize-deep-kitchen-cabinets/): do not add storage to the back of a deep box, bring the back of the box forward.

## The swing-out unit I carried back to the store

For about ten days I owned a swing-out corner system, the kind with two wire baskets on a pivoting arm that folds out of the opening. It is a good piece of hardware. It was wrong for this cabinet and wrong for this lease, for two reasons I could have checked before ordering.

The first was weight. I loaded the lower basket the way the photograph suggested, with two cast iron pans and a stockpot at the front of the wire tray, and put the whole thing on the bathroom scale. Loaded, it was 8.4 kg. The bearing is rated for 15 kg per tier, so I was well inside the limit. It still felt stiff and it still sagged about 4 mm when fully extended. That is not a weight problem, it is a lever problem, and it is the same physics that makes a loaded turntable hard to start. Weight multiplied by distance from the pivot is what the bearing feels. Eight kilograms sitting 13 inches out behaves like a much heavier load than eight kilograms sitting 6 inches out.

The second was the installation. The arm needs four screws into the cabinet floor and two into the door, and the door screws have to land in a panel that is eighty-something years old and has already been patched once. I am not drilling that. My landlord is reasonable, but a split door stile is not something I can undo when I move out, and the deposit is not a research budget. I repacked it, took it back, and bought a freestanding two-tier turntable that lifts out in one piece.

What the swing-out experiment did give me was a better inventory. While everything was out of the cabinet on the floor, I counted eleven pots, pans and lids, and admitted that four of them belonged in the [pan rack on the straight run](/blog/best-pan-organizer-rack-for-kitchen-cabinet/) rather than in the corner at all. The baking sheets went to a [vertical rack beside the oven](/blog/kitchen-baking-sheet-organizer-rack-vertical-cabinet/). That cut the corner load from eleven pieces to six before any organizer was installed, and it is the single change that made the half-used disc feel generous instead of cramped. Most corner cabinet advice starts with a product. It should start with removing two things.

## Six errors that showed up after the first week

The disc went in on a Sunday. By the following Sunday I had written six lines on the masking tape inside the door. These are the ones that actually cost me time.

1. The pivot was off center by three quarters of an inch. The base plate had two sets of predrilled holes and I used the wrong pair. Mark the center on the cabinet floor with a pencil and measure to both walls before tightening anything. A disc that clears on the left can still grind on the right.

2. The upper tier was 1.5 inches too low. I set it by eye, then tried to lift a 9.25 inch bottle out at an angle under a shelf. Raise the collar until the tallest item plus two inches of finger room fits with the door open, not with the door closed and the shelf empty.

3. Pan handles overhang the rim. A 12 inch skillet with a 7 inch handle does not care about disc diameter. I gained an inch of clearance by dropping from a 30 inch disc to a 28 inch one, and the handles stopped kissing the side panel.

4. Square containers rotated into the dead triangle and stayed there. A round disc has no front, so anything placed near the back at a random angle is out of sight. The sector labels fixed this. Without them I was spinning the disc three times per meal.

5. A tall bottle on the outer edge hit the hinge arm. The front arc has a height limit as well as a width limit, about 7 inches in my cabinet at 112 degrees of door swing. Anything taller belongs in address B.

6. I ignored the pipe chase at the back right. There is a 2 inch bulge in the back panel where the old gas line used to run, and it reduces clear depth in one corner. It is the same annoying subtraction I deal with [under the bathroom sink](/blog/under-sink-storage-plumbing-friendly/), except here it is behind a rotating shelf where I cannot see it until something jams.

None of these are product defects. All six are measurements I did not take, or measurements I took and then ignored because the photograph on the box looked convincing. The tape inside the door is now the most useful thing in the cabinet, and it cost nothing.

## A load map taped to the inside of the door

Once the errors were listed, the last fix was where the weight sat. I drew a side view on the same sheet of tape and kept it there. It looks like this.

```text
SIDE VIEW through the door opening

  upper tier, 11.5 in clear height
  +----------------------------------+
  | 4.2 kg   tins, spices, oil        |  <- light, near the rim
  +----------------------------------+
                 O  pivot / center post
  +----------------------------------+
  | 8.4 kg   cast iron, stockpot      |  <- heavy, inside 6 in radius
  +----------------------------------+
  ^ 4.5 in from door opening to disc edge

  start-up effort = weight x radius
  4 kg at 13 in = 52 in-pounds
  4 kg at  6 in = 24 in-pounds
```

The arithmetic is deliberately rough. What matters is the ratio, not the unit. Moving the cast iron inward roughly halved the effort needed to start the disc turning, and the bearing stopped making the small clicking noise it made in week one. My lower tier still weighs 8.4 kg, which is comfortably under the 15 kg rating, and it now turns with one finger. Ratings tell you when hardware will break. Radius tells you whether you will want to use it.

The reach line and the radius map together explain the whole cabinet. Light things can sit at the outer edge because they are cheap to rotate and easy to see. Heavy things sit near the pivot because they are expensive to rotate and I do not need to see them. Anything heavy and far away is the worst possible combination, which is precisely what the original stockpot was. It lived at the back, on the floor, at the end of a long lever, and it took me seven months to want it badly enough to kneel down.

The two things I could not fit went elsewhere. A second stockpot moved to a [floating shelf over the tiny studio counter](/blog/floating-shelves-storage-ideas-for-tiny-studio-kitchen/), where it is visible and therefore used. The seasonal platters stayed in the dead triangle. That sort of decision is not really about a cabinet at all, it is the same rule the [whole home system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) uses: give every object one address you can reach, and do not let an awkward address collect a second job.

## What a renter can change without a drill

Most of the advice written about blind corners assumes you own the cabinets. I do not, so here is the version that survives a move, arranged as conditions rather than a shopping list.

If the door opens less than 110 degrees, skip full round discs and measure for a kidney shape or a half moon tray. If it opens more than 130 degrees and the rim still fouls the hinge, the base plate is off center, so move the plate before you buy a smaller disc.

If the cabinet floor is particleboard and slightly bowed, put a 3 mm rubber mat under the base plate. Mine stopped the whole assembly from walking sideways when I spin the loaded lower tier, and it lifts out with the unit.

If you need screws to make it work, do not buy it. A freestanding turntable with its own post and two collars comes out in one piece, leaves four small scuffs on a rubber mat, and goes into the next apartment with you.

If the tallest bottle is over 10 inches, move that bottle out rather than raising the upper tier until the disc no longer fits under the counter. The [clear bins I use in the fridge](/blog/best-clear-plastic-bins-for-fridge-storage/) taught me that a container which fits the shelf but cannot be lifted out is not stored, it is parked. The same goes for the [glass bottles I keep on the door shelves](/blog/aesthetic-fridge-organization-tips/): height is a decision about hands, not about space.

If the dead triangle is visible from the doorway, give it one labeled bag and stop trying to make it productive. If it is not visible, put the twice-a-year things there and write the month on the label.

What I did not do is worth listing too. I did not add lighting, because a battery puck inside a rotating shelf gets knocked off within a week. I did not add a second tier of small bins, because bins on a disc just rotate a clutter problem. I did not buy the $200 acrylic set I saw on TikTok two years ago, which is how I ended up with three empty containers and a stockpot I could not find.

## One number to take to the hardware store

Six weeks in, the reach line still has its strip of tape, and the pencil mark at 15.75 inches is still on the cabinet floor under the rubber mat. The stockpot has been used four times since October, which is four more times than in the seven months before. Nothing about the cabinet changed. The panels are the same, the door still stops at 112 degrees, and the pipe chase still bulges at the back right. What changed is that the things I reach for now rotate into the opening instead of waiting behind it.

If you take one number with you, take the shortest distance from the pivot to a side wall. Double it, subtract an inch and a half for clearance, and that is the largest disc that will turn in your cabinet. Mine was 30 inches. I bought 28, and the two inches I left over are where the pan handles live. Everything else on the masking tape, the addresses, the load map, the reach line, came from watching what I actually reached for rather than from the photograph on the box.
