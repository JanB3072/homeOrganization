A three-tier cart is an appealing answer to a small bathroom because it looks like storage that does not need installing. It also has a footprint that moves, which is exactly why people buy one before drawing the room. The cart is not the problem. The floor around it is.

Start with a plan view. This is a common 5 by 8 ft layout, with the door opening inward:

```
 Top view, not to scale. Door swings into the room.

 +------------------------------------------------+
 | shower 32 x 32     :       vanity, 24" deep     |
 |                    :                            |
 +--------------------+                            |
 |                                                 |
 |  [CART 16 x 12"]   <--- 30" clear aisle --->    |
 |                                                 |
 |  toilet                   +--- door arc ------+ |
 |  21" clear in front       |                   | |
 +---------------------------+  30" door         | |
                             +-------------------+ |
```

Two constraints decide everything here. Plumbing codes in most jurisdictions ask for roughly 21 inches of clear floor in front of a toilet and about 15 inches from the bowl centreline to a side wall or fixture. A walkway wants 30 inches, preferably 36, and an inward-opening door sweeps an arc with a radius equal to the door width. Mark that arc on the floor with masking tape before you order anything. In the layout above it rules out the wall beside the toilet, the space in front of the vanity, and the whole quadrant nearest the door. One 16-inch strip of wall survives.

That is not an argument against carts. It is the reason a cart that looked perfect in a listing ends up living in the hallway.

## The dimensions that matter after the floor plan

A typical three-tier utility cart is 14 to 18 inches wide, 12 to 18 deep and 30 to 36 inches tall. The ubiquitous wire-basket version is about 14 by 18 by 31 inches, with roughly 11 inches between shelves and a load rating near 10 kg per tier. Those are the numbers on the box. The number that matters at 7 a.m. is the space you need to stand in to reach the bottom shelf, which is another 18 to 24 inches of floor in front of the cart. A cart pushed tight against a wall is a shelf you cannot crouch in front of.

Height interacts with the room differently. At 31 inches, a cart sits just below a standard vanity top, so it can tuck beside a pedestal sink but will collide with the apron of a cabinet vanity. Check what is above the spot too: a towel bar, a light switch, the swing of a cabinet door. I have seen a cart positioned perfectly on the floor plan and then unable to open because the vanity door needed 20 inches of clearance and the cart's top basket was in the way. The [under-sink layout](/blog/how-to-organize-under-bathroom-sink/) covers that clearance problem from the cabinet side.

The other surprise is the wheels. Casters of 2 to 3 inches roll over tile grout lines and the raised edge of a bath mat, but small hard-plastic wheels catch on a 6 mm mat lip every time, and the cart stops with a jolt that walks everything on the middle shelf forward. TPR or rubber tyres are quieter and grip a wet tile floor better. Get at least two locking casters: in a 30-inch aisle you will brush past the cart most days, and an unbraked one drifts a few centimetres each time until it is sitting in the door arc you measured so carefully.

## What goes on the three tiers

Load order follows stability. A cart tips when the combined centre of gravity moves outside its wheelbase, and the wheelbase of a 14-inch-wide cart is narrow relative to a 31-inch height. Put the dense things on the bottom shelf and the light things on top. Toilet paper multipacks, spare shampoo, and cleaning bottles belong low. A canvas tote hooked over one side rail, which is the most common accessory sold for these carts, is the worst possible load: it is off-centre, it swings, and it adds a lever arm at exactly the height where the cart is least stable.

Middle shelf, between roughly 12 and 24 inches off the floor, is the working zone. That is where daily hand towels, washcloths, and the things you actually reach for go. A standard toilet roll is about 4.5 to 5 inches in diameter and 4 inches wide, so a 14 by 18 inch basket holds a dozen loose rolls in two layers, or one multipack with room left over. Folded bath towels run about 12 by 8 by 4 inches each; three of them fill a tier. The [towel storage plan](/blog/small-bathroom-towel-storage-solutions/) is worth reading here, because the same "one category per zone" rule stops a cart turning into a stack of mixed textiles you have to unpack to reach the flannel at the bottom.

Top tier is for light, dry, occasional things: spare soap, a first-aid box, a sealed container of cotton pads. Paper goods want to be at least a foot off the floor and away from the toilet, partly because a mop or a splash reaches the bottom shelf and partly because of the aerosol plume from flushing with the lid up. I would not store an open packet of cotton rounds on any open tier directly beside the bowl. A lidded plastic box on the top shelf costs a few dollars and solves it.

Ventilation is the second tier decision. Wire or perforated baskets let damp items dry; solid plastic trays hold a puddle and grow a film. Rolled towels stored slightly damp in a solid tray will smell within a couple of days in a room that hits 80 percent relative humidity after a shower. The material notes in the [shelf reset](/blog/diy-bathroom-shelf-organization/) apply directly: non-porous, wipeable, and nothing woven that holds moisture in its fibres.

## What the bathroom does to the cart

A bathroom is a corrosive room for cheap metal. Chrome plate and powder coat on carbon steel are fine until moisture finds a pinhole at a weld, a basket corner, or the socket where a caster screws in. Those are the first places to show orange, because water sits there and the plating is thinnest where the wire was bent and welded. Chloride from tap water and cleaning sprays speeds it up. If you mix metals, say stainless fasteners in a painted steel frame, the damp joint becomes a small galvanic cell and the less noble metal goes first.

A 304 stainless cart or one with polymer shelves costs two to three times as much, roughly $80 to $150 against $25 to $60 for the wire-basket version, and will outlast several cheap ones. The middle path is to buy the cheap cart and treat it as consumable: dry the bottom tray after mopping, do not leave wet bottles standing on a shelf, and check the caster sockets twice a year. Hair and lint wind around caster axles in a bathroom, which is a five-minute job with scissors but eventually seizes the wheel if ignored. The same plated-steel failure shows up on [shower caddies](/blog/hanging-shower-caddy-storage-hacks/) a foot away, only there the load is hanging instead of rolling.

One more friction point that nobody mentions in the listings: a cart has to be moved to clean behind it. If the gap between the cart and the wall is under 4 inches, you will not get a cloth or a mop head in there, and the floor behind it stays dusty. Either leave a hand's width of clearance or accept that the cart gets pulled out every Saturday.

## The three positions, honestly assessed

Beside the vanity is the most convenient and the most likely to fail, because it competes with cabinet doors, the waste bin, and the place you stand to brush your teeth. It works if the vanity is a pedestal or a wall-hung unit with clear floor beside it.

Behind the door is the classic small-room answer, and it only works if the door opens outward or the cart sits outside the swing arc. Tape the arc. On a 30-inch door it covers a surprisingly large quarter-circle, and a cart that clears the door when closed will be hit by it every time someone comes in.

Along the wall beside the toilet is viable when the 15-inch side clearance survives after the cart is in place, and it is the position where an [over-the-toilet rack](/blog/over-the-toilet-storage-ideas/) is the better use of the same air. If the vertical space above the tank is free, a fixed shelf there stores the same towels with zero floor footprint, and the cart becomes unnecessary. That is worth checking before buying anything, because a lot of small bathrooms have more unused wall than unused floor.

## When the cart is the wrong answer

If the clear aisle after placing the cart is under 30 inches, you will sidle past it every morning and eventually move it somewhere else. If the only free wall is inside the door arc, same result. A thick bath mat that the wheels catch on, a toddler who will treat a wheeled tower as a toy, or a rental with a narrow doorway all argue for a fixed shelf instead. And if the real shortage is cabinet space rather than floor space, a cart adds a surface to dust without removing the pile under the sink.

The [bathroom storage section](/bathroom/storage/) covers the fixed options. For a 5 by 8 ft room with one 16-inch strip of wall, a 14-inch cart with brakes, heavy rolls on the bottom and a lidded box on top is a genuinely useful piece of furniture. It just needs the tape measure before the checkout, not after the box arrives.
