Last April I did the math on my king duvet and did not like the answer. Folded on the closet's top shelf, it ate about 13 inches of height and two thirds of the shelf depth, and it left exactly enough room next to it for one pillow, which meant the spare blankets lived on the floor of the closet for the summer. I had a trunk doing nothing but holding board games, a shelf with 14 inches of clearance, and a decision to make about how a 13-inch object fits into a 14-inch shelf without occupying the whole closet.

Storage bags were the answer, but not the way the packaging implies. I bought two kinds, returned one, spent about $22 keeping the other, and learned along the way that a zippered bag is a specific tool with specific promises, some of which it keeps and some of which it quietly does not. This article goes through each promise the way I wish a review would: what the bag claims, what actually happens, and the verdict, because "storage bags work" is a sentence that hides everything useful. One boundary note up front, since the review article on this site covers it fully: this piece is about zippered non-vacuum bags, the kind in the photo above, and the [vacuum bag system](/blog/vacuum-storage-bags-for-comforters-review/) is a different tool with a different contract, better compression and worse gentleness.

## The claim on the front, translated

Every bag's packaging promises the same bundle: compresses bulky items, protects from dust and moths, keeps things clean and fresh, fits anywhere. That bundle is really five separate promises wearing one label, and each one has its own terms and conditions. The bag I kept, a six-pack of clear vinyl zip bags about $22 when I priced them in October, is the test subject, and the storage locations are the two the packaging itself suggests: the top shelf and the trunk.

## Claim one: it compresses

The honest mechanism first, because it explains every number after. A zippered bag compresses nothing. You compress the contents, by pressing and rolling the air out, and the bag's job is to keep the air from coming back. That is why the compression is gentle: a duvet pressed down to half its loft is squeezed by the fabric around it, not by atmospheric pressure pinning it to a board.

The numbers from my own closet: the king duvet went from 13 inches to 6, the folded sweater stack lost about 40 percent of its height, and the spare pillow went from round to about 3 inches of flat. Roughly half is what to plan around; anything more belongs to vacuum territory. And the fill matters more than the fabric. Down recovers from a season at half loft with an afternoon of fluffing. Down held at a tenth of loft for six months inside a vacuum bag sulks, and some of it never fully comes back, which is the entire reason my down duvet zip-bags and my synthetic comforter vacuums. Synthetics forgive crushing; down forgives pressure but not imprisonment.

## Claim two: dust and moths

Dust is a full verdict in the bag's favor. A closed vinyl bag is a complete dust seal, which matters more than people expect on a closet's top shelf, the one zone in an apartment where dust settles for a season undisturbed and every unwashed surface wears it by August.

Moths get a fair, not a full. The vinyl body stops a moth physically, but the zipper seam is not airtight, and a determined clothes moth does not need much of a gap. So the system gets a second layer: a cedar block or lavender sachet goes inside each bag, not because lavender kills anything but because it muddies the scent trail that guides a moth to fabric. The real prevention happens before the zipper closes, and it is unglamorous: wash first. Moths eat keratin, which means wool, silk, feathers, and, yes, down, and what draws them to a blanket in the first place is the body oils and stains left on it. An unwashed duvet in a bag is not stored, it is catered.

## Claim three: moisture

This is the claim where the bag works against you, and it is the one the packaging never mentions. A sealed bag keeps moisture in as efficiently as it keeps dust out. Zip a slightly damp duvet inside and by October it carries a mildew smell that no amount of sun fully removes, because the moisture had a whole season to circulate in a closed room.

The fix costs one day and one hand. Everything gets washed, then gets 24 hours of open air, and then the middle of the fill gets checked by hand, not just the cover, because down holds water in its clumps long after the cotton shell feels dry. There is also a smaller honest complaint: cheap PEVA vinyl carries a plastic shower-curtain smell for the first week, which matters if the bag lives in a bedroom. Air the empty bags out on a balcony for a day and the smell mostly leaves. And one material the bag excludes entirely: leather never goes in sealed plastic, for reasons the [leather boots storage guide](/blog/how-to-store-leather-boots-in-closet/) covers in detail, since sealed leather dries out and molds at the same time.

## Claim four: the top shelf

The shelf math is where zip bags beat their competition, because flat is what a shelf rewards. Bagged flat, my two bags of duvet plus sweaters occupy half the height of the same pile loose, and a 14-inch clearance that used to hold a duvet and a prayer now holds the duvet, the spare blankets, and the pillows, all labeled, all visible through the vinyl.

Two rules keep it safe up there. Bags lie flatter and behave better than they stack, so two high is the ceiling, heaviest on the bottom, because a third bag under a shelf's clearance turns the zipper into a structural component, and zippers are not structural. And nothing heavy goes on top of the bag holding the duvet, because sustained weight on a shelf is just a slow vacuum, the exact crush I zip-bagged the down to avoid. Labels face the room. The clarity of the vinyl is the feature that makes bags better than opaque bins on a high shelf, and a handwritten tag on the bag's face means nobody unzips three bags hunting for the spare pillows in November.

## Claim five: the trunk

The trunk is the storage location nobody plans and every small apartment already owns. Mine spent a year as a board game warehouse; now it cycles the off-season bedding, and the bags are the reason it works, because loose fabric in a trunk becomes an archaeology dig while three labeled bags lift out in one motion and the trunk goes back to being furniture the same afternoon.

The trunk has its own physics. Nothing heavy rides on the duvet bag, same shelf rule, since a trunk lid plus a stack of books on top is sustained pressure. The bag sits flat, folded to the trunk's footprint rather than stuffed past it, and the trunk gets opened every month or two, because "sealed and forgotten" is where the moisture claim comes back to bite. Bags under the bed are the third location, and there they compete with rigid boxes; for bedding the flat bags win, for everything else the [under-bed boxes](/blog/under-bed-winter-clothes-storage-boxes/) take it, and the two systems share the space without arguing.

## What I run now

The whole system costs about $22 and one Saturday afternoon. Three bags on the top shelf, duvet plus spare blankets plus pillows, two bags in the trunk holding the heavy sweaters, each bag about three quarters full so the zipper closes without a fight, the slider run twice because the second pass catches the gap the first leaves. Six bags bought, five in service, and the sixth holds beach towels in the trunk's summer rotation. The sweater bags get packed by the [fold rules](/blog/how-to-fold-hoodies-to-save-space/) rather than stuffed, because a hoodie folded into a brick stores flat and a hoodie stuffed into a bag comes out with a memory of its cramming.

## The April switch, and the claim that matters most

None of this is storage if it runs once. The bags live inside a two-date rhythm, the [April and October switch](/blog/how-to-store-winter-clothes-in-summer/) the rest of this closet already runs: out of the bag, onto the bed, the summer set washed and dried and bagged into the space just vacated. That cycle is also the honest answer to the marketing's last implied claim, the one about "long-term storage". A zippered bag is a season-scale tool, not an heirloom vault. Bags get opened, contents get aired, and anything coming out smelling like anything gets washed before it is worn.
