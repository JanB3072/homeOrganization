Flat pans stacked flat is a design failure we all agreed to ignore. Every retrieval lifts four pans to reach one. Every return means lowering a hot pan onto a tower. The fix costs about twenty dollars and one decision, and the decision has a right answer, which is rare in this field.

This choice follows the same furniture logic as the rest of the house, the one documented in the [whole-home organization system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/): the space picks the tool, not the other way around.

## The decision tree

Answer honestly, in order:

```
Is the cabinet at least 12 inches wide inside?
├─ No ──▶Skip the rack. Use a door-mounted tray holder or the
▶        toe-kick slide. A rack in a narrow cabinet is a jam
▶        with extra steps.
└─ Yes ─▶Are most pans standard half-sheets (about 13 x 18 in)?
    ├─ Yes ─▶Fixed 7-slot steel rack, vertical.
    ▶        Cheapest option that never loosens.
    └─ Mixed widths ─▶Adjustable wire tension rack.
        └─ Do cutting boards or muffin tins live here too?
            ├─ Yes ─▶Two racks side by side, or one rack
            ▶        plus a divider zone for boards.
            └─ No ──▶One rack, done.
```

That tree settles the common purchases. A narrow cabinet does not need a better rack; it needs a different product. Mixed pan sizes do not need force; they need slots that move.

## Install it in four steps

1. Measure the interior at three points. Cabinet interiors lie; the back wall is often narrower than the front rail. Record width, depth, and clear height, and note which side the hinge sits on.
2. Sort pans into four piles by shape. Half-sheets, round tins, boards, muffin tins. The pile counts choose the rack, the way pile counts chose my [deep cabinet zones](/blog/how-to-organize-deep-kitchen-cabinets/).
3. Buy for the counts. Seven or more standard pans: fixed steel. Anything else: tension wire. Muffin tins in the mix: wide slots or a second rack.
4. Install 1.5 inches short on every side, load it, and swing the door five times. If anything catches, the rack moves an inch toward the wall, not the pans.

## The rack, in five lines

Materials make the difference between a rack you buy once and a rack you buy twice:

- Heavy-gauge steel wire, epoxy or powder coated; thin wire spreads under pan weight and slots go sloppy.
- No plastic pegs or adhesive feet. Heat and humidity unglue both within a year.
- Fixed slots for uniform pans, tension wires for mixed widths, nothing adjustable that adjusts with plastic.
- A base wide enough to stand free in the cabinet, so the rack does not tip when one slot empties.
- Dividers only as a last resort; a slot per pan beats a shared wall for the reason every [under-sink zone plan](/blog/how-to-organize-under-bathroom-sink/) repeats: visible from above, reachable in one motion, nothing buried.

A freestanding rack skips installation entirely and slides out for cabinet cleaning, which matters more in a baking cabinet than it sounds, because flour dust finds every horizontal surface you own.

## Muffin tins and cutting boards

Muffin tins are the odd shape in the drawer. Cupped, nestable, and wedge-prone. They get their own wide slots, one tin per slot, or they live on a shelf with a divider doing the same job I give folded sweaters in the [closet shelf system](/blog/closet-shelf-dividers-for-folded-sweaters/). Cutting boards are the easy guest: thin boards slide into standard slots beside the pans, thick butcher blocks go vertical against the cabinet wall.

Small strays, piping sheets, cooling racks, the pizza stone, go in a bin at the cabinet floor. A clear bin, the same cheap visibility trick as the [stackable shoe boxes](/blog/clear-plastic-shoe-boxes-stackable-design/).

## Edit before you buy

Count your pans first. Most kitchens own three pans that work and four pans that pretend. Duplicated warped sheets, the muffin tin missing a cup, the brand-free enigma from a previous apartment. The [Swedish death cleaning checklist](/blog/swedish-death-cleaning-method-checklist/) is the honest count before the rack shopping trip, because a rack sized for eight pans when you use three is storage for clutter with better posture.

## Where it goes

The base cabinet nearest the oven. Bakeware travels two steps from rack to oven and nothing crosses the kitchen, the same reach-zone logic as the [pantry spacing blueprint](/blog/walk-in-pantry-layout-ideas-and-spacing-blueprint/). If that cabinet is narrow, the tree above already answered: door mount or the toe-kick, and the overflow question goes to the [under-sink zone plan](/blog/under-sink-storage-plumbing-friendly/) instead.

Vertical is the whole principle. Files stand in the [small closet system](/blog/vertical-closet-system-maximize-small-wardrobe/), plates stand in the fridge plan that matches bins to [12-inch shelf widths](/blog/aesthetic-fridge-organization-tips/), and pans stand here. The same rule wearing three outfits. Flat storage hides, vertical storage shows, and shown pans get used.

Buy the twenty-dollar rack, size it to the cabinet, and let the stack die. It was never a system; it was a standoff.
