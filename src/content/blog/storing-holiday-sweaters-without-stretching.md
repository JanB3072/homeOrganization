The sweater had been on a wire hanger since December, and by the following November the shoulders had two peaks that would not iron out. It was a heavy Fair Isle knit, the kind that weighs close to a pound, and the hanger was sixteen inches wide against a nineteen inch shoulder. Gravity did the rest.

Holiday sweaters are the worst case for this because of the calendar. Most sweaters get worn and rewashed within a season. A snowflake pullover gets worn for six weeks and then sits for ten months, which means the storage decision is a long-term decision, not a temporary one. Fold or hang is not a style question. It is a question about what a knit does under its own weight for three hundred days.

## Why the shoulders go pointy

A knit is a loop structure with no woven backbone. When you hang it, the entire weight of the body and sleeves pulls on two points where the hanger ends meet the shoulder seam. If the hanger is narrower than the shoulder, those points press upward and the fabric above them stretches.

Two numbers decide how bad it gets. The first is the sweater's weight, which runs 10 to 14 ounces for a light merino and 24 to 32 ounces for a heavy intarsia or a beaded holiday knit. The second is the difference between the hanger width and your shoulder width. A gap of an inch and a half is enough to leave a visible peak after a month.

## The short answer

Fold it. For anything stored longer than a few weeks, folding wins, and a holiday sweater is by definition a long storage item.

| Situation | Fold | Hang |
|---|---|---|
| Stored 6 to 10 months | yes | no |
| Heavy intarsia or beaded | yes | no |
| Thin merino, worn this week | fine | yes |
| Sequins or appliqué | flat, with tissue | no |
| No shelf space at all | stacked box | last resort |

The one honest exception is a light, unstructured knit that you plan to wear within the week. That is display, not storage.

## The head-to-head

| Criterion | Folding | Hanging |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder shape | preserved | peaks in 4 to 6 weeks |
| Dust | covered in a box | exposed on the rod |
| Moths | sealed box plus cedar | open to the closet |
| Space | 8 sweaters on one shelf | 8 sweaters take 16 to 20 in of rod |
| Creasing | fold lines on intarsia | none |
| Access | lift the stack | slide one out |

The trade is clear. Hanging wins on access and creasing, and loses on the two things that actually ruin a sweater: shape and moths.

## When hanging is the better call

There are real cases. A thin cashmere or merino cardigan with a structured shoulder can hang for a week without harm. A sweater you are airing after wear should hang, because a folded damp knit grows mildew in the fold. And if the closet has no shelf at all, a padded hanger is better than a pile on a chair.

If you do hang, use a padded hanger that matches the shoulder width, and never hang a wet knit. The [heavy-coat hanger](/blog/best-space-saving-hangers-for-heavy-coats/) logic applies to the width, but a coat hanger is too rigid for a knit. The padded version distributes weight instead of concentrating it.

## The fold that does not crack the pattern

The standard fold works for a solid color and fails for intarsia. A Fair Isle or snowflake pattern is knitted in blocks, and a hard fold across a motif for ten months leaves a permanent line that reads as a crack.

1. Lay the sweater face down and smooth the back.
2. Fold the sleeves in at the shoulder seam, not at the elbow.
3. Fold in thirds, not halves, so the crease lands between motifs rather than through one.
4. Slide a sheet of acid-free tissue into each fold.
5. Stack no more than four high, and put the heaviest at the bottom.

For beaded or sequined holiday sweaters, skip the fold entirely. Roll the sweater around a tissue core, or store it flat in a single layer with tissue between it and anything hard. The [shelf divider approach](/blog/closet-shelf-dividers-for-folded-sweaters/) keeps a short stack from collapsing into a pile, and a [mesh divider box](/blog/wardrobe-clothes-organizer-with-mesh-dividers/) keeps the stack upright on a high shelf.

## Ten months is the real problem

The stretch is visible. The moth damage is not, until you pull the sweater out in November and find three holes under the arm.

Holiday sweaters are usually wool or a wool blend, and wool is protein. Moths eat protein, and they prefer the collar, cuffs, and underarms where body oil collects. Wash before storage, dry fully, then freeze for 48 hours if you have seen a moth anywhere in the closet. Cedar repels adults but does not kill larvae, which is the same limit the [cashmere care routine](/blog/how-to-wash-and-store-cashmere-sweaters/) runs into.

Store in a breathable box, not a vacuum bag. Compression crushes the loft and the pattern, and a sealed bag holds any moisture in. Label the box, put it high, and set a reminder to check it in March, which is the same rhythm as the [seasonal rotation checklist](/blog/seasonal-closet-rotation-checklist-printable/) and the [winter-to-summer swap](/blog/how-to-store-winter-clothes-in-summer/).

## What I do now

Seven holiday sweaters, one shelf, one box. The heavy Fair Isle goes at the bottom of the stack, folded in thirds with tissue in the creases. The beaded one lies flat in its own box. The thin merino hangs for the week of the party and goes back in the stack after.

The pointy shoulders stopped the year I threw out the wire hangers. That was the whole fix, and it cost nothing. Fold the heavy ones, hang the light one for a week, and give the box a check in March. Ten months is a long time for a knit to hold its shape on its own.
