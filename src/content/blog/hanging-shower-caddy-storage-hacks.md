The caddy came down at 6:40 on a Tuesday. I remember the time because I was late for a train and had shampoo in my eye. Two suction cups, a wire basket, maybe five bottles. There was a crack, a slide, and the whole thing landed on the tap. One plastic bottle split at the shoulder. Nothing broke, but I spent ten minutes picking conditioner out of the drain cover, and I stopped trusting suction cups that morning.

What bothered me afterwards was that the cups had held for four months. If they were simply bad, they would have failed in week one. So I looked at what changed.

## Why a cup that holds in June lets go in October

A suction cup works on pressure difference. Press it against a smooth surface, squeeze the air out, and atmospheric pressure pushes the cup against the wall. That is all. There is no adhesive involved, which is why the same cup can be peeled off and reused, and also why its hold depends entirely on the seal staying airtight.

Bathroom tile is rarely as smooth as it looks. Glazed ceramic is fine if the glaze is intact and the tile is flat, but a lot of shower walls use a slight texture for slip resistance, and grout lines are porous. A textured surface has microscopic channels running from the outside air to the middle of the cup. Air does not rush in; it creeps, over hours. Warm water softens the PVC lip, cooler air hardens it again, and after a few hundred hot-cold cycles the lip no longer conforms to the surface the way it did out of the packet. Condensation behind the seal speeds the whole thing up.

The load matters more than the surface, and this is the part the packaging never explains. A rating like "holds 5 kg" is measured with a weight hanging straight down, close to the wall, on dry polished tile in a lab. In a shower, bottles sit on the outer edge of a basket that projects 4 or 5 inches. That turns a shear load into a torque: the bottom of the cup gets pressed in while the top edge gets pulled away. Peel strength for a suction cup is a fraction of its shear strength. A caddy carrying well under its rated weight can therefore peel off one corner at a time, which is exactly the slow failure I watched happen.

Cheap adhesive hooks have the same weakness with a different mechanism. The foam tape sticks fine to clean dry tile, then sits under a daily sauna. Moisture gets into the foam, the adhesive plasticizes, and the hook gradually slides down the wall leaving a pale shadow. I have had better luck with them on painted drywall outside the shower than inside it.

## What actually holds

The shower arm is the sturdiest thing in the enclosure that you are allowed to touch. It is a 1/2-inch NPT pipe screwed into a drop-ear elbow behind the wall, usually brass or zinc-plated steel, and it already carries the weight of the showerhead. An over-the-arm hanger slips a collar over that pipe and hangs the basket below it. There are no holes, no adhesive, and no surface to fail. The limit is the arm itself, not the hanger: a long, thin decorative arm with a heavy rainfall head at the end is already carrying a fair bit of leverage, and adding 4 kg of bottles at the neck is asking a lot of a threaded joint. Keep the total under about 10 to 15 lb, which sounds generous until you weigh the bottles.

I did weigh mine, out of curiosity. A 500 ml shampoo bottle full is roughly 550 g. The 750 ml "value" size is close to 800 g. Add a conditioner, a body wash, a shave gel, a tube of face wash, a razor, and the steel basket itself, and I was at 4.3 kg before the sixth bottle. That is 9.5 lb. People who say their caddy is "not that full" usually have not put it on a scale.

A tension pole caddy is the other real answer, especially in a tub-shower combo where the arm is low or the head is on a slide bar. It is the floor-to-ceiling version of an [over-the-toilet rack](/blog/over-the-toilet-storage-ideas/), with the same warning about what the top foot is pressing against. A spring-loaded pole wedges between the floor of the tub and the ceiling, with rubber feet at each end, and baskets clip onto it at whatever heights you like. It holds more than an arm hanger and lets you put the heavy bottles low. Two conditions apply. The ceiling has to be something solid: a plasterboard soffit or a suspended ceiling tile will dent under the top foot, and the pole will slowly work itself loose. And the pole has to be plumb; a few degrees of lean puts a constant side load on the spring, which is why the cheap ones start to sag after a year. I would check the tension every couple of months, the same way you would check a shower curtain rod.

Drilled shelves are the permanent option, and in a tiled shower that means a diamond bit, a steady hand, and a willingness to hit a tile you cannot easily replace. Worth it in a house you own, when the enclosure is big enough that nobody will bump into a fixed shelf. In a rental, or in a 32-inch neo-angle stall, no.

## Arranging what is left

Once the thing is secure, the interesting problem is that most caddies hold far more than anyone needs. Mine had eleven items in it. Four were half-empty duplicates: two shampoos because I bought a new one before finishing the old, a body wash nobody liked, and a hotel-size conditioner kept "for travel" for two years.

I now keep one open bottle per person per category. Backups live in the cabinet under the sink, which is dry, dark, and not steamed twice a day. The [under-sink zone map](/blog/how-to-organize-under-bathroom-sink/) is the reason that cabinet, rather than a second shower shelf, is where the overflow belongs. That single rule removed more clutter than any organizer I have bought. It also removed the need for a bigger caddy, which is worth saying because the first instinct when a shelf is crowded is to shop for a larger shelf.

Weight distribution is the next thing. Heavy bottles on the bottom shelf and near the wall, light things up top. A caddy is a pendulum when it swings, and a 750 ml bottle at the outer edge of the top shelf is what starts the swaying. Razors go in a small hook or a mesh pocket, not loose on a wire shelf where they slide. The [towel storage plan](/blog/small-bathroom-towel-storage-solutions/) uses the same hook-and-rail logic for anything that has to dry between uses. Bar soap needs a draining dish with ridges; flat on a wire shelf it melts into a translucent smear that runs down to the shelf beneath and glues the bottles to the wire.

Position in the stall matters too. I moved the whole hanger about six inches out of the direct spray. It is still within reach, but it no longer gets hammered by hot water for ten minutes every morning. Six months on, the white crust on the bottom tray is much slower to come back. That crust is worth understanding: soap is a fatty-acid salt, and in hard water it reacts with calcium and magnesium to make insoluble stearates that stick to metal. Evaporated droplets leave calcium carbonate on top. So it is a layered deposit, which is why scrubbing with one cleaner is disappointing. A soak in citric acid or white vinegar takes off the mineral layer; dish detergent and a brush deal with the soap film. Do them in that order, and never in the same bowl as bleach, because acid plus hypochlorite releases chlorine gas.

## The maintenance that actually happens

I tried a weekly wipe-down schedule once. It lasted eleven days. What has stuck is tying the job to something already happening: on the first Saturday of the month I take the caddy off the arm, empty it, and put the whole basket in the sink with warm water and a scoop of citric acid while I clean the shower glass. Twenty minutes of soaking, a quick brush, rinse, rehang. Baskets with open wire or perforated shelves make this easy because water drains through; solid plastic trays with a lip hold a puddle, and that puddle is where the biofilm starts.

While it is off, I check the collar for rust at the weld and look at the shower arm where the hanger sits. Plated steel in a chlorinated, humid room will pit eventually, and a brown streak on the arm means the plating is gone. A caddy with 304 stainless or a decent powder coat costs maybe ten dollars more and does not leave orange marks on the tile. It is the same material call as the [under-sink comparison](/blog/under-sink-storage-plumbing-friendly/), where plated steel meets the same damp, chlorinated air. Aluminium ones are light and never rust, though the thin extruded ones bend if you overload them.

There are showers where none of this applies. A frameless glass enclosure with no overhead arm and no place to wedge a pole is better served by a recessed niche or a small corner stool. A rain head on a ceiling-mounted arm gives you nothing to hang from. And if two people share a 30-inch stall, a hanging basket at chest height is going to be knocked with an elbow every single morning, however well it is anchored.

For the ordinary tub-shower with a wall-mounted arm, though, the fix is unglamorous and cheap: hang it from the pipe, weigh the bottles, keep one of each, and move it out of the spray. The rest of the [bathroom storage guides](/bathroom/storage/) deal with the cabinet and shelf half of the room; this is only the wet corner. I have had the same setup for two years now. It has not fallen once. I still would not put a suction cup anywhere near my shampoo.
