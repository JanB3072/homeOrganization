> Sunday, 7:40 pm. Six compartments, twenty-nine folded shirts, three of them already leaning into the next slot. The box had been on the shelf for forty hours.

I wrote that on the back of a laundry receipt because nothing had been shoved in and the box was new. By the third evening the middle dividers had bowed and a grey t-shirt had slid into the black row beside it. It turned out to be a measurement problem. A mesh divider is a soft wall with a number written into it, and mine was too small. Once I found the number, six filled slots became four, and the two spare ones turned out to be the useful part.

## Why did the stack lean on the third day?

A thin cotton shirt folds to about 15 mm. Eight of them make 120 mm. Dividers in a six-slot mesh box stand 100 to 120 mm, because the box itself is about 150 mm high and the panels stop below the rim so you can see over them. A stack as tall as its own wall has nothing to lean on but the next stack. It did not collapse in one moment, either. Every time I lifted a shirt out, my fingers pressed the top of the pile sideways, and each visit moved the top two shirts a few millimetres further over.

The rule I use now is divider height at least 1.2 times the stack height, which for 120 mm is 145 mm. I could not buy a taller mesh box in this footprint, so I lowered the stack instead: eight shirts, never nine. Nine was Sunday's count, and nine is where the grey shirt started to travel. The panels do have a thin plastic stiffener sewn inside, which is what holds a full slot upright. Compare [shelf dividers for folded sweaters](/blog/closet-shelf-dividers-for-folded-sweaters/), where a rigid upright does what a fabric flap cannot, or the [wooden accessory tray](/blog/closet-accessory-tray-configuration/) in the other drawer, where the walls never bow at all.

## What does the mesh do that a solid box does not?

You can see through it, it weighs about 400 g empty against roughly 1.1 kg for a rigid plastic divider of the same footprint, and it breathes. Shirts out of the dryer carry warmth and a little moisture. In a closed nonwoven bin that damp sits against the fabric until Tuesday, and the bottom shirt comes out smelling of nothing but dryer sheet. Mesh lets it go.

That only works if the clothes cool first. I now leave the folded pile on the dresser for ten to fifteen minutes before it goes into the box. Putting warm laundry into any container just moves the damp from the air into the fabric. I made the same mistake with [collapsible fabric bins with lids](/blog/collapsible-storage-bins-with-lids-fabric-stackable/) and with [under-bed boxes for winter clothes](/blog/under-bed-winter-clothes-storage-boxes/), where the fix was to wait rather than to buy a different container. [Vacuum bags for a comforter](/blog/vacuum-storage-bags-for-comforters-review/) are the extreme version: no air at all, right for a dry duvet, wrong for something worn last week.

## How wide is a folded t-shirt, really?

A file fold turns a shirt roughly 300 mm wide into a packet about 200 by 150 mm. Thickness does all the variation. A 140 gsm cotton tee folds to 12 mm, a 200 gsm one to 20 mm, and a 280 gsm hoodie to 30 mm. So a compartment needs at least 210 mm of clear width and about 160 mm of depth for the fold to sit without curling at the front edge.

A standard 400 by 250 mm box with two columns of three slots gives roughly 200 mm per slot, right at the limit, which is why one hoodie bulges the neighbouring wall by a few millimetres. Hoodies go in the [folded hoodie drawer](/blog/how-to-fold-hoodies-to-save-space/) and jeans on their own shelf using the [denim stacking method](/blog/how-to-organize-jeans-on-closet-shelves/). Grouping by folded thickness rather than by colour keeps every stack the same height, and equal stack heights are what let eight shirts stay put.

## Does a divider need to reach the top?

No. It needs to reach past the top of the clothes, which is a different number. A wall that goes all the way to the rim would hide the row behind it and make the box harder to reach into. The more useful question is what holds the top edge of a soft panel.

Each divider is stitched to the base and to the two stiffened outer walls. The middle panels have no anchor at the front rim. A full slot braces its own panel with the shirts inside it. An empty slot leaves that panel with nothing on one side, so it bows 5 to 10 mm toward the gap, and over a week the bow sets into a crease. Product photographs always show every compartment full, so they never show this. A [vertical closet system](/blog/vertical-closet-system-maximize-small-wardrobe/) has uprights fixed at both ends and never bows. Fabric boxes trade that rigidity for weight and price, which means the empty slots have to be managed rather than ignored.

## What should sit in an empty compartment?

Something rolled, and placed in the middle of the box. My first instinct was to leave the two spares at the ends, out of the way. That let both outer dividers bow inward at once and squeezed the four filled slots between them. Moving the empties to the centre changed the geometry: the filled slots now sit against the stiffened outer walls, and the two soft middle panels brace each other.

I keep a rolled hand towel standing in one empty slot, about 90 mm across and 140 mm tall. It props the panels apart without pushing them out of shape. The other slot stays genuinely empty, so a new shirt or one waiting for a repair has somewhere to go. A box with no empty slot ends up with a stranger balanced on top of a stack, which is how the leaning starts again. The [twenty-minute decluttering rule](/blog/20-20-decluttering-rule-explained/) is my threshold for what leaves before that happens.

## Which fabrics snag on mesh?

Cheap mesh openings run 2 to 3 mm across. A ribbed cuff, a modal tee or a fine-gauge knit has loops small enough to catch in that grid, and pulling the garment out draws a thread with it. I found two pulled loops on a grey ribbed shirt in the first month, both along the folded edge that had been resting against a panel.

Openings at or under 1 mm behave differently, and a thin cotton square cut from an old pillowcase works even on coarse mesh. I laid two squares in the slots that hold knits. They are not attached to anything, so they come out with the laundry. Smooth heavy things are better on a [hanger built for weight](/blog/best-space-saving-hangers-for-heavy-coats/) than folded into a fabric grid at all, and anything leaving the weekly rotation belongs in [breathable bags for bulky clothes](/blog/space-saving-storage-bags-for-blankets-bulky-clothes/) instead of a shallow shelf box that gets opened every morning. Mesh is for clothes in use. Washing the box itself is a cool-water hand job with the stiffeners pulled out; a machine cycle folds those thin sheets, and a creased stiffener never stands straight again.

## How many compartments does one shelf need?

Fewer than the box offers. My shelf is 350 mm deep, so a 400 mm box has to turn sideways, which turns six slots into two rows of three. The back row is then harder to reach and quietly becomes storage. I use one box per shelf and four filled slots.

Counting from the clothes gives a clearer answer than counting the product: twenty-nine shirts at eight per slot need four slots, while six would hold forty-eight, more than I own and more than I want in one place. The [capsule wardrobe layout](/blog/capsule-wardrobe-closet-layout-design/) starts from a similar count, which is why I never bought a second box. Cost is worth putting here too. A mesh six-slot box was nineteen dollars, a nonwoven hard-sided box fourteen, and a rigid plastic divider twenty-four. The nonwoven hid everything and held damp. The plastic was too heavy for the shelf span. The mesh needed two cotton squares and a rolled towel that I already had.

## Where on the shelf should the box sit?

Near a shelf end, not in the middle of a long span. Loaded, this box is about 2.2 kg, and 15 mm particleboard over a 600 mm span bows under a central point load long before it fails. Set it over the end support and the same weight goes straight into the upright.

Height matters more than I expected. Between shoulder and waist I can see into every slot and lift a shirt without tipping the box. Above shoulder height I can see the front row and nothing else, and the back row stops being used within a fortnight. That is the same finding the [whole-home system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) notes about anything stored overhead: if reaching it needs a decision, it will not be reached.

The box in the photograph now goes back with four filled slots, two folded flat in the middle, a rolled hand towel standing in one of them, and every collar facing up so the dark shirts can be told apart from above.
