The first thing to clear up is the reason the question is hard to answer. MOB Kitchen is not a place you can walk into. It has no dining room, no host stand, no printed menu and no delivery window. It is a British recipe platform, and the word kitchen in the name is doing a lot of misleading work.

Once you know that, finding its "menu" is easy. The recipes live on mobkitchen.co.uk, grouped into collections like Dinner, Packed Lunches and Meal Prep. Individual dishes have their own pages, and there is a planner with a shopping list on the sister site mob.co.uk. This piece explains how the brand got there, where to look, and how to use the recipes in a small kitchen without turning the counter into a disaster.

## The misunderstanding, stated plainly

Searching for a restaurant menu when the brand is a content site produces a particular kind of frustration. You find directory pages with addresses that do not exist, or lists of dishes with no order button. The problem is not that the information is hidden. It is that the question assumes the wrong kind of business.

MOB began in 2016. The Telegraph reported that Ben Lebus, then a university student, noticed that none of his friends were cooking and started making recipe videos with his phone. The aim was to help busy young people cook good food without spending much. The Evening Standard described the early proposition as meals for four people under ten pounds. That origin explains the whole brand: cheap, fast, filmed, and shared online.

By 2020 the company had built a proper website. Its own welcome post, dated 1 July 2020, describes a new cooking space that brought existing content together and added profiles, recipes, weekly articles and a shopping list. The same post mentions four MOB books and a shop. Nothing in that description is a restaurant.

## What people mean when they say menu

On the MOB site, the closest word to menu is collection. The homepage I checked showed sections named Dinner sorted, New Recipes, High-Protein Dinners, Packed Lunches, Make-Ahead Breakfasts, Quick and Healthy Dinners, Meal Prep made easy, Healthy Snacks and Get Ahead. There are also batch-cooking collections, including one called One Batch, Multiple Dinners and another for reheating leftovers.

Individual recipes sit at clear addresses. A Thai Green Chicken Curry is at /recipes/thai-green-chicken-curry, a Spicy Chorizo and Pepper Stew at /recipes/spicy-chorizo-pepper-stew, and a batch of Overnight Oats at /recipes/pb-j-overnight-oats-with-a-chocolate-smash-lid. Those pages are the menu in practical terms: ingredients, method, and often a video.

There is a second use of the word menu on the older MOB website. The 2020 post describes a feature allowing users to save recipes into different menus in their profiles. That is a personal collection, closer to a pinboard than a restaurant card. If you read an old article mentioning MOB menus, this is probably what it meant.

Diet filters make the archive easier to navigate. Recipe cards on the homepage carried tags including Vegan, Vegetarian, Gluten-Free, Dairy-Free, Nut-Free and Shellfish-Free. That is a real improvement over the early video era, when you had to watch a clip and hope it suited you.

## Who actually writes the recipes

One detail surprised me. The site does not read like a single author's blog. Its homepage lists a long roll of contributing chefs, including Ben Lebus, Ben Lippett, Ed Smith, Sophie Wyburd, Nicola Lamb, Chloe René, Justin Tsang, Christina Soteriou and many more. Recipe cards name the person responsible for each dish.

That matters when you are deciding whether a recipe is trustworthy. A site with one founder and thirty contributors is a different proposition from a person cooking for their own audience. It also explains the range: the homepage mixes meal-prep recipes, puddings, packed lunches and contributions from brands such as ZOE. You are not getting one person's idea of dinner. You are getting a magazine-style selection.

The founder is still present. Ben Lebus appears in the chef list, and the press coverage of the 2016 origin names him as the person who started it. But treating every recipe as his work would be wrong, and it would miss the point of what the site has become.

## A practical way to use the archive

If you arrived here wanting dinner tonight, here is the shortest route. Open mobkitchen.co.uk, choose Dinner or Quick and Healthy Dinners, then filter by whatever you actually need. Pick one recipe. Add its ingredients to the shopping list at mob.co.uk/planner rather than opening five tabs and trying to remember them all.

The planner is the part most people overlook. It is more useful than the recipes themselves if you cook for a household, because it turns a set of dishes into one list. The 2020 welcome post describes the ability to add ingredients from a recipe to a shopping list and tick them off while shopping. That is a small feature with a large effect on how often you follow a recipe instead of improvising at the fridge.

If you are new to this kind of cooking, start with a traybake or a one-pot dish. The site has a whole collection of them, and they suit a small kitchen because the washing up is short. A recipe that uses one baking sheet and a bowl is more likely to be repeated than one that uses four pans and a blender. I made the same argument in the [baking sheet organizer guide](/blog/kitchen-baking-sheet-organizer-rack-vertical-cabinet/): if the equipment is awkward to reach, the recipe quietly disappears from the rotation. The [pull-out shelf guide](/blog/diy-pull-out-shelves-for-kitchen-cabinets/) solves the same problem when the tray lives in a deep lower cupboard.

## The small-kitchen problem with recipe sites

Recipe platforms make food look easy. The photographs show a finished plate, a clean worktop and a person who seems to have appeared in the kitchen only once. Real cooking leaves a trail: packaging, a half-used herb, three bowls and a tray that needs a home.

The way to handle that is not to cook less. It is to decide where the ingredients go before you start. A [clear bin](/blog/best-clear-plastic-bins-for-fridge-storage/) in the fridge makes it obvious what needs using up, and the [fridge zone guide](/kitchen/fridge/) sorts the cold side by what happens where. On the dry side, the [small pantry ideas](/blog/small-pantry-cabinet-organization-hacks-for-apartment/) show how a narrow cupboard can hold a week's worth of ingredients without hiding them.

Batch cooking deserves a mention because MOB publishes so much of it. A batch recipe only saves time if the containers are ready. A [label](/blog/how-to-label-pantry-jars-custom-vinyl-stickers/) and a stack of boxes turn leftover soup into next Tuesday's lunch. Without them it becomes an unlabelled container at the back of the fridge, which is a different kind of waste. The [airtight container comparison](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers-for-flour-and-sugar/) is worth reading before you buy a set, and the [cabinet zone guide](/kitchen/cabinets/) helps decide where the set lives.

There is one more trap. Recipe sites encourage you to save everything. MOB lets you save recipes and group them into menus, which is genuinely useful until you have two hundred saved dinners and cook none of them. The [twenty-minute rule](/blog/20-20-decluttering-rule-explained/) applies to digital clutter as well: if a saved recipe has not been cooked in a month, it is entertainment, not a plan.

## Why the name keeps causing confusion

Kitchen in a brand name is a shortcut. It tells you the subject matter and nothing about the business model. MOB Kitchen is a recipe publisher, a book brand, a video channel and a planner. It is not a place to eat, and no amount of searching for a table will change that.

The useful answer to "where is its menu" is therefore a set of links rather than an address. The recipe archive, the collections and the planner are where the value sits. If you want cheap meals for four, start there and ignore anything that looks like a restaurant directory.

The rest of this site is about making the room those recipes happen in work a little better. The [kitchen hub](/kitchen/) groups it by zone, and the [whole-home organization system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) takes the same approach across the apartment. A good recipe and a workable kitchen are two separate problems, and solving the second one makes the first one far more likely to happen.
