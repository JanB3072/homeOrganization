The first time I moved in Chicago, I labeled zero boxes. I wrote KITCHEN on boxes that contained bathroom towels and BEDROOM on boxes containing the espresso machine, because by hour nine of packing I was writing words to feel finished rather than to communicate anything. On night one in the new apartment I slept on a bare mattress. The sheets were found on day three, in a box marked, I am not making this up, MISC 2. That move cost me four days of living out of a duffel and one genuine crying session over a can opener.

The second move, into the one bedroom apartment I am typing in right now, ran on a system: 34 boxes, every one numbered and legible from three angles, a phone ledger that found the box opener in under a minute, and a first night box that made the first morning almost pleasant. Same person, same amount of stuff, radically different move. The moving packing tips below are that second move, written down so you can skip the first one. A move isn't a Saturday, it's a system, and 34 boxes became something I could actually find.

## Before one piece of tape: the purge that pays you

Movers bill by the hour, and every box you carry is a box you buy, tape, lift, truck, carry again and unpack. The pre-move purge is the only step in the entire process that saves money at every later stage, so it goes first, weeks out, and it gets done with the standard [pre-move declutter checklist](/blog/how-to-declutter-before-moving-checklist/) rather than vibes.

If the scale of the mess has you frozen before you begin, the same entry logic from [decluttering when overwhelmed](/blog/how-to-start-decluttering-when-overwhelmed/) applies here: five minutes, one surface, timer. A move is actually the easiest time to purge because the deadline does the motivating. The question for every object changes from the usual "do I like this" to the harder, cheaper one: "is this worth paying to move?" You are paying either in mover hours or in your own spine, and most of a closet fails that test honestly.

Two purge details that matter more than they look:

- **Off season stuff leaves first.** The winter coats and duvets get [vacuum bagged](/blog/vacuum-storage-bags-for-comforters-review/) or boxed into the [under bed containers](/blog/under-bed-winter-clothes-storage-boxes/) at the four week mark, which means half your bedroom is packed before you have made a single daily-life sacrifice.
- **The sentimental stuff gets a scheduled slot, not a pass.** Photos, keepsakes, the box of letters: they go on the calendar for the third week with a time limit, because open ended sentimental sorting is where moves go to die. If it helps, the question from [Swedish death cleaning](/blog/swedish-death-cleaning-method-checklist/) cuts faster than any other frame: who moves this box for me if I do not?

## The label code that actually gets read

Here is the professional secret, and it is not the colored tape. The color helps. The structure is what saves you.

**Line one: the number.** Every box gets a sequential number across the whole move, 1 through 34 in my case, not restarting per room. Numbers make the ledger searchable, make "how many boxes" answerable, and make a missing box obvious the moment the truck unloads.

**Line two: the room.** Written as a destination, the room it goes to in the new place, which is not always the room it came from.

**Line three: one contents line.** Not "misc kitchen." Specific enough to matter: "cutlery drawer, coffee kit, knife roll."

All three lines go on **two sides and the top**, in marker, big. This is the part everyone skips and the part that matters most, because boxes spend moving day in stacks. A label written only on top is a label you will never see again after the second layer. Colored tape per room goes around the box edge so the sorting crew at the new door can route boxes at a glance:

| Tape color | Destination room | My box range | Unpacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blue | Kitchen | 1 to 11 | Day one |
| Green | Bedroom | 12 to 18 | Day one |
| Yellow | Bathroom | 19 to 22 | Day one |
| Red | Living room | 23 to 30 | Week one |
| Plain | Storage / closet | 31 to 34 | Week two |

Tape the legend to the front door of the old place and the new one. Movers and helpers route themselves, and you stop being the human sign.

One detail about the numbering that looks like overkill until you see it done wrong: do not restart the count per room. "Kitchen 1 of 11, Bathroom 1 of 4" sounds tidy and creates four boxes numbered 1, which is how a bathroom box ends up stacked in the kitchen's future spot by a helper who did exactly what the label said. One continuous count across the whole move gives every box a name and only one box has it, which is the entire property a tracking system needs.

## The box ledger, thirty seconds at a time

The tracking system is one note in your phone. As each box closes, one line: number, room, contents, done. "No. 7: kitchen, cutlery drawer, espresso gear, the good knives." Thirty seconds, and the whole ledger for a 34 box move took under twenty minutes total, spread across the packing week.

Two upgrades that cost nothing:

1. **Photograph the open box** right before taping anything fragile or fiddly. Day two, standing in the new kitchen needing the colander, the photo of box 9 shows you exactly which corner of which box to open, and you skip unwrapping eleven things to find it.
2. **Circle the number** of any box that contains "open first" candidates. My circled numbers were 5, 12 and 20, and they came off the truck first and got unpacked before dinner.

The ledger earns its keep again six months later, incidentally, when you cannot remember which sealed box in the storage closet holds the spare cables. That is the same problem the [small clear boxes](/blog/clear-plastic-shoe-boxes-stackable-design/) solve inside drawers, and the ledger solves it at box scale: named, numbered, findable without excavation.

One more professional category that belongs on the list from day one: the things no moving truck will carry. Movers refuse flammables, pressurized cans, bleach, paint thinner and propane, and nobody wants a can of aerosol cooking oil cooking in a truck in July. Medications, documents, jewelry and hard drives ride with you regardless. Keep a running "car pile" from the first packing session so none of it accidentally gets taped into a box. If you hire a company instead of driving the truck, the federal FMCSA keeps the vetting checklist for a mover before you sign [FMCSA](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/), and it is the one step that prevents the worst moving disaster.

## Moving packing tips: the box shopping math nobody does

Boxes get bought twice in most moves: once too early in the wrong sizes, and again mid-pack at a premium from whatever is open late. A little arithmetic up front fixes both.

Rough box counts that hold up in practice: a studio or one bedroom runs 30 to 40 boxes, a two bedroom 50 to 70, and a house with a garage laughs at both numbers. Buy in a ratio, not a stack: about 40 percent small (1.5 cubic foot), 40 percent medium (3 cubic foot), and 20 percent large (4.5 to 6 cubic foot), plus one or two double walled dish packs and one wardrobe box if you own coats worth the box. The small boxes will run out first. They always run out first, because the weight rule keeps funneling dense things into them, so buy a couple more than the ratio suggests.

Two sources and one warning:

- **New boxes** cost about a dollar to three dollars each and stack properly, which matters more than it sounds, because uniform sizes are what turn a truck into tetris instead of a Jenga experiment.
- **Free boxes** from marketplace and liquor stores are genuinely fine for a third of your move, with two caveats. Grocery produce boxes can carry roach eggs, so never take produce or banana boxes into a kitchen. And mismatched sizes do not stack, so sort free boxes by footprint before packing day, not during.
- **The thing not to cheap out on is tape.** Cheap tape on a cold staircase morning peels at the glue line, and a box that opens from the bottom loses everything it owes you. Two rolls of the actual packing tape, applied in the H pattern, outlast three rolls of whatever was on sale.

## The packing calendar, four weeks out

Sequence is the difference between a calm pack and a panic pack. The order that worked, oldest-dust-first:

- **Four weeks out:** off season clothing, spare linens, books, decor, the fireplace basket of candles nobody lights. Your daily life does not touch these. Also the purging pass, room by room, working the same [twice a year rotation](/blog/how-to-store-winter-clothes-in-summer/) that decides what actually earns shelf space at the destination.
- **Three weeks out:** heavy non-dailies. Books into small boxes, twenty paperbacks each, under 40 pounds per box, tested with a one hand lift. The weight rule is simple and repeated on every box: heavy things in small boxes, light things in large ones, and a medium box of books at 50 pounds is how seams fail on stairs and backs fail on trucks.
- **Two weeks out:** the kitchen's back bench, the appliances you use monthly, extra towels, the guest bedding. Daily plates and one pan stay out and travel on the last morning in their own box.
- **One week out:** clothes on hangers get the wardrobe treatment, kept on their hangers and bagged in groups of ten, which works for everything down to the [heavy coats](/blog/best-space-saving-hangers-for-heavy-coats/), and saves two full days of re-hanging at the destination. Bedding beyond tonight's set goes in, and the linen closet empties enough to start feeling like the move is real.
- **Two days out:** everything in the bathroom that is not the daily kit. Everything in the fridge gets eaten or tossed, because a cooler of condiments is a lie you tell yourself. The [list of what to leave behind](/blog/what-to-throw-away-when-moving-house/) is what I use to keep that call short.
- **Last morning:** beds stripped, sheets into the first night box, daily plates and the coffee kit boxed and labeled in blue, and the box that never leaves your sight.

## Fragile things: plates on edge, H tape, no voids

The dish techniques are the ones worth over-learning, because breakage is where DIY moves pay for their savings.

**Plates travel on edge, standing upright like records, never flat.** A plate is strongest across its edge profile and weakest across its face. Flat stacked, one dropped box transfers every impact into the face of the bottom plate, and eleven plates become a mosaic. Upright and individually wrapped in a double walled dish box, the same drop chips one rim. The photo at the top of this article is the technique: divided box, plates vertical, paper stuffed so nothing slides.

**Tape the bottom seam with an H.** One line along the flap seam, then one line across each edge, 40 extra seconds, and the box survives the top-of-the-stairs moment that single-tape boxes famously fail. Every box gets the H before packing, not discovered missing after.

**No newspaper on dishes.** The ink transfers onto ceramic and takes a scrubbing session to remove, so it is plain kraft paper or towels for anything that touches food surfaces. Towels and socks are free padding that also needs moving, which makes them the most honest packing material in the apartment.

**No voids, ever.** Fill every gap with crumpled paper until the contents do not shift when the closed box is shaken gently. Damage is not caused by drops as much as by half an inch of travel space multiplied by a hundred vibrations. A rattle is a repairable defect: open, stuff, re-tape, thirty seconds.

Three neighbors of the plate get their own handling notes, because they fail in specific ways:

- **Glasses and stemware** stand upside down on their rims, stuffed with paper, individually wrapped. Hanging them upside down sounds backwards and is correct: the rim is the strongest circle, and a stuffed bowl cannot implode. Never lay glass on its side, where vibration walks it into the box wall.
- **Lamps** travel in two parts, shade alone in a large box with nothing on top of it, bulb and harp bagged and taped to the base. A shade with a towel pressed on top arrives with a memory of that towel.
- **Framed art and mirrors** get corner protectors or folded cardboard sleeves on all four corners, then travel on edge, never flat, with a blanket face wrapped. Flat framed glass in a truck absorbs every chassis flex; on edge, flex passes across the frame instead of through the pane.

## The first night box, and why it rides in the car

The first night box is the single highest-return item in the whole system. Contents, refined over two moves:

- Sheets for one bed, one towel each, one change of clothes each
- Medications and toiletries, toilet paper, soap, hand towel
- Phone chargers, laptop charger, a power strip
- The coffee kit: pour-over, grounds, two mugs. Day one runs on this
- Basic tools, scissors, a box cutter for the actual unpacking
- Two lightbulbs and a spare fuse, because pre-war buildings
- Paper towels and all purpose spray, for the wipe-down every new kitchen needs

Write OPEN FIRST on two sides, put it in the car with the car pile, and guard it. The truck can be late; the first night box cannot. It converts the first evening from a 90 minute excavation into a normal night, which matters more on day one than any other convenience in the whole move.

## Seven mistakes I made so you can skip them

Most packing failures are the same seven, and I have personally committed at least five of them:

1. **Labeling only the top.** Boxes stack. A top label is buried from box two onward. Sides and top, always.
2. **Overfilled medium boxes.** The box survives; the bottom seam or your lower back does not. The 40 pound rule with the one hand lift test, no exceptions for enthusiasm.
3. **Packing the purge-able stuff.** Every box of "decide later" items is moving your indecision at about three dollars a box. The purge comes first precisely so this box does not exist.
4. **Newspaper on dishes.** Ink on every plate face, and a scrubbing evening at the destination where you wanted none.
5. **No first night box.** The bare mattress of 2019, the MISC 2 sheets of day three. Never again, and it rides in the car.
6. **Unpacking everything on day one.** The burst dies by 4 p.m. and the rest of the boxes become scenery. Kitchen, beds, bathroom, stop. The rest is week one and it goes better for the rest.
7. **Forgetting the car pile.** Something flammable or essential taped into a random box, discovered at the worst moment. The car pile starts on packing day one and stays visible.

## Unpacking order: the four hour kitchen

The unpacking sequence inverts the packing one, with one deliberate exception: **the kitchen goes first, not the bedroom.** The bedroom tolerates chaos. The kitchen paralyzes it, because takeout gets ordered on day three and the boxes stop moving entirely. Mine unpacked in about four hours on day one, in this order: boxes 1 through 11 open, everything lands in its destination zone, nothing gets set on counters "temporarily." The [under sink cabinet](/blog/under-sink-storage-plumbing-friendly/) got its layout in the first hour, before the counters could collect drifters.

Then beds, made with the first night box sheets. Then the closet, which goes up in one pass: hangers from the wardrobe bags straight onto the rod, shelves into rows with [dividers](/blog/closet-shelf-dividers-for-folded-sweaters/), the [vertical system](/blog/vertical-closet-system-maximize-small-wardrobe/) rebuilt before the first load of laundry, and the [door organizer](/blog/over-the-door-shoe-organizer-large-pockets/) hung on the closet door the same evening so shoes never meet the floor. Two rules keep the momentum through week one: every opened box empties completely the same day, and every empty box collapses flat and leaves. A half unpacked box becomes furniture, and furniture with a lid is where systems go to die. The [storage bins](/blog/collapsible-storage-bins-with-lids-fabric-stackable/) that traveled north came back into service holding the things that earned their spot, nothing more.

Week one has its own pacing rule, and it is not "unpack everything." Kitchen, beds, bathroom and closet land by day two. The living room and storage boxes get a deadline, not a sprint: every box numbered 23 and up is open, empty and gone within fourteen days, checked against the ledger, because the ledger also tells you when you are done. The two week mark is chosen honestly, from having missed it once: past two weeks, the remaining boxes stop registering as tasks and start registering as decor, and the odds of a box surviving sealed to the next move go up sharply. If a sealed box makes it to the deadline unopened, that is not a unpacking backlog, it is a purge list, and it leaves without being reopened, by the same logic the ledger existed to enforce.

## The system packs flat too

The whole apparatus fits in one folder when the truck pulls away: a tape legend, a ledger note, a first night list. It is the same document that keeps the apartment running after the boxes are gone, the loop described in the [whole home organization system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/), because unpacking is just the first week of living somewhere and the rules that work on day one are the rules that work on day one thousand. The sheets made it onto the bed by 9 p.m. this time, the coffee happened at 7 a.m., and the can opener was in box 1, line 3 of the ledger, exactly where it said. That is the entire difference between the two moves: not more effort, just the effort pointed at the three inches of marker ink that make everything after it findable.
