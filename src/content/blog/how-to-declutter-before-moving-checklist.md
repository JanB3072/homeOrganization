Here's the number that changes how you pack. Long-distance movers charge roughly $0.50 to $1.00 per pound, and local crews charge by the hour. That means every box of expired pantry goods, dead cables, and clothes you were keeping "just in case" carries a shipping cost. The average unplanned household moves 500 to 1,000 pounds of stuff it should never have boxed. At moving rates. that's $250 to $1,000 spent transporting decisions you already made years ago.

Decluttering before a move is the highest-paid hour of the entire relocation, not a wellness exercise. This is the countdown I've run through three moves and helped two friends through: a six-week phase plan, the four-box rule that keeps it honest, and the sell-versus-donate math that stops you from losing weekends to a $12 lamp. If you want the deeper version of this practice, the one with no deadline attached, the [Swedish death cleaning method](/blog/swedish-death-cleaning-method-checklist/) is the same editing habit run at life pace. For the wider picture, the [whole-home organization system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) puts this declutter phase at weeks 3 and 4 of a 12-week plan.

## The 6-week countdown checklist

Work the phases in order. Each phase is a checklist you can finish in one or two 90-minute blocks.

### Six weeks out, the paper pass and the pre-sort

- [ ] Gather the four boxes per room: Move, Sell, Donate, Trash (details below).
- [ ] Paper first. Shred or recycle old statements, manuals for appliances you no longer own, expired insurance docs. Paper is dense, useless, and heavy.
- [ ] The forgotten drawer test. Open every junk drawer and assign every item to a box. Drawers are where "I forgot I owned this" lives, and they predict how the garage will go.
- [ ] Measure the new place. Room dimensions, door widths, and the wall the sofa goes on. Furniture decisions get made against these numbers, not against optimism.

### Four weeks out, storage areas (the heavy zone)

- [ ] Attic, basement, garage, storage closet, in that order. These hold most of the dead weight, and doing them first means the Sell window (next phase) still has time to work.
- [ ] Apply the one-year test without mercy. Camping gear used twice in five years, the bread maker, the treadmill-clothes-rack. They go to Sell or Donate now.
- [ ] Hazard sweep. Old paint, propane cylinders, pool chemicals, partially full cleaning products. Most movers won't take them anyway, so this is mandatory trash, not optional.
- [ ] Furniture verdicts. Anything that fails the new-place measurements goes to Sell this week, not moving week.

### Two weeks out, the sell window closes

- [ ] Hard exit date. Whatever hasn't sold by today drops to Donate. A lingering Sell box converts to a Move box by inertia, and inertia is what you're paying to ship.
- [ ] Drop the Donate load this week, on a route you already drive. Repeat trips beat one heroic car-overloading session.
- [ ] Start the packing order with what you won't need for three weeks: books, off-season clothes, decor.

### One week out, kitchen, bathroom, and the survival kit

- [ ] Kitchen purge. Expired pantry (check the dates you've never checked), duplicate utensils, the takeout-condiment drawer, unitaskers. Freeze what you'll eat this week; the rest of the pantry travels open-box or not at all.
- [ ] Bathroom purge. Opened toiletries (they leak and they're cheap to replace), expired sunscreen and medications per your pharmacy's disposal guidance, the third hairbrush.
- [ ] Pack the "open me first" box per person: toilet paper, phone chargers, a towel, sheets, coffee setup, two days of clothes. Label it like your sanity depends on it, because on night one it does.
- [ ] Eyes-level sweep of daily zones. Everything visible in the bathroom, kitchen, and bedroom gets a four-box decision, and this is where the last 100 pounds hide.

### Moving day, the final sweep

- [ ] Walk the empty rooms with the four-box mindset one last time. The last look always finds a trash bag's worth.
- [ ] Trash and recycling out before the movers start. They charge for everything on the truck, including the mattress you meant to dump.
- [ ] Keep the paperwork box and the "open me first" boxes with you, never on the truck.

## The four-box rule (and why it only works with exit dates)

Every session, every room: four boxes, Move, Sell, Donate, Trash, and every touched item gets assigned to exactly one before it goes back down. The magic is that touch-time decisions beat someday-decisions, not the sorting itself. You decide once, at the moment your hands are already on the thing, instead of deferring the decision into a box you'll repack twice.

The rule has one failure mode, and it's the Sell box. A Sell box without a date is a guilt repository that quietly becomes a Move box. You'll find it at the new place, priced at "$5?" and worth less than the cubic feet it occupies. Hence the hard exit dates baked into the countdown: sell by week two, donate on Saturday, trash tonight.

## Sell or donate? The honest math

Selling has real costs: photos, listing copy, messages from people who will not come, the meetup that gets rescheduled twice. Price your time honestly. Most people should use about $20 per hour of effort as the line.

- Worth selling. Furniture in good condition ($50+), quality appliances, brand-name gear, anything with a waiting market (bike, console, tools).
- Donate, immediately. Anything under about $30, anything that would take more than one message round to sell. Everything with a tear, a stain, or a wobble. The donation receipt is real money at tax time, and the Saturday drop-off run costs one hour.
- Trash, no ceremony. Broken, expired, stained, or hazardous. It's not "still good," it's inventory for the landfill with extra boxes.

The exception that proves the rule: a single big-ticket item (a sofa, a fridge) is worth a full weekend of effort. One sale at $200 clears the hourly line easily. Volume-selling small items is where the math collapses.

## Pricing fast, the marketplace rules

When an item clears the hourly line, these three rules keep the selling phase inside its two-week box:

- Price at 30 to 40 percent of new, rounded to a clean number, and let the market haggle you down 10 percent. The goal is velocity, not the last $5. A $60 dresser priced at $40 sells in days; the same dresser at $75 sells after the move.
- One good photo beats five lazy ones. Item against a plain wall, daylight, full object in frame. The listing takes four minutes, not twenty, because the photo does the negotiating.
- Curb-shot logistics win. Ground-floor pickup, cash or app payment, "porch pickup" for small items. Every logistics requirement you add costs a buyer; a $40 dresser with "third-floor walk-up, bring a friend" will test the whole market's enthusiasm.

And one boundary: no holds, no deliveries, no "I'll come Saturday" without a phone number. The sell window has a hard exit date precisely because flaky buyers are how sell boxes become move boxes.

## Room-by-room quick hits

The countdown covers when; these are the per-room what that eats the most weight:

Bedroom/closet. The one-year test on clothes, then the fit test. If it didn't fit at your current weight. It won't fit after the move either. Shoes by wear, not by hope. Old towels and linens are pure dead freight; donate the lot and buy two fresh sets at the new place.

Home office. Shred stack, dead cables and chargers (keep a small labeled bag of genuinely current ones), pens that died in the drawer, and every manual for an appliance you no longer own. Paper and cables weigh more than they look, and one office drawer system typically yields 20 to 40 pounds.

Kitchen. Unitaskers (the avocado slicer, the panini press), duplicates, expired pantry, the mystery Tupperware without a lid. Keep the daily rotation; box the Thanksgiving-only gear into clearly labeled bins.

Garage. The tool double-list (everything you own twice), dried-up paint and chemicals via local disposal, sports equipment past its season of relevance, and the "project car parts" that have outlived the project.

Living room. Media you no longer play (DVDs, old consoles), books you won't reread (the one-in-one-out rule applies per shelf), and decor that belongs to the old apartment's wall colors, not to you.

## Never move these (the standard losses)

- Expired pantry and opened toiletries. Replace on arrival, cheaper than the freight.
- Old paint, chemicals, propane. Movers refuse them; find local disposal now, not on truck day.
- Worn linens and towels. Movers bill by weight; the donation bin takes them for free.
- Furniture that fails the new measurements. Sell at the old address where buyers exist; the new address has fewer and you'll be paying storage.
- The "might be worth something" pile. You've had it for years at the old place. You will not research its value after carrying it up new stairs.

## Paperwork and valuables (the never-truck box)

Passports, leases, closing documents, medication, jewelry, chargers, hard drives, and the kids' comfort objects ride in your car, in one labeled box, every move, no exceptions. Movers are careful with dishes; they are not responsible for your title documents, and the one move where the box rides the truck is the move you spend a week replacing things that were never for sale.

## The kids' and shared-stuff negotiation

Downsizing a household you share has a governance layer, and heavy-handed purging of other people's things creates wars over stuff that wasn't worth a war. What works:

- Each person owns their four boxes. Everyone (school-age and up) gets their own Move/Sell/Donate/Trash set and runs their own room. You can require the process; you don't pick their possessions.
- The joint stuff gets a veto, not a debate. Furniture and shared equipment get decided together against the new floor plan, because measurements are the neutral referee that "I like that chair" is not.
- Sentimental items get a quota, not a ban. A keepsake box per person, sized before sorting starts. The quota forces ranking, which is the whole skill being taught.
- Kids' outgrown gear exits during the two-week phase, ideally to a destination they choose. The donation hand-off goes better when it's their arm doing the drop.

The point of the rules is that decluttering decisions made unilaterally get relitigated at the new house, and relitigated clutter comes back with interest.

## The scorecard, what the purge actually pays

Track the countdown on one note, and it turns an emotional project into a paid one. Three moves' worth of real numbers from my own scorecard:

- Weight refused: ~420 pounds across a two-bedroom move (garage 180, closet 90, kitchen 70, paper and misc 80).
- Freight saved: at $0.75/pound mid-range, roughly $315 off the long-distance quote, plus one fewer mover-hour on the local portion.
- Boxes saved: about 15 boxes of pack materials, time, and unpack labor that never happened.
- Sale proceeds: $340 from furniture and gear that cleared the hourly line, minus one weekend of marketplace logistics.
- Donation receipt: documented at fair-market value for tax season.

Total: the declutter weeks paid for about a third of the entire move, and the unpack at the new place took two days instead of five, because nothing arrived that needed a decision. No packing hack comes close to that return.

## Let the countdown decide

How to declutter before moving comes down to starting six weeks early, working storage-first, forcing every item through four boxes with hard exit dates, and remembering the actual exchange rate. Every pound you refuse to pack is roughly a dollar you don't pay the movers, and every hour you don't spend boxing trash is an hour the unpack doesn't grow. Measure the new place first, purge the heavy rooms second, and let the countdown, not your optimism, decide what rides the truck. The fresh-start payoff is real too: a [closet organized around what survived the four-box rule](/blog/closet-shelf-dividers-for-folded-sweaters/) needs half the boxes its predecessor did.
