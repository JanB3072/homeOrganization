The tray arrived with good reviews, thirty-two compartments and a price that suggested it would be the last one I bought. It sat in the drawer at a slight angle, lifted at the front left corner, and the drawer would not close without a push. Underneath it, the velvet was already showing a pale line where the corner had been rubbing.

Nothing was wrong with the tray. It was square, the drawer was not, and four millimetres of difference was enough to spoil the fit.

## A drawer is not a rectangle

Four measurements decide whether an insert fits, and the one people take is usually the wrong one.

Width at the front, middle and back varies by 2 to 4 mm in a wooden drawer, because the box is assembled from panels that move with humidity. Take the smallest of the three. An insert cut to the largest reading fits in February and jams in July.

Then the runners. On a side-mount slide, the mechanism sits inside the drawer box and intrudes 12 to 15 mm at each side near the back. An insert sized to the outer interior rides up on the slides and sits at an angle, which is the fault I blamed on the tray.

Then depth, from the drawer floor to the lowest point of the front panel, which on a shaker style drawer is 6 to 10 mm lower than the sides. A 25 mm insert in a 22 mm opening will not let the drawer shut.

And the corner radius. Most boxes leave a CNC with an 8 to 12 mm radius, and a square insert rests on that curve rather than on the floor. Trim the foam corners to match and the rocking stops.

The symptom to watch for is a drawer front standing proud by a millimetre or two, which reads as a badly fitted drawer and is usually a tray sitting on its corners. Open the drawer, press the insert down at each corner, and whichever corner gives is the one whose radius is wrong.

## Velvet has a direction

Velvet is a pile fabric, so it has a nap, and the nap has a direction. Run a hand one way and the surface looks deep and dark; run it the other and it looks pale and flat.

Under one light source, panels cut in mixed directions read as two different colours, and no amount of arranging fixes it because the fault is the fabric. Lay every panel the same way before cutting. On a pre-assembled tray, hold it under the light and turn it 180 degrees, and the seam shows where the factory ran out of a roll.

The nap also decides how the tray ages. A band pressed into the pile leaves a dent that lifts overnight, because the fibres are bent rather than crushed. A bracelet left in one cell for months leaves a line that stays, and the line is the first thing the eye finds when the drawer opens. Moving pieces between cells once a season costs two minutes and keeps the surface even.

## Velvet against silver is not neutral

Most inexpensive velvet is viscose, and viscose processing can leave sulphur compounds in the fibre. Sulphur is the main cause of silver tarnish. A lined drawer, closed most of the day, holds those compounds against the metal in a small volume of air, and silver darkens faster there than on an open shelf.

Cotton and acid free velvet do not carry the same residue. The price difference is a few dollars, and the visible difference is whether a chain needs polishing every two months or twice a year.

If the label is gone, line the cells with cotton or keep a strip of anti tarnish paper under the tray. The [jewelry box piece](/blog/closet-jewelry-organizer-box-with-drawers/) goes further into tarnish and the chalk trick, which works on the same principle of controlling the air around the metal.

## What the velvet itself has to be

Fabric sold as velvet covers a wide range, and most of the difference only shows after a year in a drawer.

Weight is the first number. 200 to 280 grams per square metre wraps a foam core without showing the foam through it. Under 180 the weave is thin enough that the grey of the EVA reads through pale colours, and over 300 the fabric will not fold into a 25 mm cell without bulging at the corners.

Pile height, the second. 1.5 to 2.5 mm is the practical band. Shorter pile wipes clean and does not hold dust between the fibres; longer pile looks richer and collects lint in the bottom of every cell, which is where rings sit.

Backing matters more than it gets credit for. A woven backing holds its shape when wrapped around a corner and stays flat after a season of use. A non-woven backing is cheaper and starts to pill and shed at the cut edges within months, which leaves fibres on the jewellery.

Then the finish. Acid free and cotton velvets cost a few dollars more per metre and are the ones that will not tarnish silver. Standard upholstery velvet is usually viscose, and the section above explains why that matters in a closed drawer.

A 150 cm roll width cuts two full drawer inserts and leaves scraps for dividers. One drawer of 40 by 50 cm takes 0.4 to 0.5 metres, so a single metre covers the whole vanity for less than a bought tray that fits badly.

Colour is the last decision and the least important. Pale grey and oat hide dust and show the jewellery; black hides wear and swallows the pieces; white looks right for a month and then shows every ring mark.

## Cell sizes that match what you own

Compartment count is a marketing number. What decides whether a tray works is whether each cell matches the object in it.

| Piece | Cell size | Depth | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ring | 45 by 45 mm | 25 mm | Wide enough for a band to lie flat, not stand |
| Stud earrings | 40 by 40 mm | 20 mm | Pair stays together, nothing rolls |
| Chain necklace | 90 by 90 mm | 30 mm | Below 30 mm the pile has nowhere to spread |
| Bracelet | 100 by 60 mm | 30 mm | Cuff lies without resting on the clasp |
| Brooch or watch | 120 by 80 mm | 35 mm | Anything with a profile needs the headroom |

Depth is the variable most often got wrong in both directions. Under 30 mm a necklace has to pile on itself, and the pile is where knots form. Over 45 mm you cannot see the bottom without lifting the tray, and a cell you cannot see into stops being used. The [tray configuration piece](/blog/closet-accessory-tray-configuration/) makes the same point about accessories generally: more cells than objects produces empty cells that fill with things that do not belong.

## Cutting and wrapping your own

Ten millimetre EVA foam for the core, cut to the smallest width, corners trimmed to the radius. Velvet wrapped over the top and down the sides, all panels facing the same way, glued with a spray adhesive that stays flexible. Dividers are the same foam wrapped separately and pushed into slots cut halfway through the base, which is faster than building a grid and lets the layout change later.

Seams want a 45 degree cut at the corners, because a straight overlap shows a ridge on the velvet and the ridge is what catches a chain the first time you drag it across. Fit the whole thing in cardboard first, shut the drawer, and listen for the scrape.

## When the drawer is too shallow for a flat tray

A vanity drawer is often 40 to 50 mm deep, and a flat tray with 25 mm cells leaves no room for anything with a profile. Brooches, watches and cufflinks all need headroom that a single layer cannot give.

The instinct is to stack two trays. That works for one week and then fails, because the bottom layer becomes invisible, and an invisible layer is where the pieces you never wear go. Anything under another tray has effectively left the collection.

Three answers work better. An angled insert, which holds rings on a slope so they can be read without lifting anything and fits in half the depth. A shallow tray plus a rail across the back of the drawer, with small S hooks for chains, so the necklace length uses vertical space instead of floor space. Or a deeper single layer with fewer, larger cells, which holds less and gets used more.

The measurement that decides between them is the height from the drawer floor to the lowest point of the opening, taken with the drawer fully open. A tray that fits a closed drawer can still foul the front panel when it is pulled out, because the opening is narrower than the box.

## The one week reset test

After seven days of normal use, count how many pieces went back into their assigned cell without thinking about it. Above 90 percent and the layout matches how you put things down. Below that, the cells are too fine, and consolidating two into one does more than any label.

I landed on thirty pieces into nineteen cells, which is the first layout that survived a month. Fewer, larger cells also solve the opposite failure, which is a tray so subdivided that putting a ring away becomes a small decision, and small decisions are what turn a drawer back into a flat surface with jewellery on it.
