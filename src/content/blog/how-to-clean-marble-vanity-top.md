Marble on a bathroom vanity is calcite wearing its best clothes. The stone that makes the tops in this room so pale and dramatic is calcium carbonate, the same mineral class as chalk and eggshell, and nearly every cleaning disaster on a marble vanity is one specific reaction: calcium carbonate meeting an acid and turning into a salt and a gas. Lemon juice, vinegar, most limescale removers, a great many "natural" cleaning recipes, all of them perform that reaction on contact, in about thirty seconds, permanently.

That single fact explains most of what follows. But not everything wrong with a marble top is the same thing, and the single most useful skill in this whole subject is telling the four kinds of damage apart before choosing a product. The wrong diagnosis with the wrong chemical is how small marks become whole-surface refinishing jobs. I have done exactly that once, on a rental flat's Carrara vanity, by treating a general limescale film with a proper descaler, and the patch of etched stone that followed is why this guide is organised by diagnosis rather than by product.

## The four families, and the touch test

| What you see | What it actually is | Confirmed by | Caused by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dull, pale, slightly rough patch, often a ring or splash shape | Etching: acid dissolved the polish | Feels rougher or less glassy than the surrounding stone | Lemon, vinegar, toilet cleaner, some skin acids and medications |
| Dark or coloured patch, surface still glossy | Staining: pigment or oil sits in the pores | Feels identical to clean stone, just coloured | Oils, dyes, coffee, rust, mildew under a bottle |
| Grey film, especially where soap and water run | Soap scum: calcium stearate from soap + hard water | Feels greasy-fine, wipes away with detergent | Bar soap, body wash overspray |
| Hard white or off-white crust, usually at the tap | Limescale: calcium carbonate from the water itself | Feels like grit under the fingernail, flakes | Hard water evaporating |

The touch test comes first because etching and staining look deceptively alike in a photo and behave as opposites in treatment. Run a fingertip across the mark and across clean stone. If the mark is rougher, or reflects light less sharply, it is an etch, a chemical burn in the surface itself, and no cleaning product on earth restores it, because there is nothing left to clean: the polished layer is dissolved. If the surface feels identical and only the colour is wrong, it is a stain, and stains are removable because the stone underneath is intact and the pigment is only sitting in the pores. Two completely different jobs, and the difference lives in a fingertip.

## Etching: the burn, and what actually repairs it

The reaction is worth knowing in one line, because it is why the treatment sounds strange. Calcite plus an acid yields a calcium salt plus carbon dioxide; the acid literally eats the surface and bubbles it away as gas. The mark left behind is not dirt and cannot be wiped off, any more than a burn can be wiped off skin. What repairs it is re-polishing: burnishing fresh calcium carbonate powder, or a dedicated marble polishing compound, back into the surface until the gloss returns.

Shallow etches, the kind a splash leaves in a day, respond to hand work: sprinkle a marble polishing powder over the damp dull patch, work it with a damp microfibre cloth in small circles for a minute or two, wipe, and check the reflection at a low angle under the window light. Honest expectation: shallow and medium etches often improve to invisible; a deep burn, the kind toilet cleaner leaves in a ring, feels like sandpaper and needs mechanical honing by a stone professional, who will grind the whole surface down a layer and re-polish it.

One housekeeping note that prevents most of this: the acids arrive by accident more than by cleaning schedule. A shaving-cream splash, a vitamin C serum drop, a cotton pad soaked in acid exfoliant laid down on bare stone, the everyday habits covered in the [vanity display guide](/blog/vanity-top-makeup-organizer-ideas/) matter here twice over, because on marble the actives are not just degrading themselves, they are degrading the counter under them. Actives on a glass dish, splash wiped promptly, always.

## Staining: pull it out, do not scrub it

A stain is pigment that has soaked below the surface, which is why wiping never works and scrubbing only scratches. The treatment that actually works is older than any product line: a poultice, which is an absorbent paste that wets the stone from above, dissolves the stain into itself, and pulls it back out as it dries over a day or two. It is the same capillary physics that got the stain in, run in reverse.

The recipe changes with the stain, and this is the part worth copying down:

- Oil, makeup, hand cream: absorbent, flour or talc or bicarbonate, with acetone or a dedicated stone degreaser. The paste goes on half an inch thick, film over it, edges taped, 24 to 48 hours, and the scrape-and-wipe reveal.
- Coffee, tea, most food colouring: same absorbent with 12 percent hydrogen peroxide, which oxidises the colour out of the pore.
- Ink or marker: peroxide for light stone, acetone for dark, tested small first.
- Rust: this one is mineral, not pigment, and a poultice with a dedicated rust remover is the only home version; iron stains in the stone itself sometimes never come out.

Rust marks under a metal canister, and the slow brown halo under a shaving-cream lid, are the bathroom's most common stains, and both trace to one habit: metal on damp stone. Coasters, or a plain ceramic dish under anything metal, ends the category. A stain that resists two rounds of poultice has usually oxidised deeper than capillary reach, and professional work is cheaper than a replacement top; the [small vanity guide](/blog/small-bathroom-vanity-with-built-in-storage/) lists material choices at purchase time, and this paragraph is the argument for choosing cultured or quartz there if stain anxiety is real.

## The two mineral families: scum and scale

Soap scum and limescale get their own section because they are the marks people clean most aggressively, and marble punishes aggressive cleaning harder than any other surface here. The chemistry of both is in the [counter guide](/blog/bathroom-counter-clutter-solutions/): scum is the calcium salt of soap's fatty acids, scale is calcium carbonate left by evaporating hard water. On tile or acrylic the standard fix for both is a mild acid, which is exactly why the descaler under the bathroom sink is a loaded gun on marble: it dissolves the scale and then keeps going into the polish.

The marble-safe routine splits them instead. Scum lifts with alkalinity, a pH-neutral cleaner left to dwell for five minutes, a soft brush for the texture, and patience; heavy scum takes a dedicated stone soap scum remover, formulated without acid. Scale, the crust at the tap, is the harder honest case: non-acid limescale removers exist for stone, they work slowly, and the fast chemical route is the etch I described at the top. A plastic razor blade at a shallow angle removes a tap crust mechanically with zero chemistry, and a felt pad with marble polishing powder cleans up the faint shadow it leaves.

## The daily and weekly routine, sized to minutes

Daily care on marble is smaller than the anxiety around it. A microfibre cloth, damp with water, over the working zone after the morning routine, takes off the toothpaste, the powder, the shaving flecks and the water spots before any of them does anything. Weekly, a pH-neutral stone cleaner or one drop of washing-up liquid in warm water, cloth wrung nearly dry, the whole top, then a second pass with clean water because soap film is what we were trying to avoid. Bleach, diluted, once in a while for disinfecting, rinsed after; it is the sealer it slowly degrades, not the stone, that limits its welcome.

What never touches the top, in any form, at any dilution: vinegar, lemon juice, descaling sprays, most glass cleaners, abrasive creams and powders, magic-eraser style melamine sponges used dry, and bathroom sprays generally, because reading the label beats memorising lists, and the word to look for is acid, anywhere in it, including the citric that sounds so harmless.

## Heat, and the marks people misread

Marble formed under mountain-building pressures and temperatures, so the stone itself does not fear a hair straightener; what fears heat is the marriage of stone and counter. A hot tool parked on the top can leave a pale ring not by burning the calcite but by flash-drying whatever film sat under it, wax, sealer overspray, oily residue, and by stressing the thin layer of polished stone enough to micro-crack it, which reads as a dull patch and feels like an etch. The distinction matters: a heat ring that reappears every time the spot gets wet is usually a film ghost, and lifts with the pH-neutral routine, while a true micro-cracked patch stays dull when dry. Either way the fix is behavioural. A silicone or cork trivet next to the mirror, where the straightener lands every morning, costs less than any repair.

The other heat-adjacent misread is the yellowing that develops under a bottle left for a season. That is usually oil oxidising in the pore rather than the stone changing, which is why it responds to the oil-poultice from the staining section while everything aimed at "yellow stains" in the cleaning aisle does nothing. Peroxide in a poultice, patient repeats, and the halo lifts in most cases; if it does not, the oil reached a depth where only honing reaches.

## Myths that cost stone

Three pieces of folk wisdom account for a steady share of damaged vanities, and each fails for a reason the chemistry predicts.

Toothpaste on a dull spot is the most-repeated tip, and it half works, which is why it survives. The fine abrasives in toothpaste do burnish calcite slightly, and for one shallow etch on a small patch the spot genuinely looks better for a week. What the tip omits is that toothpaste also carries acids and flavour oils that sit in the fresh micro-pores the polishing just opened, and many users report the patch returning, some report it growing. Marble polishing powder costs less than toothpaste and contains none of the extras, so the folk remedy is strictly worse than the product made for the job.

Lemon and bicarbonate of soda is the "natural cleaner" pairing that worries stone professionals most, because the baking soda feels gentle and the pairing sounds traditional. The soda is mildly alkaline and fine in moderation; the lemon is the acid etch from the first section, warmed up, freshly applied to the whole top. Natural and safe are different properties, and calcite is the proof.

And the third, repeated in good faith even by some cleaners: "sealed marble is protected marble". The sealing section above covers what a penetrating sealer does, and the shorthand version is that it makes the stone stain-resistant, not acid-proof or etch-proof or scratch-proof. Treating a sealed top the way the sealer marketing implied, citrus sprays and all, produces a stone that beaded water beautifully for two years and etched uniformly the whole time.

## Sealing: what it does, what it never does

A penetrating sealer is not a plastic film, it is a Fluoropolymer treatment that fills the stone's pores below the surface so liquids sit up as beads instead of wicking in. That is its entire job, and it is worth stating what it does not do: it does not stop acid. An etch is a reaction with the surface itself, and the sealer is inside the surface, so lemon behaves identically on sealed and unsealed marble. People who believe sealer makes stone carefree are the people who find etch rings under their citrus-scented cleaner.

The drop test tells you where yours stands. Drip a teaspoon of water on the counter, come back in 15 minutes: if it still beads, the sealer is working; if it has darkened the stone beneath it, the pores are open and it is time to re-seal, which is a twenty-minute job, stone cleaner first, sealer applied with a cloth, dwell per the bottle, buff. In a working bathroom, once a year; a show-guest-only powder room stretches to two.

Ventilation matters more to marble than it gets credit for, since standing humidity keeps films wet longer and feeds both scum and mildew; the fan routine described in the [floating vanity guide](/blog/how-to-style-a-floating-vanity/) is part of stone care, not just cabinet care.

## Knowing your marble, because they are not all the same

The stone sold as marble spans a spectrum of acid tolerance, and the label helps predict it. White calcite marbles, Carrara, Calacatta and friends, are the acid-sensitive mainstream, and this whole guide is written for them. Darker green and black "marbles" are often serpentine or contain more silicate minerals, and tolerate acids noticeably better while bringing their own quirks with alkalis and oils. Engineered quartz and cultured marble swap the chemistry entirely, quartz being resin-bound and near-immune to acids but sensitive to solvents and heat, cultured marble being a gel-coat surface that fears abrasion rather than chemistry; their care lives in a different column of the material table in the [vanity selection guide](/blog/small-bathroom-vanity-with-built-in-storage/). If you are unsure what the top actually is, the drop test again, on an inconspicuous corner, with a drop of lemon juice left for five minutes and wiped: a dulled spot confirms calcite marble and settles every question in this article, at the cost of one tiny etch in a corner.

## Where the professional line sits

Most of this article is deliberately doable in a bathroom, but the line between a home job and a stone professional's visit is worth drawing precisely, because crossing it by trial and error costs more than the visit would have. A stone mason earns their call for any crack that catches a fingernail, for etch burns deep enough to feel like sandpaper across more than a palm-sized area, for a top that has gone generally grey from years of wrong sprays and needs mechanical honing, and for open seams around an undermount basin, where water is entering the substrate and the failure ahead is structural rather than cosmetic. Prices vary by city, but the shape of the arithmetic is stable: honing and re-polishing a vanity top runs in the low hundreds, and a replacement slab plus fabrication runs into four figures, so an hour of professional work is usually the cheap half of any comparison.

The home kit, by contrast, covers everything short of that line: polishing powder for shallow etches, poultice materials for stains, pH-neutral cleaner for the routine, a sealer for the yearly refresh, a plastic razor for crusts. All of it together costs less than one professional call, and nothing in it can make a situation dramatically worse the way an acid descaler can, which is the real division of labour. Chemistry you understand stays on the home side; grinding, resin work and seam repair belong to someone with a diamond pad and a second pair of hands.

The rest of the [vanity guides](/bathroom/vanity/) cover the furniture, the wall above it, and the arrangement on top of it. Underneath all of them, the marble asks for remarkably little: a pH-neutral wash, prompt attention to splashes, something between the bottles and the stone, and a diagnosis by fingertip before any bottle under the sink is trusted near it.
