On a Tuesday in September I spent forty seconds looking for one cufflink. It was not lost. It was in the third cell of the second row of a 24-compartment wooden tray I had bought two years earlier, lying on its side under a watch with a scratched case back. Its partner was four cells away, which I know because I wrote the whole drawer down on the back of a delivery slip that morning instead of getting dressed. Eleven watches, nine pairs of cufflinks, seven rings, six pairs of studs, four tie bars, three spare link bundles and two pairs of glasses were sharing a grid designed for none of them in particular.

The tray was not too small. It was configured wrong. I had chosen it from a product photograph, and the photograph showed a tidy grid of identical squares. My accessories are not identical squares. A 42 mm watch with a crown protruding 4 mm needs a different cell from an 8 mm stud, and both need a different divider height from a 58 mm tie bar. Every time I pulled the drawer open, the lightest pieces hopped over the 10 mm dividers into neighbouring cells. That is how one cufflink ended up four squares from its partner, and why the watch underneath it had a hairline scratch across the case back.

I lifted the tray out, measured the empty drawer with a tape measure, and kept the delivery slip. That scrap of paper decided the whole layout. It also changed how I think about the rest of the closet, which I have written about separately in the [ninety-second capsule layout](/blog/capsule-wardrobe-closet-layout-design/) and the [whole-home method](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/). This piece is narrower: the ledger, the arithmetic, and a six-week check on whether the result held.

## The inventory, written down before anything was bought

The first column is the object. The second is the largest dimension it needs when it lies flat. The third is how tall it sits. The fourth is how often my hand reaches for it in a normal week. That last column is the one nobody puts on a product page, and it is the one that decides almost everything about a drawer.

| Item | Count | Largest dimension | Resting height | Reaches per week |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Watches | 11 | 42 mm case, plus a 4 mm crown | 12 mm | 5 |
| Cufflinks | 9 pairs | 16 mm per pair | 9 mm | 1 |
| Rings | 7 | 22 mm outer diameter | 6 mm | 4 |
| Stud earrings | 6 pairs | 8 mm per pair | 5 mm | 3 |
| Tie bars and a pin | 4 | 58 mm long | 7 mm | 0.25 |
| Spare links and straps | 3 | 90 mm long | 10 mm | 0.25 |
| Glasses | 2 | 145 mm wide | 30 mm | 5 |

Two things fell out of that table before I spent a dollar.

Glasses do not belong in the tray at all. At 145 mm wide they would consume three cells each and still need a lid to keep dust off the lenses. They went into a shallow shelf basket above the drawer instead, which is the same reasoning I used for [storing jewelry in a small closet armoire](/blog/how-to-organize-jewelry-in-a-small-closet-armoire/), where the bulky case gets a shelf and the small pieces get the drawer.

The second finding was less obvious. My most frequently touched items are also my tallest and heaviest. Watches are 12 mm high and get picked up five times a week. Earring studs are 5 mm high and get picked up three times a week. A grid of identical squares treats those as the same object. A configuration that respects the table cannot. I had made the same mistake with a [jewelry organizer box with drawers](/blog/closet-jewelry-organizer-box-with-drawers/) in the bedroom, where every compartment was sized for a necklace and the rings rolled around in the leftover space.

## A drawer has a cell budget, and it is smaller than it looks

The drawer in the photograph is a nominal 22-inch drawer. Nominal is not interior. The outer width is 559 mm, and the two side walls are standard 1/2-inch stock, 12.7 mm each. That leaves about 534 mm of clear width. Depth is 17 inches, 432 mm, minus the front and back boards, so about 406 mm clear.

Wood moves. In a Chicago apartment the same drawer measures 1 to 2 mm wider in August than in February. I leave 6 mm of fitting clearance on each side, which gives 522 mm of usable width and 394 mm of usable depth.

Now the part I wish someone had told me two years ago. A compartment is not just its inside width. Every cell costs its own width plus one wall. With a 48 mm cell and a 3 mm wall, the pitch is 51 mm, and the budget is:

```text
columns = floor(usable_width  / (cell_width + wall)) = floor(522 / 51) = 10
rows    = floor(usable_depth  / (cell_width + wall)) = floor(394 / 51) = 7
budget  = 10 x 7 = 70 cells
```

That formula is the whole reason the word "configuration" belongs in the title. Buying a tray is a purchase. Working out how many cells a drawer can physically carry before you buy anything is a configuration. The same arithmetic appears in [bathroom vanity drawer organization](/blog/bathroom-vanity-drawer-organization-tips/) and in the [cabinet pull-out measurements](/blog/diy-pull-out-shelves-for-kitchen-cabinets/), and it is worth seeing the three common drawer widths side by side:

| Nominal drawer | Clear width after 1/2-inch walls | Usable width after clearance | Columns at 51 mm pitch | Cells at 7 rows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 18 in (457 mm) | 432 mm | 420 mm | 8 | 56 |
| 20 in (508 mm) | 483 mm | 471 mm | 9 | 63 |
| 22 in (559 mm) | 534 mm | 522 mm | 10 | 70 |

A 24-cell tray in a 22-inch drawer wastes 46 cells of capacity while still cramming eleven watches into cells that are too narrow. It is not a small tray problem. It is a pitch problem.

There is one mechanical detail that the budget ignores and the drawer does not. The bottom of my drawer is 3 mm fibreboard. A 60-cell wooden tray weighs about 1.4 kg empty and close to 3 kg loaded, and a thin bottom sags under four point loads long before it sags under a load spread across the whole panel. I chose a tray with a continuous flat base, not one standing on four corner feet. If you are replacing drawer hardware at the same time, the slides are almost never the limit; common three-quarter-extension ball-bearing slides are rated 35 to 45 kg, which is ten times what accessories weigh.

## What the cell actually has to hold

Three numbers came out of the inventory table, and each one killed a product I had been about to order.

A 40 mm watch case with a 4 mm crown needs 44 mm just to exist. Give it 4 mm more and you get 48 mm, which is why 48 mm became my cell width. With the crowns all pointing toward the back of the drawer, two watches in adjacent cells cannot press crown against case. The 42 mm pieces get 6 mm of slack, which is enough that a thick leather strap does not wedge.

Divider height is the second number. When a drawer accelerates, anything that is not held down tends to climb. I measured my first tray at 10 mm dividers with 9 mm cufflinks inside, and I was collecting two or three escaped pieces a week. A reasonable rule is that the divider should be at least 1.5 times the resting height of the contents. For 9 mm cufflinks that is 14 mm; I went to 18 mm because the difference in cost was four dollars. Six weeks later the escaped-piece count is zero. The same ratio applies to [makeup drawer dividers](/blog/bathroom-drawer-dividers-for-makeup/), where compacts are much taller than the dividers sold with them.

The third number is not a dimension at all. Silver tarnishes when it meets sulphur compounds in the air, and some flocked linings are glued with adhesives that off-gas sulphur right against the metal. I had a pair of silver studs go dark in about five months in the old tray. Acid-free glue or plain cotton canvas is the safer lining, with a 20 g silica gel packet in the drawer changed monthly. I learned that the hard way with [leather boots and trapped moisture](/blog/how-to-store-leather-boots-in-closet/), where the fix was also about the material touching the object rather than the object itself.

| Object | Minimum clear cell | Minimum divider | Lining |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 40 mm watch | 48 mm | 18 mm | Cotton, strap unrolled |
| Cufflink pair | 20 mm | 18 mm | Cotton, one pair per cell |
| Ring, 22 mm | 26 mm | 12 mm | Cotton or acid-free flock |
| Stud earrings | 12 mm | 12 mm | Cotton pad to stop static |
| 58 mm tie bar | 64 mm long cell | 12 mm | Any |

Leather straps are the other quiet problem. Left folded flat in a square cell for months, a strap takes a permanent crease at the bend. I let the two straps I own lie unrolled in a long cell rather than buckling them, and the watches I wear most often sit on their sides with the strap relaxed. It is the same logic as [acrylic dividers for handbags](/blog/acrylic-purse-dividers-for-closet-shelves-organizer/), where a bag that is forced to hold someone else's shape eventually keeps it.

## Three failures, and what fixed each

I wrote the delivery slip because of three specific complaints. Here is each one with the cause, the change, and what the drawer looked like at the end of week six.

| Complaint | Measured cause | Change | Week six |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scratched case backs | 24 identical 40 mm cells for 42 mm watches, crowns facing random directions | 48 mm cells, one watch each, crowns all facing the back | No new scratches |
| Cufflink pairs separating | One piece per cell, plus 10 mm dividers letting pieces hop | One pair per cell, 18 mm dividers, one lidded orphan box | 9 of 9 pairs intact, 1 orphan |
| Small items arriving in the wrong row | 5 mm studs in open cells with no friction at the base | Cotton pad under the front two rows, lightest items nearest the handle | Zero escaped pieces |

The orphan box deserves a line of its own. It is one of the clear lidded boxes visible behind the tray in the photograph. Anything found without a partner goes in it. When three pieces have accumulated, I stop and match them against the tray. Starting count was seven orphans, most of them earring backs and one cufflink from a pair I had written off in 2024. Current count is one. The box works because it makes separation visible instead of letting it become background noise, which is the same reason [hat storage by shape and frequency](/blog/hat-storage-ideas-for-small-closet/) works better than a pile on a shelf.

The front two rows are reserved for the lightest objects, not the most valuable ones. That is counterintuitive until you open a loaded drawer a few hundred times. The lightest pieces are the ones that move, so I put them where I see them first and where a cotton pad does the most good. Watches, which are heavy enough to stay put, sit in the middle three rows. Monthly objects, tie bars and spare links, sit in the back two rows. The back rows are slightly harder to reach and that is exactly the point.

## A branching check for what goes where

A shopping list tells you what to buy. A configuration tells you what to do with the next object that arrives, including objects you do not own yet. I keep this as a note taped inside the closet door, written as a series of questions rather than a set of rules, because a question still works when the object is unfamiliar.

```text
START: hold the item in your hand

  Q1  Will I reach for it at least 3 times a week?
      NO   -> back two rows, lidded or long cell, monthly patrol
      YES  -> forward five rows, open cell

  Q2  Is it taller than 12 mm when it lies flat?
      NO   -> 18 mm divider is enough, any free forward cell
      YES  -> long cell or tray pillow, never a standard square

  Q3  Does it tarnish, or does it have a partner?
      TARNISH -> acid-free lining, keep the silica packet nearby
      PARTNER -> one pair per cell, never one piece per cell
      NEITHER -> any free cell inside the 80% filled zone

  Q4  Would this push the tray past 80% full?
      YES  -> something leaves first, see the 20-minute decluttering rule
      NO   -> put it down, close the drawer, done
```

The last branch is the one that keeps the configuration alive. A grid at 100% has no slack, so the next gift or repair forces a reshuffle, and a reshuffle is when pairs separate again. I use a shortened version of the [20-20 decluttering rule](/blog/20-20-decluttering-rule-explained/) for this: if the item that has to leave can be replaced for under twenty dollars in under twenty minutes, it can leave. For accessory drawers the threshold is much lower than for kitchen gear, because the whole contents of the tray are worth less than the space and the calm.

The same branching habit scales up. The [whole-home system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) uses decisions rather than room-by-room checklists for the same reason: a rule about "where small metal things go" survives a move, while a diagram of one specific drawer does not.

## What the trays are made of, and why that changed the layout

I tried four materials before settling. Each one changed a number in the configuration, which is why material belongs after the arithmetic rather than before it.

| Material | Dimensional behaviour | What it does to accessories | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solid wood, 60 cells | Grows 1 to 2 mm in humid months, 1.4 kg empty | Flat base spreads the load, takes screws for taller dividers | Kept, with 6 mm clearance |
| Acrylic | Holds size to under 1 mm | Static holds earring backs against the cell floor | Rejected for the front rows |
| Flocked velvet, glued | Thin, adds about 1 mm to each wall | Quiet and scratch-free, but sulphur adhesives darken silver | Rejected unless acid-free |
| Silicone | Flexible, washable | Dividers above 12 mm fold under a fingertip | Rejected for anything with a pair |

The tray in the photograph is a wooden frame with a cotton canvas lining fixed using acid-free adhesive, 18 mm dividers, and a continuous base. The clear lidded boxes behind it are acrylic, which is fine there because they hold straps and orphan pieces rather than tiny studs. Mixing materials by row cost less than buying one premium tray, and it let me put the static-prone material exactly where nothing static-sensitive lives.

The whole setup came to 56 dollars: 34 for the wooden tray, 9 for the canvas and adhesive, 6 for silica gel packets, and 7 for two lidded boxes. The tray I replaced had cost 29 dollars and had cost me, by my own stopwatch, forty seconds of searching on most weekday mornings for about two years. That is the comparison I would make before looking at any product page. A cheaper tray that is configured wrong is the expensive one. If you want to see the same cost logic applied to a bigger storage decision, the [vertical closet system](/blog/vertical-closet-system-maximize-small-wardrobe/) breaks down what each shelf height actually buys.

One more consequence of choosing wood: the 6 mm fitting clearance is not optional. I measured the tray again in August and it was 1.6 mm wider than in February. In a drawer with zero clearance, a humid summer either jams the tray or bows the side walls. This is also why shelf dividers for folded knitwear need a little play, a point I go into with [closet shelf dividers](/blog/closet-shelf-dividers-for-folded-sweaters/).

## Six weeks later: the ledger, re-counted

I re-counted on the first Sunday of November, six weeks after the tray went in. Eleven watches are still in eleven cells. Nine cufflink pairs are still nine pairs. The orphan box holds one earring back instead of seven. The scratched case back has not acquired a neighbour. Nothing has hopped a divider since the 18 mm walls went in.

The stopwatch number improved from roughly forty seconds to six. That is the mean of twelve timed mornings, not a guess, and most of the gain came from the front five rows holding only things I actually touch. I did not buy a single additional organizer during those six weeks.

Of the 70 cells the drawer can carry, 54 are occupied and 16 are empty. Three of those 16 were used in October, for a watch I had repaired, a pair of cufflinks my brother returned after borrowing them in 2024, and a tie pin I found in a coat pocket. The grid absorbed all three without moving anything else. That is the entire argument for leaving 20% empty, and it is the part of the configuration I would defend hardest.

The one thing I would change is the seventh row. Seven rows of 48 mm squares is one row too many for objects I touch monthly. On my next pass I am replacing it with a single long cell for straps and spare links, which brings the grid to 60 cells plus one long cell. I am not in a hurry. A configuration that survives six weeks of real mornings has earned the right to be adjusted one row at a time rather than rebuilt.
