The scratch is 9 mm long and sits just left of the right lens of a brown pair I have owned since 2021. It is not deep enough to bother my eyes and it is exactly deep enough to catch the light when I turn the glasses toward a window. I found it one morning in April, on the day I decided fourteen pairs could no longer live in a pile in the second drawer of the dresser.

I assumed a temple arm had done it. Sunglasses scratch each other, everyone says that, so I spent the first week separating the pairs into little clear compartments and pointing the folded arms the same way. In July a second line appeared, on a different pair, in a compartment where no frame touched another frame. That is when I stopped blaming the glasses and looked at what sat between the lens and the tray.

## A 9 mm scratch, and what actually made it

Acrylic is soft. On the Mohs scale it sits around 3, while the silica grit in ordinary household dust is closer to 7, and a resin lens with a mirrored or polarized coating is softer still. The plastic grid was never going to cut the lens. What cut it was a few grains of dust underneath the frame, dragged back and forth by the two or three millimetres the glasses travel every time a drawer opens and closes.

That detail changed the whole problem. Any solution built around "a softer surface" was aimed at the wrong thing. What I needed was a bottom that let grit fall out of contact and stopped the frame sliding at all. Felt does both. Its fibres are loose enough for a grain to sink below the lens, and the friction holds a folded pair still through the little jolt of a drawer starting and stopping. A smooth acrylic floor does the opposite on both counts. It holds the dust at the surface and lets the frame skate.

I had the same misconception with [clear acrylic bins with dividers](/blog/clear-acrylic-bathroom-bins-with-dividers/), where I kept wiping the compartments and could not work out why the polishing cloth came away grey again a week later.

## The felt square, April

The first fix cost eleven dollars: one 900 by 600 mm sheet of wool felt and a pair of scissors. I cut it to the drawer base, laid the fourteen pairs flat on top, lenses up, and went to work.

No new scratches appeared in April or May. That part of it worked immediately, and it is why some version of that felt square is still in the drawer today. Everything else about the arrangement failed. Fourteen dark lenses seen from above are fourteen nearly identical ovals. Picking the pair I wanted meant lifting three or four others to see the frame shape underneath, and the folded arms of one pair rested across the bridge of its neighbour. By the end of the month the felt looked tidy from a distance and the pile on top of it had quietly reverted to being a pile.

A flat liner solves friction. It does not solve identification, and it does not give anything a fixed address. I had run into the same distinction while adding [shelf dividers to folded knitwear](/blog/closet-shelf-dividers-for-folded-sweaters/), where a liner made things quieter but the absence of divisions let everything drift back into a heap within a fortnight.

## The acrylic tray, July

The second fix cost twenty-six dollars and looked, in the product photograph, exactly like the drawer in the picture here. Twelve clear compartments, one pair each, lenses up, every frame visible at a glance. For about six weeks it was the best version I had.

Then the second scratch arrived, which is what sent me back to the grit question. Two other problems surfaced at the same time. Twelve slots do not hold fourteen pairs, so the two extras went into a lidded box on the counter; I reached for those two perhaps once in the following month. And the wraparound sports pair would not seat properly. Its curved front stands about 52 mm tall when it lies flat, higher than a standard frame, and against a low divider it tipped whenever the drawer moved.

I will say one thing for the clear tray: it made the collection legible. Being able to see twelve frames without touching anything is worth a lot on a weekday morning. The lesson from July was not that acrylic was wrong, it was that acrylic needed something underneath it. I had already learned the same thing with [stackable clear shoe boxes](/blog/clear-plastic-shoe-boxes-stackable-design/), where adding a thin liner stopped the contents from skating every time I pulled one down from a stack.

## The stackable case, October

The third attempt was a thirty-eight dollar set of three clear display cases that stack on the counter. They are in the photograph too, next to the little drawer unit. They fix dust completely, they look good, and they failed for a reason that had nothing to do with the glasses.

To reach the bottom case I had to lift two others and put them down somewhere. I did that twice in the first week and not at all by the third week, which meant the bottom two cases had quietly become storage rather than display. The pair I wanted most often ended up wherever I had last set a case down. There is a version of this problem in every vertical stack in a small apartment, and I wrote about the geometry of it in the [over-the-door shelf project](/blog/diy-over-the-door-storage-shelf-bedroom/), where anything above shoulder height stops being used no matter how well it is arranged.

I kept one case. It sits on the counter and holds the three pairs I actually wear in a given season. The other two went back in the box. That is also a useful check on [small-space vertical storage](/blog/small-space-vertical-storage-solutions-wall-hangers/) in general: if an organizer needs two hands and a clear patch of counter to open, it is not an everyday organizer.

## Why felt won, for a reason I had wrong

What is in the drawer now is the July grid with April's felt cut into twelve pads, one under each compartment. Thirty-seven dollars of parts. The grid gives every pair an address and lets me see twelve frames at once. The felt catches grit and stops the frames moving. Neither piece did the job alone, and the combination costs less than the display cases did.

There is a second reason the felt stayed, and I only noticed it because the counter sits near a west-facing window. Polarized films and mirrored coatings do not like sustained heat or damp. Lens makers warn against leaving coated glasses in a hot car or a steamy bathroom for long stretches, so I set my own cutoff at 40 C and 70 percent humidity and move the pairs when the counter gets there. A drawer inside the closet, away from the radiator and out of the afternoon sun, is a kinder place than a windowsill. Felt holds moisture though, so I lift the pads out once a month, shake them, and leave them in the air for twenty minutes before putting them back.

That pairing of a rigid frame with a soft, replaceable base is the same idea I ended up using for the [accessory tray in the other closet drawer](/blog/closet-accessory-tray-configuration/), though the dimensions there are much smaller. It also explains why [jeans folded against a shelf divider](/blog/how-to-organize-jeans-on-closet-shelves/) hold their shape longer than a stack on bare board. Something has to absorb the small movements.

## The measurements I wish I had taken first

I bought all three organizers before measuring a single pair of glasses. Here are the numbers that would have saved the thirty-eight dollar case and two scratched lenses.

- A folded pair is about 150 mm across and 80 mm from the front of the frame to the ends of the folded temples. My widest wraparound is 152 mm. A slot needs at least 160 mm by 90 mm to leave 8 mm of clearance at the sides and 10 mm front to back.
- Interior drawer height matters more than width. Ordinary frames stand 35 to 45 mm at the highest point of the lens curve; a curved sports frame can reach 52 mm. Many closet drawers give only 50 to 75 mm of clear height, so a shallow drawer simply cannot take a thick frame lying flat.
- A nominal 22-inch drawer has about 534 mm of interior width. At a 163 mm pitch, a 160 mm slot plus a 3 mm wall gives three columns, and a 400 mm deep drawer gives about four rows. Twelve slots. Fourteen pairs will not fit, so plan where the other two live before buying anything.
- A hard shell case measures roughly 170 by 75 by 55 mm per pair. It is larger than a bare slot, hides the frame completely, and has to be opened with two hands. Keeping the cases for travel makes more sense than keeping them for storage.
- The grid should sit flat on the drawer base. A thin fibreboard bottom will bow under four corner feet long before it bows under a load spread across the whole panel, which is the same detail that comes up in [vanity drawer organization](/blog/bathroom-vanity-drawer-organization-tips/).

None of these numbers are on a product page. They are the reason a tray that photographs beautifully can still be wrong for a particular drawer, a point I keep coming back to in the [vertical closet spacing notes](/blog/vertical-closet-system-maximize-small-wardrobe/).

## A four-line check before anything goes in the drawer

The arrangements that lasted all share four rules, so I stopped thinking of them as a layout and started treating them as a gate a pair has to pass before it goes in.

- Lens up, temples folded back, every hinge facing the same direction. Nothing coated touches the bottom of a slot.
- One pair per slot. Never stack. The upper temples press on the lower bridge, and the whole stack shifts when the drawer moves.
- Sort by occasion, not by lens colour. Commuting, driving, sport, and spare are four different destinations; dark grey and dark brown look identical from above.
- The front row holds this season's three pairs. The middle rows hold the occasional ones. Spares and anything waiting for a replacement nose pad sit at the back where I have to mean it.

That last rule is what keeps the drawer from refilling itself. Every month I take out whatever has not been worn, and I use a much lower threshold than I would for kitchen gear: if a pair can be replaced cheaply and I have not reached for it in a season, it can leave. The [twenty-minute decluttering rule](/blog/20-20-decluttering-rule-explained/) is the short version, and the [whole-home system](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) explains why a rule about a category outlives a diagram of one particular drawer. A [smaller capsule wardrobe](/blog/capsule-wardrobe-closet-layout-design/) would make all of this easier, but fourteen pairs is where I am, so the drawer has to do the work.

## What the note inside the drawer says

There is an index card taped to the inside of the dresser, above the felt. It has four lines on it, the same four lines as the check above, written in pencil because I have rewritten it twice. The first version said "wipe the compartments every week", which I stopped doing almost immediately once I understood where the scratches came from. The current version says "shake the pads once a month", and that one has stuck.

The felt is cut slightly too wide along the left edge and curls up about 3 mm where the drawer front closes over it. I have been meaning to trim it since April. I have not, because the curl does nothing except make the drawer quieter, and the two scratched lenses are still the only two I have.
