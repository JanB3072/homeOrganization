The sweater cost $180 and came out of the machine the size of a toddler's. It had been a gift, it was 100 percent cashmere, and I had treated it like a cotton sweatshirt: warm water, a scoop of regular detergent, a normal cycle. By the time the spin finished, the sleeves had shortened by a third and the surface had gone from soft to felted. Nothing brought it back.

That is the lesson cashmere teaches early. It is not a fabric in the way cotton is a fabric. It is hair, and hair responds to heat, soap, and friction in ways that are predictable if you know what you are handling. Once you do, washing it is a twenty minute job you do twice a season, and storage is a box on a shelf.

## Cashmere is hair, not fabric

Cashmere comes from the undercoat of a goat, and the fibers are 14 to 19 microns wide, finer than most wool. Under a microscope each fiber has scales that point in one direction. In cool water with a pH-neutral soap and no movement, those scales lie flat and the fiber stays soft.

Add heat, agitation, or an alkaline detergent and the scales open and lock into each other. That is felting. It tightens the fabric, shortens the sleeves, and cannot be reversed by any product. The same mechanism explains why a hot wash shrinks wool and why a cool soak does not.

## The four ways a sweater dies

Almost every ruined cashmere sweater fails in one of four ways. The fix is different for each, and only one of them is actually about washing.

| Symptom | Real cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pilling | friction from bags, seats, hangers | fabric comb, not a razor |
| Shrinking | heat, agitation, alkaline soap | cold soak, no wringing |
| Stretched shoulders | hung wet or on a thin hanger | dry flat, store folded |
| Holes | moth larvae eating protein | freeze 48 hours, store clean |

The important one is the first. Pilling gets blamed on washing, but a sweater that pills on the sleeves after two wears is telling you about friction, not water.

## Wash day: two buckets and a timer

The method is a cold soak, a same-temperature rinse, and a press. That is all.

1. **Fill a basin** with 30°C water, about 85°F, and a capful of pH-neutral wool wash. Regular detergent is alkaline and will felt the fiber.
2. **Submerge and soak** for 10 to 15 minutes. Do not rub, knead, or agitate. Let the water do the work.
3. **Drain and rinse** with water at the same temperature. A cold rinse after a warm soak shocks the fiber, and shock is what causes shrinkage.
4. **Press out water** by rolling the sweater in a clean towel. Never wring or twist.
5. **Dry flat** on a mesh rack and reshape the shoulders, sleeves, and hem while damp.

| Step | Water | Time | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soak | 30°C, pH-neutral | 10 to 15 min | rubbing, hot water |
| Rinse | same 30°C | 2 to 3 min | cold shock |
| Press | towel | 1 min | wringing |
| Dry | flat, mesh | 24 to 48 h | hanging, tumble dry |

## Drying is where most sweaters are lost

A wet cashmere sweater weighs several times what it does dry, and the shoulders are the weakest point. Hang it and gravity pulls the knit down two to three inches by morning. That stretch is permanent, and it is the reason so many secondhand sweaters have long, pointed shoulders.

Lay it flat on a mesh rack, not a towel on a table, because mesh lets air reach the underside and cuts drying time in half. Reshape the shoulders to the width they were before the wash and check the sleeve length against your arm. If the rack sits near a radiator, move it. Direct heat does the same damage as a hot wash.

## Storage: the moth map

Moths do not eat randomly. They go for the collar, the cuffs, and the underarms first, because those areas hold body oil and skin cells. A clean sweater is a less attractive sweater, which is why washing before storage matters more than any repellent.

- **Freeze first.** If you have seen a moth, seal the clean sweater in a bag and freeze it for 48 hours at minus 18°C. That kills eggs and larvae, which cedar cannot.
- **Cedar and lavender deter, they do not kill.** Use them as a second layer, not as the only one.
- **Store folded, in a breathable box.** A sealed plastic bin traps moisture and holds in any moth smell. A mesh or cotton box lets the fiber breathe, which is the same reason a [breathable garment bag](/blog/breathable-garment-bags-for-wedding-dress-storage/) beats a plastic one for a gown.
- **Never hang cashmere.** A wire hanger leaves shoulder bumps and the knit stretches under its own weight. Fold it, and leave the [heavy-coat hangers](/blog/best-space-saving-hangers-for-heavy-coats/) for coats, not knits.

## Pilling is not a wash problem

I spent a year blaming my detergent for pills on the sleeves. The pills were friction. A tote bag strap, a seatbelt, a tight hanger, and the same sweater worn three days running all rub the surface and pull loose fibers into little balls.

The fix is a fabric comb or a sweater stone, used gently in one direction, plus a rest day between wears so the fibers can recover. Shaving pills with a razor cuts the knit and makes the next round worse. If the pills keep coming back in the same spot, look at what is rubbing there, not at the wash.

## What I got wrong for three years

I used warm water because it felt cleaner. I used regular detergent because it was in the cupboard. I hung the sweater to dry because the rack was full. Each of those is a small choice that, repeated, turns a soft sweater into a felted one.

Now the routine is boring: cold soak, same-temperature rinse, towel press, flat dry, fold into a breathable box with cedar. The sweater has kept its shape for four seasons, and the only pills are where the tote bag rubs. Cashmere is not fragile. It is just hair, and hair has rules. Learn the two that matter, temperature and agitation, and the rest is [keeping the closet in order](/blog/whole-home-organization-system/) like everything else.
